# Communication fails on names, numbers and intent

Across a dozen unrelated spoken and signed languages, someone flags a problem with what was just said about **once every 1.4 minutes**, and a universal "huh? / who? / you mean X?" repair system fixes nearly all of it within seconds. So how *often* communication fails matters less than you might expect. The damage comes from failures that nobody notices, or that can't be repaired because there is no live back-and-forth. Those failures cluster in a short, consistent list. The first is **isolated tokens with no surrounding context**: numbers, names and call signs. The second is **who is meant**: in European air–ground radio incidents, similar call signs were the single biggest contributing factor (**33% of 535 occurrences**). The third is **what the speaker is doing with the utterance**: Tenerife, the deadliest air accident, turned on a statement of action heard as a report of position. The fourth is **stance**: readers pull "very likely" toward 50–50 in 24 countries, and sarcasm sent by email is detected barely above chance. Several famous suspects have little evidence of real-world harm: ambiguity in general, languages without tense, and negation as such. The real hazards are narrower: answers to negative questions ("yes, I'm not"?), relative time phrases like "move the meeting forward", and long, dense messages. **Fixes with measured evidence are few.** They are closed-loop confirmation and fixed handoff checklists (I-PASS: 23% fewer medical errors, in a before/after study), putting a number next to a probability word (roughly doubles agreement with the intended meaning), vocabularies chosen by confusion testing (the ICAO spelling alphabet), shorter messages, and controlled vocabulary for difficult texts. Popular design ideas have no outcome data (speech-act markers, urgency tiers) or mixed and even negative results (grammatical evidentiality). For a designed language whose goal is faithful transmission of thought, the evidence supports fixing that short list rather than redesigning everything. One large caveat applies: nearly every measured fix was tested as a professional protocol for adults, never as the grammar of a native language, and native speakers wear down whatever redundancy they are given.

*How to read the evidence tags.* Unmarked findings were read by the researchers in the primary paper or official report. **[secondary]** means the finding is known only from an abstract, search summary, review, press article, Wikipedia or a citing paper, not checked against the full primary text. **[unverified]** means the researchers could not confirm it in any source they read. Some numbers that circulate widely are left out on purpose, because the notes flagged them as likely fabricated or unattributable. These include a breakdown of repair causes from a summarised PDF that contradicted the published article, a widely repeated share of hospital sentinel events with inconsistent attribution, unverified controller hearback-miss rates, and uncited maritime accident shares.

## Conversation breaks every 1.4 minutes, and repair usually catches it

The best baseline comes from **other-initiated repair** (OIR): the moments when a listener signals trouble with the previous turn ("huh?", "who?", "you mean Tuesday?"). Dingemanse and colleagues recorded informal conversation among family and friends in **12 languages from 8 families on 5 continents**, including one sign language, Argentine Sign Language (LSA). In **48.5 hours they found 2,053 repair initiations, about one every 1.4 minutes**, with no significant difference in rate between languages ([Dingemanse et al. 2015, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0136100); [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4573759/)). English figures are similar. Kendrick found **227 core repairs in 411 minutes**, about one every 1.8 minutes ([Kendrick 2015](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf)). In the British National Corpus, clarification requests are **just under 4% of sentences in everyday talk**, and over 96% target something said no more than four sentences earlier ([Purver, Ginzburg & Healey 2001](https://aclanthology.org/W01-1616.pdf)). Even the word for "huh?" converges. Across 10 unrelated languages it is a single syllable with a low, central-front vowel and questioning intonation, and similar words turned up in 31 languages surveyed ([Dingemanse, Torreira & Enfield 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0078273)).

Three repair formats recur in every language studied, and together they make up **92% of cases**. An *open request* ("huh?") says "I lost the whole thing". A *restricted request* ("who?") says "one piece didn't arrive". A *restricted offer* ("she had a boy?") says "here's my guess, confirm it". People choose the most specific format they can. That shifts effort onto the listener, but the speaker then only has to fix one piece, and a two-turn repair sequence ends up about as long as the original trouble turn ([Dingemanse et al. 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0136100)). Restricted formats outnumber open ones by roughly 3 to 1 (English: **18.5% open**, with "candidate understanding" the most common single format at **28.2%**) ([Kendrick 2015](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf)). This tells us something important: most trouble is local to one element, usually a referring expression, and the listener knows which one.

Nobody has published a clean cross-linguistic count of *what kind* of trouble triggers repair: mishearing, an unknown word, unclear reference, or a surprising claim. That gap is deliberate. The same "huh?" can serve a hearing problem or a polite disagreement, and Svennevig found that acceptability and understanding problems are often first presented as hearing problems, as the easiest fix to try ([Dingemanse, Blythe & Dirksmeyer 2014](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_3048557_7/component/file_3149644/content)). The authors replace the old "speaking, hearing, understanding" triad with Clark's **action ladder**: attending to the signal, identifying the words, working out meaning and reference, and recognising the intended action. Trouble can occur at any rung. The best proxies are indirect. In the 12-language study, the probability that a repair is open (the format typical of signal loss) is **39% at baseline but 78% under noise, 76% under overlapping talk and 76% when the listener is busy with something else**, approaching 100% when all three are present ([PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4573759/)). In Finnish conversation the share of open repairs doubles from **17% to 34%** when one partner has severe hearing loss ([Pajo & Laakso 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2023.1173179/full)). English clarification requests divide roughly into **~50% querying the whole message or intent, ~35% "did you say X?" (word identification), and ~15% "what/who do you mean by X?" (meaning or reference)** ([Purver et al. 2001](https://aclanthology.org/W01-1616.pdf)). Across the sampled languages, *person, thing and place* dominate the restricted repairs ([Dingemanse et al. 2014](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_3048557_7/component/file_3149644/content)).

Sign languages break down the same way. Of 213 repairs in LSA conversation, **59% were explicit restricted requests and 29% open**. The visual channel adds a silent "freeze-look", where the listener holds still and keeps their gaze on the signer, in 10% of cases ([Manrique & Enfield 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01326/full)). That corpus has a higher raw rate (213 cases in 110 minutes), but the notes caution that this could reflect corpus selection or multi-party settings rather than modality. When deaf signers of *different* sign languages meet, open repair nearly vanishes: in one study **3 of 51** repair sequences were open, and almost all trouble targeted specific signs whose meaning was not shared ([Byun et al. 2018](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/tops.12303)) [secondary]. When the signal arrives clearly but the vocabulary isn't shared, the lexicon becomes the main source of failure.

These rates count only trouble someone *noticed*, so they are a lower bound. The real danger is misunderstanding that neither side detects. In Keysar and Henly's experiment, speakers of ambiguous sentences believed they had been understood **77% of the time against an actual 67% for syntactic ambiguity, and 69% against 57% for lexical ambiguity** ([Keysar & Henly 2002](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/1467-9280.00439)) [secondary]. That gap of about 10–12 points of false confidence is invisible to repair, because neither party thinks anything went wrong. In survey interviews, letting interviewers explain concepts reduced answer errors most for "complicated mappings", where respondents didn't know they needed help ([Conrad, FCSM 1999](https://nces.ed.gov/FCSM/pdf/V_B_Conrad2_FCSM1999.pdf)) [secondary]. The lesson for design is that live conversation already handles *detectable* trouble cheaply. Language design adds the most where repair is missing: in writing, one-shot radio calls and recorded instructions, and wherever the speaker doesn't realise they were unclear.

## Mishearing hits consonants, isolated tokens and fine handshapes first

On the physical channel, the evidence is old, strong and consistent. In Miller and Nicely's 1955 noise study of 16 English consonants, **voicing** (whether the vocal cords buzz, as in b vs p) and **nasality** (air through the nose, as in m vs b) survived at a signal-to-noise ratio of −12 dB. That means the noise was much louder than the speech; 0 dB means equal loudness. **Place of articulation** (where in the mouth the sound is made, as in b vs d vs g) was hard to hear below +6 dB: "a difference of some 18 db in efficiency". At 0 dB, listeners still received about **95% of voicing information but only 37% of place information**. At −12 dB, place fell to about 4%. Place cues sit above about 1.9 kHz, so muffled speech, distance and walls destroy them first ([Miller & Nicely 1955](https://jontallen.ece.illinois.edu/uploads/537.F18/Papers/MillerNicely55.pdf)). Vowels are far sturdier than consonants. In babble noise, going from 0 to 8 dB improved vowel identification by only 2 points but consonant identification by 24, then another 12 from 8 to 16 dB. Non-native (Dutch) listeners scored about 80% of native accuracy at every noise level. They carried their first language's gaps into the channel, transmitting English voicing worst word-finally because Dutch devoices final consonants ([Cutler et al. 2004](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_60592/component/file_60593/content)).

Mishearing in natural conversation has the same profile. Bond's corpus of almost 1,000 everyday "slips of the ear" finds consonants far more often affected than vowels, stressed syllables swapping with other stressed syllables, and listeners hearing the wrong version "as clearly and distinctly as any correctly perceived stretch of speech" ([Bond, Wiley Handbook chapter](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/9781119184096.ch10)) [secondary]. Frequent words and words with many similar-sounding neighbours are the ones affected ([Vitevitch 2002](https://doi.org/10.1177/00238309020450040501)) [secondary]. Language history shows a matching long-run filter: across mergers in eight languages, sound contrasts that distinguish more word pairs were significantly less likely to be lost ([Wedel, Kaplan & Jackson 2013, reviewed by Trott](https://seantrott.github.io/sound-change/)) [secondary]. Contrasts that do little work erode.

Redundancy is the natural defence, but it is spread unevenly. Shannon estimated printed English at roughly **50% redundant over 8-letter windows and about 75% with long-range context** ([Shannon 1951](https://www.princeton.edu/~wbialek/rome/refs/shannon_51.pdf)). Across **17 languages from 9 families, speech carries a near-constant ~39 bits per second** (SD 5.1). Languages with information-dense syllables, like Vietnamese, are spoken more slowly, and languages with light syllables, like Spanish and Japanese, faster; even so, the trade-off is imperfect, and Japanese came out at about three-quarters of Vietnamese's information rate in the earlier 8-language study ([Coupé et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6984970/); [Pellegrino et al. 2011](https://gwern.net/doc/cs/algorithm/information/2011-pellegrino.pdf)). The convergence suggests a shared human bottleneck that a designed language won't beat by packing more into each syllable. The redundancy vanishes exactly where errors are most expensive. As Miller and Nicely put it, for "isolated words, numerals, station call letters … a miss is as good as a mile; there is no redundancy in the message to enable the listener to correct an error" ([Miller & Nicely 1955](https://jontallen.ece.illinois.edu/uploads/537.F18/Papers/MillerNicely55.pdf)).

Radio procedure is the engineered patch for that gap, and its history is the best-documented vocabulary design in existence. The ICAO/NATO spelling alphabet ("Alfa, Bravo, Charlie…") was finalised in 1956 after testing in noise with speakers from several NATO nations. Words were scored on two things: an *articulation score* (how often the word is heard correctly) and a *confusability score* (how often it is heard when a different word was said). FOOTBALL beat FOXTROT on the first measure but lost because listeners heard it when HOTEL and VICTOR were spoken. Both foreign and American listeners did significantly better with the final list from −5 to −16 dB (p < 0.001). One three-word substitution created more confusions than it removed "in unexpected parts of the matrix", so every change had to be retested against the whole set. The four worst words in the older list were all one-syllable words (QUEEN, KING, GEORGE, JIG). Longer words survive because losing a syllable to a noise burst still leaves the word identifiable. The designers also kept final vowels, because French and Spanish speakers weaken final consonants ([ICAO Word-Spelling Alphabet rationale, 1959](https://www.governmentattic.org/4docs/ICAO-WordSpellingAlphabet_1959.pdf)). A key method insight from the testing: noise "do[es] not create confusion, but do[es] intensify those confusions already inherent between the words". A confusion matrix measured at moderate noise therefore predicts failures before deployment.

Sign languages show the visual version of the same pattern: coarse contrasts survive and fine ones fail. **Location** (where on the body the sign is made) is identified first in gating studies, in which viewers see progressively longer fragments of a sign ([Emmorey & Corina 1990](https://doi.org/10.2466/pms.1990.71.3f.1227)) [secondary], and it is the easiest contrast for learners ([Bochner et al. 2011](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2011.00671.x)) [secondary]. **Handshape** is the parameter most prone to production slips ([Newkirk et al. 1980, via citing paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/369112456_Development_of_sign_phonology_in_Kata_Kolok)) [secondary]. **Movement** is the hardest contrast for learners and the parameter BSL signers most often misperceive ([Orfanidou et al. 2009](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/mc.37.3.302)) [secondary]. Signers look mostly at the face, so handshapes made on the torso fall in low-acuity peripheral vision ([PMC7887614](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7887614/)). ASL appears to compensate by placing rare handshapes near the face ([Caselli, Emmorey & Cohen-Goldberg 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0010027722000282)) [secondary]. Deaf signers and hearing non-signers make the same handshape confusions, so handshape confusability looks like a property of human vision, not of language experience ([Lane, Boyes-Braem & Bellugi 1976](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Preliminaries-to-a-distinctive-feature-analysis-of-Lane-Boyes-Braem/2412293d346d5d59398440f15c718ca19badcc28)) [secondary]. That matters for design: one handshape confusion matrix could serve every user population.

Typology points the same way. The modal vowel system is five vowels ([WALS ch. 2](https://wals.info/chapter/2)) [secondary], and **/i/, /u/, /a/, /o/ and /e/ each occur in over 80% of 913 languages** in the PHOIBLE sample ([Phonetics Speech Sci. 16(3)](https://www.eksss.org/archive/view_article?pid=pss-16-3-1)). Theories based on maximal acoustic spacing (dispersion) predict three- to six-vowel systems well but over-predict central vowels in larger ones ([Stanford handout on Liljencrants & Lindblom](https://web.stanford.edu/class/linguist205/index_files/Handout%208%20-%20QT%20&%20Adaptive%20Dispersion.pdf)) [secondary]. The notes found no direct test showing that common inventories are less confusable in noise than rare ones. That link is inferred from theory, not measured.

## Names and numbers carry most of the costly errors

When the stakes are high and the record is good, as in aviation, the failures concentrate on two things: **who is being addressed or talked about, and numbers**. On routine radio tapes, readback errors are rare. Across Cardosi's field studies they ran "consistently less than 1%" of clearances, and "the vast majority" were caught ([Schroeder 2006, FAA AM-06/21](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/200621.pdf)). A later study of 11,159 readbacks at five busy US approach-control facilities found **93.8% correct**, and "aircraft headings and radio frequency changes still are the most frequently occurring readback errors". The number errors had recognisable shapes. Numbers got truncated (heading 140 read back as "ONE FOUR"), and formats leaked between number types ("THREE POINT FIVE" for 3,500 ft, borrowing the "point" used for frequencies) ([Prinzo et al. 2006, FAA AM-06/25](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/200625.pdf)). The "two/to" homophone produced a documented crash. "Descend two four zero zero" was heard as "descend to 400", and "the aircraft crashed into high ground" ([ICAO Doc 9835](https://www.skybrary.aero/bookshelf/books/2497.pdf)). In one ASRS analysis, confusion between 10,000 and 11,000 ft appeared in **38% of 191 altitude deviations** ([Flight Safety Foundation](https://flightsafety.org/asw-article/failure-to-communicate/)) [secondary]. Digits dominate radio traffic: in a 1950 count of 121,296 words from more than 100 towers, ONE appeared 4,955 times against 1,045 for ROGER. Most confusions are digit-with-digit or letter-with-letter ([ICAO 1959](https://www.governmentattic.org/4docs/ICAO-WordSpellingAlphabet_1959.pdf)). Medicine has a written equivalent. Of 643,151 MEDMARX medication errors, **4.7% involved abbreviations**, led by "QD" and "U" for units, which gets misread as 0 or 4 ([AMN Healthcare summary of MEDMARX data](https://www.amnhealthcare.com/amn-insights/news/abbreviations-may-save-minutes-prohibiting-abbreviations-may-save-lives/)) [secondary].

Number *words* matter beyond noise too. Chinese children show more counting competence than US children by age five, and the difference has been traced to Chinese naming 11–20 regularly as "ten-one, ten-two" where English has irregular teens ([Miller et al. 1995, cited in Ng & Rao 2010](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/0034654310364764)) [secondary]. Claims about the costs of French vigesimal numbers (counting in twenties, as in *quatre-vingt-dix*, "four-twenty-ten" for 90) and German "one-and-twenty" digit order are [unverified] in these notes.

Addressing and reference fail as often, and are harder to catch. In EUROCONTROL's study of 535 European air–ground communication occurrences, **a similar call sign was the top contributing factor (175 occurrences, 33%)**. It was also a factor in 37% of the readback/hearback errors, and outcomes included "wrong aircraft accepted clearance" (6%) and "instruction issued to wrong aircraft" (4%). Within that set, **11 of 15 incorrect readbacks went undetected by the controller** ([EUROCONTROL Air-Ground Communication Safety Study](https://skybrary.aero/sites/default/files/bookshelf/162.pdf)). That is a warning about readback: the listener's echo only helps if the sender actually checks it. In trauma bays, only **32.6% of 387 verbal orders were directed at a named person** ([El-Shafy et al. 2018](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1931720417300387)). Everyday conversation shows the same pattern. Person, thing and place dominate restricted repair, and several languages have dedicated "which one?" machinery. Bequia Creole has three distinct "who?" formats: one for not hearing, one for a name that fits more than one person, and one for not recognising the person meant. Murrinh-Patha's "what?" carries a noun-class marker, so the question itself narrows down the kind of thing that went missing ([Dingemanse et al. 2014](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_3048557_7/component/file_3149644/content)). Reference failures also surface *late*. English "who's X?" repairs come straight after the problem turn only 22% of the time, against 90.5% for open "huh?" repairs ([Kendrick 2015](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf)). By then the conversation has built on the wrong referent.

Natural languages have evolved dedicated reference-tracking grammar. **Switch-reference** marks on the verb whether the next clause has the same subject or a different one ([Camacho, Wiley Companion to Syntax](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/9781118358733.wbsyncom014)). **Obviation**, found for example in Algonquian languages, ranks third persons into "the one in focus" (proximate) and "the others" (obviative). Gender on pronouns helps too: eye-tracking shows English listeners use pronoun gender within a fraction of a second, and fall back on "first-mentioned person" when gender doesn't decide ([Arnold et al., as cited in a 2001 follow-up](https://cpb-us-w2.wpmucdn.com/web.sas.upenn.edu/dist/4/81/files/2019/07/Arnold_Novick_Brown-Schmidt_Eisenband_Trueswell_2001-tgem5q-pdf.pdf)) [secondary]. The notes found **no comparative evidence that any of these systems reduces real-world reference errors**. Switch-reference markers also drift into marking changes of time and place, which suggests natural systems don't keep them strictly referential ([Mbyá Guaraní study, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12919634/)). This is the area where the problem is best measured and the grammatical fix least measured.

A third factor amplifies both: **message overload**. Readback error rates stay at roughly 2–6% for clearances below complexity 10, rise to 11–19% at complexity 11–13, and approach **38% above 16** ([Prinzo et al. 2006](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/200625.pdf)). Transmissions with five or more elements were 4% of transmissions but 26% of en-route errors ([Schroeder 2006](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/200621.pdf)). The curve is flat and then steep, consistent with working-memory limits. Grammar shows matching capacity limits. In corpora, multiple **center-embedding** (clauses nested inside clauses, "the rat the cat the dog chased bit died") is "practically absent" in speech and never deeper than three levels in writing ([Karlsson 2007](https://researchportal.helsinki.fi/en/publications/constraints-on-multiple-center-embedding-of-clauses)) [secondary]. In 37 languages, words that depend on each other sit closer together than chance would predict ([Futrell, Mahowald & Gibson 2015](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2015PNAS..11210336F/abstract)) [secondary]; a later reappraisal questions how universal this is ([PMC9692064](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9692064/)). A perfectly unambiguous message still fails if it asks the listener to hold too much at once.

## Ambiguity is everywhere but dangerous in only a few places

Ambiguity has a worse reputation than its record supports. Piantadosi, Tily and Gibson argue that "all efficient communication systems will be ambiguous, assuming that context is informative about meaning": reusing short, easy words for several meanings is efficient as long as context picks the right one ([Piantadosi et al. 2012, Cognition](https://philpapers.org/rec/PIATCF)). The specific finding that shorter and more frequent words carry more meanings is [unverified] in full text. Speakers are poor at noticing their own linguistic ambiguity. In a picture-description task they were less likely to disambiguate "bat" (the animal vs the baseball bat) than two baseball bats of different sizes ([Ferreira, Slevc & Rogers 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15996561/)) [secondary]. When ambiguity was possible, they added a disambiguating word only about 60% of the time ([Wasow 2015](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/319593818_Ambiguity_Avoidance_is_Overrated)) [secondary]. Most of this is harmless, because only one sense fits the context. The dangerous cases are senses that **fit the same context and lead to different actions**. At Tenerife in 1977, which killed 583 people, the KLM crew's "We are now at takeoff" meant "we are taking off" and was heard as "we are at the takeoff point, waiting" ([Cushing, *Fatal Words*](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/F/bo3633901.html); [ICAO Doc 9835](https://www.skybrary.aero/bookshelf/books/2497.pdf)). Function words next to numbers ("to two seven zero zero") belong in the same category. Afterwards ICAO reserved "takeoff" for the actual clearance and introduced "departure" for everything else ([Wikipedia, Tenerife disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenerife_airport_disaster)) [secondary].

Structural ambiguity is robust in the lab. Garden-path sentences make readers backtrack, and "I saw the man with the telescope" has two readings. But the notes found no incident data showing that it causes real-world errors at any measurable rate. Prosody (the melody and timing of speech) and context usually prevent it in speech, so the residual risk sits in text: headlines, instructions, contracts. Ambiguous **scope** (does "every horse didn't jump" mean none did, or not all did?) is resolved by a strong preference for the reading that follows word order ([Szendrői, on Kurtzman & MacDonald 1993](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/~uczaksz/papers/Quantifier_Scope.pdf)) [secondary]. Children frequently miss the inverse reading, though the exact percentages are [unverified] ([Lidz & Musolino 2002](https://semantics.uchicago.edu/kennedy/classes/s09/experimentalsemantics/lidz-musolino02.pdf)).

**Negation** takes measurably longer to verify than an affirmative statement in isolation ([Tian & Breheny](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1571834/1/negation%20tian%20&%20breheny%20pre-pub%20draft.pdf)). The extra cost "softens or even eliminates" when context makes the negation natural ("with proper equipment, scuba diving is not dangerous") ([Nieuwland & Kuperberg 2008](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/23718838_When_the_Truth_Is_Not_Too_Hard_to_Handle_An_Event-Related_Potential_Study_on_the_Pragmatics_of_Negation)). The genuine negation hazard is narrower: **answering a negative question**. Languages split two ways. In *polarity-based* systems like English and Swedish, "no" to "Aren't you tired?" means "I'm not tired". In *truth-based* systems like Japanese, "yes" means "yes, what you said is right: I'm not tired" ([Holmberg 2015](https://recos-dtal.mmll.cam.ac.uk/system/files/documents/Holmberg2015Thesyntaxofyesandno.pdf)). Even within English, a large online experiment found that bare "yes" to a question with *not* is either read as confirming the negative or judged indeterminate ([Kramer & Rawlins, summarised in ACL Y17-1001](https://aclanthology.org/Y17-1001.pdf)) [secondary]. The same word can mean opposite things between languages and nothing reliable within one. Maritime phraseology already requires "fully worded answers to yes/no questions" ([IMO SMCP](https://aflopez.webs.ull.es/nautica/IMO_Standard_Marine_Communication_Phrases.pdf)). The notes found no real-world rate for this failure.

**Time** follows the same pattern of reputation versus evidence. Mandarin and Cantonese have no tense morphology, and the notes found no evidence that this causes misunderstanding. Speakers get time from adverbs like "yesterday", aspect markers (*le* for completed action), and words like "before" and "after", and they detect temporal mismatches in brain-response studies ([Brain Sciences 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/brainsci12040474)). Even intermediate learners interpret Mandarin time reference "highly accurate[ly]" ([Modern Language Journal 2015, ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1070158)) [secondary]. The documented failures are lexical and metaphorical. Told "Next Wednesday's meeting has been moved forward two days", English speakers split roughly in half between Monday and Friday, with one comparison group at **54% Monday vs 46% Friday** ([Cognitive Science 2018 meta-analysis](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/cogs.12559)) [secondary]. The ambiguity exists in Swiss German but not in Standard German ([Stocker & Hartmann 2019](https://econtent.hogrefe.com/doi/10.1024/1421-0185/a000220)) [secondary]. That points to a lexical convention, which design can fix, rather than a cognitive limit. The reputation of "next Friday" as similarly ambiguous is [unverified]; no primary study was found.

Behind all of this sits a principle from Boas and Jakobson: "Languages differ essentially in what they must convey and not in what they may convey" ([Jakobson 1959](https://culturalstudiesnow.blogspot.com/2011/10/roman-jakobson-on-linguistic-aspects-of.html)). An obligatory category, a dimension the grammar forces every sentence to mark, like Russian verbal aspect or a worker's gender, guarantees the dimension is present. It also forces speakers to commit to values they may not know. For faithful transmission, that **forced false precision is itself a fidelity loss**. The notes found no study showing that *lacking* an obligatory category causes misunderstanding. Tense and gender suggest context and optional devices cover the gap.

## Stance and intent are the least reliably transmitted layer

If one layer of meaning fails systematically, even between native speakers in quiet rooms, it is **stance**: how sure the speaker is, how they know, and what they are doing with the utterance. Probability words are the clearest case. In 1951 a US intelligence estimate called a Soviet attack on Yugoslavia "a serious possibility". Its author, Sherman Kent, meant about 65:35 in favour. When he polled the board that had approved the wording, "the low man was thinking of about 20 to 80, the high of 80 to 20" ([Kent 1964, CIA](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Words-of-Estimative-Probability.pdf)). In a survey of about 1,700 people, the middle 90% of readings of "real possibility" spanned **20–80%** ([Mauboussin & Mauboussin 2018, HBR](https://hbr.org/2018/07/if-you-say-something-is-likely-how-likely-do-people-think-it-is)) [secondary]. There are two distinct failures. **Dispersion** means listeners disagree with each other. **Regression** means everyone pulls terms toward 50%. Across **25 samples in 24 countries and 17 languages (~11,000 responses)**, readers interpreted the IPCC's calibrated terms as less extreme than intended, a pattern "remarkably stable across all samples and languages" ([Budescu et al. 2014, Nature Climate Change](https://www.nature.com/articles/nclimate2194)). The response count comes from a [secondary] summary. Press coverage reported a mean reading of about 62% for "very likely", which the IPCC defines as >90% ([Mother Jones 2014](https://www.motherjones.com/environment/2014/04/ipcc-global-warming-doubt-psychology/)) [secondary; not verified in the paper]. Translation adds error on top: Chinese readings of IPCC terms were more regressive and less differentiated than British ones ([Harris et al. 2013](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10584-013-0975-1)) [secondary]. Social context distorts readings further. "Possible", said about a severe medical outcome, gets read as polite softening of a higher probability ([Bonnefon & Villejoubert 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01776.x)).

This is also the area with the cleanest measured fix. Showing the number next to the word (the "dual format") moved interpretations toward the intended values and made them more similar *across languages* ([Budescu et al. 2014](https://www.nature.com/articles/nclimate2194)). In a test of the US intelligence standard with 924 readers, overlap between readers' intervals and the intended ones rose from **32% with no guidance to 66% with numbers in brackets**. Tooltips and click-to-open tables barely helped (40%, 39%), because only about half of readers ever opened them ([Wintle et al. 2019, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0213522)). The fix works when the number travels with the word. Coarseness also costs accuracy. In a large forecasting tournament, rounding forecasts into fewer than about 6 bins lost accuracy, and the best forecasters needed about 11 ([Friedman et al. 2018](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-organization/article/abs/behavioral-consequences-of-probabilistic-precision-experimental-evidence-from-national-security-professionals/9C7D7A3E5F960BA0DF3D8E70BC5DB0EF)) [secondary]. A five-word confidence scale would discard information good reasoners actually hold.

**Evidentiality** is grammar that marks *how* the speaker knows something: saw it, inferred it, or was told. It is the obvious candidate for a fidelity-first language, and the evidence for it is disappointing. It is common: of 418 languages in WALS, **237 (57%) have some grammatical evidential**, clustered by region (western Americas, Caucasus, Himalayas) rather than by family ([WALS ch. 77](https://wals.info/chapter/77)). Aikhenvald's stricter count, which includes only obligatory systems, is about a quarter ([Wikipedia: Evidentiality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evidentiality)) [secondary]. Children master it late: Turkish children don't command the semantics of the direct and inferential markers until about age 6–7 ([Öztürk & Papafragou 2016](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/15475441.2015.1024834)) [secondary]. The cognitive payoff is mixed. Korean 3–4-year-olds showed no advantage in remembering where information came from ([Papafragou et al. 2007, as cited in Kandemirci et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10953015/)) [secondary]. Turkish children's evidential use correlated with source monitoring at r = .34 ([Kandemirci et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10953015/)), but that is a correlation across samples from different countries. And adult Turkish speakers remembered *second-hand* information **worse** than English speakers ([Tosun, Vaid & Geraci 2013](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/260794783_Does_obligatory_linguistic_marking_of_source_of_evidence_affect_source_memory_A_TurkishEnglish_investigation)) [secondary]. In real use, evidentials get absorbed into social work. In Nanti they are used "to subtly disclaim responsibility for mishaps". Garrwa speakers show "a marked disinclination to index source". Social explanations proposed for evidentials include avoiding confrontation and attributing blame ([Nuckolls & Michael](http://www.cabeceras.org/ldm_publications/l_michael_Pragmatics&Society_intro.pdf)). The lesson is that **source and confidence are separate dimensions**, and that any stance marker will be repurposed socially, just as hedges get read as tact. No study was found on whether evidentials help *listeners* weigh hearsay appropriately.

Implied meaning is unreliable even between native speakers. Asked whether "Some giraffes have long necks" is acceptable, about 41% of adults accepted it, meaning roughly 4 in 10 did not draw the "some, not all" implicature (an implied meaning beyond the literal words) ([Noveck 2001, summarised](https://www.sfu.ca/~hedberg/earlysteps)) [secondary]. Senders predicted that receivers would identify sarcastic vs serious messages **78%** of the time. Receivers managed **73% from voice recordings and 56% from email**, where chance is 50% ([Kruger et al. 2005](https://web-docs.stern.nyu.edu/pa/kruger_email_ego.pdf)) [secondary]. Emotion carried by voice alone crosses cultures better than chance but poorly. German actors' portrayals were recognised **66%** of the time across nine countries, from 74% in Germany down to 52% in Indonesia, with joy recognised only 42% of the time ([Scherer, Banse & Wallbott 2001](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Emotion-Inferences-from-Vocal-Expression-Correlate-Scherer-Banse/818cfcf6afabe5bd5f44c49686d0f20ba0b2c3d2)) [secondary]. Across cultures, Thomas's classic analysis separates *pragmalinguistic* failure (misjudging the force of a form, such as a literally translated request) from *sociopragmatic* failure (different beliefs about rights and distance) ([Thomas 1983](https://academic.oup.com/applij/article-abstract/4/2/91/167524)). The popular "high-context vs low-context culture" country rankings rest on "less-than-adequate evidence" ([Kittler, Rygl & Mackinnon 2011](https://dspace.stir.ac.uk/handle/1893/3174?mode=full)).

**Speech acts**, what an utterance *does* (asks, tells, orders, warns, promises), are often marked only by melody. In **173 of 955 languages (18%)**, intonation is the only thing that tells a yes/no question from a statement. The commonest strategy, in **585 languages (61%)**, is a question particle, a short word that survives writing and noise ([WALS ch. 116](https://wals.info/chapter/116)). In requests, the preferred strategy across most languages studied is the conventionally indirect "Could you…?", which is grammatically a question and functionally an order ([CCSARP, Blum-Kulka, House & Kasper 1989](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/31338837_Requests_and_Apologies_A_Cross-Cultural_Study_of_Speech_Act_Realization_Patterns_CCSARP1)) [secondary]. Speech-act failures are rare but catastrophic. Tenerife was one. Another was Avianca 52 in 1990, where the crew said "priority" and "running out of fuel" but never "emergency", and the NTSB cited the lack of "standardized understandable terminology" for fuel states ([Wikipedia, quoting NTSB AAR-91/04](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avianca_Flight_052)) [secondary]. The engineered answer is explicit markers. Maritime SMCP defines eight optional ones spoken before a message: INSTRUCTION, ADVICE, WARNING, INFORMATION, QUESTION, ANSWER, REQUEST and INTENTION. They encode *authority* as well as mood: after ADVICE, "the decision whether to follow … still stays with the recipient" ([IMO SMCP](https://aflopez.webs.ull.es/nautica/IMO_Standard_Marine_Communication_Phrases.pdf)). Military radio separates ROGER ("received") from WILCO ("understood and will comply") ([Wikipedia, Procedure word](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Procedure_word)) [secondary]. Sign languages offer a natural precedent: across **35 sign languages**, the face marks question type, raised brows for yes/no questions and lowered brows for wh-questions, at the same time as the hands carry the content, and more consistently than question words do ([Zeshan 2004](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_58963_3/component/file_58964/Zeshan_2004_interrogative.pdf)) [secondary]. **None of these markers has outcome data.** The notes found no trial, before/after study or compliance audit showing that they reduce errors.

## Only five kinds of fix have measured evidence

Put side by side, the eight failure sources look quite different in how often they occur and what they cost. The table below is a synthesis. The notes warn that incident taxonomies across domains are incompatible, so the rankings are judgments, not a pooled statistic.

| Failure source | How frequent | How costly when it fails | Strength of the evidence on the problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mishearing / signal loss | The most common flagged trouble in everyday talk; dominates under noise, overlap and distraction | Cheap when repair is available; expensive for isolated numbers, names, call signs | Strong (Miller & Nicely, Cutler, 12-language repair data) |
| Reference and addressing | Most-named *understanding* trouble; the top factor in European radio incidents (33%) | High: detected late; wrong aircraft takes the clearance | Strong for the problem; nothing measured on grammatical fixes |
| Numbers | Most frequent readback errors (headings, frequencies) | High: altitude busts, crashes, dosing errors | Strong in aviation; medicine [secondary] |
| Speech acts / urgency | Rare as a recorded failure | Catastrophic (Tenerife, Avianca) | Case studies; the scale of question/statement marking is from WALS |
| Stance / confidence | Pervasive and mostly undetected | Moderate to high (intelligence, climate, medicine) | Strong and cross-linguistic (24 countries) |
| Implicature, irony, tone | Pervasive; undetected | Varies; worst in text | Lab studies, mostly [secondary] |
| Lexical / structural ambiguity | Everywhere, almost always resolved | Low, except same-context, action-relevant cases | Lab processing evidence; incidents are anecdotes |
| Negation | Processing cost disappears in context | Real for answers to negative questions | Answer indeterminacy measured [secondary]; no real-world rate |
| Time reference | Tenseless languages show no measured problem | Real for relative and metaphorical phrases | One meta-analysis [secondary] |

The fixes divide more sharply still. **Closed-loop confirmation and fixed information schemas** have the strongest outcome evidence. I-PASS is a handoff structure: illness severity, patient summary, action list, situation awareness, and synthesis by the receiver, who restates what they heard. Across 9 hospitals and **10,740 admissions, medical errors fell 23% (24.5 to 18.8 per 100 admissions) and preventable adverse events fell 30%**, with no increase in handoff time ([Starmer et al. 2014, NEJM](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMsa1405556)) [secondary: abstract via search summary]. The design was before/after, not randomised, and the components came as a bundle. Closed-loop verbal orders in trauma bays were completed **3.6× sooner**, yet only **26.1%** of orders were actually closed-loop ([El-Shafy et al. 2018](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1931720417300387)). A systematic review rated the evidence for SBAR, a simpler handoff structure, as "moderate" ([Müller et al. 2018](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/327181945_Impact_of_the_communication_and_patient_hand-off_tool_SBAR_on_patient_safety_a_systematic_review)). The stakes justify the effort. Communication failures contributed to **30% of 23,658 US malpractice cases, linked to 1,744 deaths** ([CRICO via Harvard RMF](https://www.rmf.harvard.edu/News-and-Blog/In-the-News-Home/In-the-News/2016/February/Communication-failures-linked-to-1744-deaths-in-five-years)). Medicine's dominant failure is omission at a transfer of responsibility, not mishearing.

The other measured fixes are narrower. Numbers beside probability words roughly double agreement, as described above. Confusion-tested vocabularies beat their predecessors in noise at p < 0.001, though only for the spelling alphabet ([ICAO 1959](https://www.governmentattic.org/4docs/ICAO-WordSpellingAlphabet_1959.pdf)). The case for limiting message complexity is strongly correlational, with no intervention trial ([Prinzo et al. 2006](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/200625.pdf)). Controlled vocabulary in Simplified Technical English means about 900 words, each with one meaning and one part of speech. In a study of 175 aircraft technicians it cut comprehension errors on *difficult* workcards and for non-native readers, with no effect on easy ones ([Chervak, Drury & Ouellette 1996](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/154193129604000502)) [secondary]. Everything else is motivated by accidents but not measured. That includes reserved words ("departure" vs "takeoff"), speech-act markers, the MAYDAY/PAN-PAN urgency tiers, prowords like WILCO, and the medical "Do Not Use" abbreviation list. The notes found no controlled before/after study isolating any of them.

Two patterns cut across every domain. First, **compliance erodes**. In one study 70% of en-route speech acts with a prescribed phraseology didn't follow it ([Mell 1992, via ICAO Doc 9835](https://www.skybrary.aero/bookshelf/books/2497.pdf)). In an IATA survey, 44% of pilots said they meet non-standard phraseology at least once per flight ([FSF](https://flightsafety.org/asw-article/failure-to-communicate/)) [secondary]. Speakers group and drop digits to save airtime, which "may create the problems that pilots and controllers are trying to prevent" ([Prinzo 1996, FAA AM-96/26](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/AM96-26.pdf)). Second, **closed codes run out**. In one analysed gear-malfunction dialogue, "fully 60 per cent of the dialogue required plain language", because "no set of standardized phraseologies can fully describe all possible circumstances" ([ICAO Doc 9835](https://www.skybrary.aero/bookshelf/books/2497.pdf)). Both patterns bear directly on a language meant to be *native*. Native speakers optimise for effort even more relentlessly than pilots do, and they have to talk about everything.

## Conclusion

The evidence moves the design target. "Remove ambiguity" is the wrong goal: natural languages are efficiently ambiguous, and live repair handles detectable trouble every 1.4 minutes at low cost. The right goal is narrower: **make the few high-cost failures detectable or impossible, and leave the rest to context and repair**. Those few are context-free tokens, competing referents, unmarked force and urgency, stance words without numbers, and overloaded messages. The best-evidenced fixes are not exotic grammar. They are confirmation loops, fixed slots for critical content, and numbers attached to words. All three were proven as *procedures* for trained adults, and the largest open question is whether they survive becoming *grammar* for children who will wear them down, as natural languages wore down every contrast that did too little work.

That also suggests questioning the north star itself, as a proposal rather than an override. "Faithful transmission of thought" has no counterweight, yet every natural system in these notes trades fidelity against effort: ~39 bits/s across 17 languages, ambiguity as efficiency, 70% non-compliance with prescribed forms. Obligatory categories show fidelity hurting itself, by forcing speakers to state precision they don't have. A requirement framed as "faithful transmission *at a cost speakers will actually pay*", with an explicit "unspecified" value wherever the speaker doesn't know, would fit the evidence better. Without it, a fidelity-maximising language risks becoming the grammatical equivalent of Kent's estimative scale: correct on paper, fudged in use.

## Candidate design requirements, traced to evidence

Each requirement below names its evidence and an evidence-strength label. **Measured** means a study measured the fix itself, even if only as a professional protocol. **Well-motivated but unmeasured** means the problem is documented but the fix is untested. **Speculative** means both the problem and the fix rest on theory or thin data. The "Origin" column separates requirements the research notes themselves inferred from those added during this synthesis. The added ones deserve the most scrutiny, because they sound reasoned without having been tested.

| # | Requirement | Traced to | Strength | Origin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Confirmation as the default grammar.** The standard acknowledgment restates the content ("I will not overtake…") and distinguishes received / understood / will act. Senders get a cheap form to flag a wrong echo. | I-PASS −23% errors [secondary, pre/post]; closed-loop orders 3.6× faster; 11 of 15 bad readbacks undetected (EUROCONTROL); SMCP echo answers; ROGER vs WILCO | **Measured** (as protocol, bundled; never as grammar) | Notes |
| 2 | **Fixed slots for high-stakes content**: a short, leading schema form (what / how urgent / what to do / contingency) for handoffs and instructions | I-PASS; SBAR "moderate"; omission as medicine's leading handoff factor (JC SEA 58) | **Measured** (pre/post, bundled) | Notes |
| 3 | **Confidence carried by number-anchored markers**: enough levels (≥6), each with a fixed numeric value that is taught, kept separate from politeness | Budescu 2014 (24 countries, dual format); Wintle 2019 (32% → 66%); Kent 1964; forecasting bins [secondary]; Bonnefon & Villejoubert (hedge read as tact) | **Measured** for numeric anchoring; level count [secondary] | Notes |
| 4 | **Phoneme and sign inventory chosen for robustness.** Heavy-load contrasts ride on manner, voicing, nasality, vowel backness and stress; avoid place-only minimal pairs, especially within one part of speech; five vowels /i e a o u/. For signs: location and gross movement carry load, small maximally distinct handshape set, any fine handshapes near the face. | Miller & Nicely; Cutler 2004; Wedel functional load [secondary]; PHOIBLE; Emmorey & Corina, Bochner, Orfanidou, Caselli [secondary]; Lane 1976 [secondary] | **Measured** perception data; whole-language benefit unmeasured | Notes |
| 5 | **Validate the core lexicon with whole-set confusion matrices** in noise (and visual degradation for signs), with speakers of many first languages, in isolation and in context; retest after every substitution | ICAO 1959 (p < 0.001; "unexpected parts of the matrix"; noise intensifies existing confusions) | **Measured** (method, for one closed set) | Notes |
| 6 | **Numbers get their own encoding**: strictly compositional base-10 words, phonologically distinct from every function word and from each other; a mandatory type/unit tag (heading vs altitude vs dose); a digit-by-digit form for context-free numbers | "two/to" crash (Doc 9835); headings and frequencies as top readback errors; format leakage (AM-06/25); 10,000/11,000 busts [secondary]; digit dominance (ICAO 1959); Chinese teens [secondary]; "QD"/"U" [secondary] | **Well-motivated but unmeasured** (problem measured; digit-respelling rationale [unverified]) | Notes |
| 7 | **Built-in redundancy where context is thin**: names, numbers and codes are longer, multi-syllabic, and differ in stress and syllable count, while predictable function words stay short | Miller & Nicely ("a miss is as good as a mile"); Shannon 75% vs ~0 for isolated tokens; ICAO polysyllable finding; smooth-signal and uniform-information-density studies [secondary] | **Measured** for the alphabet; otherwise well-motivated | Notes |
| 8 | **Distinctive referent handles**: an optional referent index or proximate/obviative device for when two or more same-kind referents compete; a cheap marker of whether the speaker assumes the listener knows who is meant | Call signs 33% (EUROCONTROL); person/thing/place dominate repair; late detection of "who's X?"; Bequia's three "who"s; switch-reference and obviation exist, but with **no** measured benefit | **Well-motivated but unmeasured** | Notes |
| 9 | **Fast, specific repair forms**: a short open "huh?"; "which-referent?", "which-number?" and kind-marked "what?" queries; a candidate-offer form | Universal three formats (92%); specificity principle; Murrinh-Patha class-marked "what?"; LSA freeze-look | **Well-motivated but unmeasured** (formats universal; benefit of designing them untested) | Notes |
| 10 | **Speech act marked by a segment or manual sign, never by melody alone**, including the authority distinction (instruction vs advice), kept separate from politeness. The signed surface may use a parallel face channel. | Tenerife; WALS 116 (18% intonation-only, 61% particles); SMCP markers (no outcome data); sarcasm in email 56% [secondary]; Zeshan's 35 sign languages [secondary] | **Well-motivated but unmeasured** | Notes |
| 11 | **Urgency on a small discrete scale at message start**, as a closed-class token rather than a matter of tone | Avianca [secondary]; MAYDAY/PAN-PAN; I-PASS leads with illness severity | **Well-motivated but unmeasured** | Notes |
| 12 | **No bare yes/no after negative questions**: echo answers (repeat the verb) or separate "agree" and "contradict" particles | Polarity- vs truth-based systems (Holmberg); English bare "yes" indeterminate [secondary]; SMCP fully worded answers | **Well-motivated but unmeasured** | Notes |
| 13 | **Explicit time anchors**: anchor (now / a named event) plus a signed offset; no motion metaphors for rescheduling; tense optional | "Moved forward" 54/46 split [secondary]; Swiss vs Standard German; Mandarin tenseless with no measured harm | **Well-motivated but unmeasured** | Notes |
| 14 | **Short dependencies and a per-message load cap**: right-branching by default, flattening devices instead of nesting, a recommended chunk size for critical messages | Complexity curve 2–6% → ~38% (AM-06/25); ≥5 elements = 26% of errors; center-embedding limits [secondary]; Simplified Technical English on hard texts [secondary] | **Measured** (correlational field data; small STE trials) | Notes |
| 15 | **Scope follows word order**, with an explicit marker for the rare inverse reading | Surface-order preference [secondary]; children miss inverse readings [unverified figures] | **Speculative** | Notes |
| 16 | **Explicit non-literal marker** (irony, joke, hypothetical), and a lexical distinction between "some" and "some but not all" | Sarcasm 78% expected vs 56% achieved in text [secondary]; ~40% miss scalar implicature [secondary]; emotion prosody 52–74% across cultures [secondary] | **Well-motivated but unmeasured** | Notes (marker); **added in synthesis** (some/some-not-all) |
| 17 | **Plain mode that keeps the safety rules**: a fully expressive register that still obeys requirements 1, 3, 6, 10 and 11 | 60% of an emergency dialogue in plain language (Doc 9835); ICAO language-proficiency accidents | **Well-motivated but unmeasured** | Notes |
| 18 | **Required forms cheap to say and costly to omit**: safety redundancy should be short, and its absence audible (for example, a number without a type tag is ungrammatical) | 70% non-compliance (Mell 1992); 44% non-standard per flight [secondary]; digit grouping (AM-96/26); only 26% of trauma orders closed-loop | **Well-motivated but unmeasured** | Notes; "ungrammatical when omitted" **added in synthesis** |
| 19 | **An explicit "unspecified" value** on every marked dimension (source, confidence, time, number, referent), so the grammar never forces false precision | Jakobson/Boas on obligatory categories; Tosun 2013 [secondary]; Kent's "poets" | **Speculative** | Notes |

The evidence also supports **removing** several features a fidelity-first language might be expected to include. Each deletion has an add-back trigger: the concrete finding that would bring the feature back.

| Proposed deletion | Why | Add-back trigger | Origin |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Obligatory evidentiality** (keep it optional, separate from confidence) | 57% of languages have it, but children master it late, the cognitive benefit is mixed, Turkish adults remember second-hand information *worse*, and it gets repurposed for blame and politeness | A study showing listeners in evidential languages weigh hearsay more accurately (candidates the notes found but didn't read: a 2026 Turkish trust preprint, [arXiv 2604.24665](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.24665), and a hearsay/commitment EMG study, [Open Mind](https://direct.mit.edu/opmi/article/doi/10.1162/OPMI.a.382/138850/The-Effect-of-Hearsay-on-Speaker-Commitment-An)) | Notes |
| **Obligatory tense** | Mandarin and Cantonese show no measured harm; learners read time accurately | Evidence that speakers of tenseless languages misread time in real communication more often | Notes |
| **Zero ambiguity / no homophones anywhere** (target only same-context, action-relevant ambiguity) | Ambiguity is efficient (Piantadosi); context resolves most of it; removing it costs word length | Confusion testing that shows context-resolved homophones failing in noise or in text | Notes |
| **Grammatical gender as a reference device** (use the optional referent index of requirement 8 instead) | Gender helps pronoun resolution only when it happens to differ between referents, and forces a commitment speakers may not intend | A comparison showing higher pronoun misresolution in genderless languages that an index can't match | **Added in synthesis** |
| **A closed phraseology as the language's core** | 70% non-compliance; closed codes run out in novel situations | None expected. Closed sets stay limited to numbers, spelling and urgency tokens | **Added in synthesis** |

Two cautions apply to the whole list. Every "measured" label refers to adults using a protocol or reading a document, mostly in English-speaking or Western samples, never to a native language learned in childhood. And the two features most central to faithful transmission, explicit speech-act markers and explicit stance, are also the least tested. They make the best first experiments for a prototype: give two groups the same messages with and without the marker, and measure the gap between what the speaker meant and what the listener understood.
