# A1: Design sketch from the goal

## Framing

The aim is B1: meaning gets through. B2/B3/B4/B6/B9/B10 must hold. Everything else is priced as lifetime effort (B8) plus learning effort (B7), with cross-language convergence (B5) as the evidence. One pattern recurs below: **economy by default, precision on demand**. The unmarked form is short and vague; every distinction B3 needs has an explicit, regular marker that can be added. The guarantee is that the precise form *exists*, not that people use it.

Three working rules:
- **Arbitrary costs must pay for themselves (C1).** A root, marker or distinction is added only if its lifetime savings exceed its learning cost.
- **"Many languages share it" means many unrelated families, not many speakers (B5, N3).** I use WALS-type samples, not population counts.
- **Time is the real B8 metric, not syllables.** Coupé et al. (2019) found 17 languages converge on about 39 bits/s: syllable-dense languages are spoken slower, syllable-light ones faster. Simple syllables therefore cost little time, which frees the sound system to optimise B2 and B10.

## 1. Sound system

**Vowels: 5, /a e i o u/, length not contrastive.** Five is the modal inventory (188 of 564 languages, WALS 2). It can be spread to the corners of the vowel space, maximising acoustic distance (B2), and is the easiest system for adults of any first language (B10). More vowels would shorten words (B8), but the time argument makes that saving small.

**Consonants: 13, /p t k b d g f s m n l w y/.**
- All are among the world's most frequent segments; /m k n j/ each occur in over 90% of PHOIBLE languages.
- Voicing contrasts in stops, as in most languages.
- One liquid, written *l*, pronounced [l], [r] or [ɾ]. Many families (Japanese, Korean, Chinese, much of Bantu) don't contrast l and r; merging them removes a classic mishearing (B2) and learner trap (B10).
- /s/ may be [s] or [ʃ], for the same reason.
- No /h/: acoustically weak, easily lost in noise and often lost historically (B9).
- No affricates, clicks or ejectives: each would buy B8 savings at a B10 and B2 cost.
- /f/ is the doubtful one (section 7). It earns its place mainly by keeping world-shared loans recognisable (L4).

The inventory is small next to the mode of 22 (WALS 1): sized for distinctness, not density.

**Syllables: (C)V(n).** Only a nasal coda, assimilating in place.
- CV is the universal unmarked syllable, and children's first syllables are CV everywhere.
- A nasal coda is among the most widespread coda types (Japanese, Mandarin, Spanish, Yoruba). It doubles the syllable inventory to 14 onsets × 5 vowels × 2 = 140 syllables at little perceptual cost.
- No clusters. Most languages (56.5%, WALS 12) allow some, but clusters are where adult learners (B10) and noisy channels (B2) fail.

**Prosody: no lexical tone; fixed, non-contrastive stress on the first syllable of each root, so it is unwritten.**
- 58% of languages are non-tonal (WALS 13).
- Tone is hard for adult learners (B10), and newer studies show even native children master tone later than once thought.
- Tone can't be written in plain Latin letters without diacritics (B4, L3).
- Fixed stress marks word boundaries (B2). Initial and penultimate are the two most common fixed positions (WALS 14); for the typical two-syllable root they coincide.
- Intonation is free; nothing grammatical depends on it (section 5).

## 2. How word forms are chosen

- **Length tracks information content, not just frequency** (Zipf's law of abbreviation; Piantadosi et al. 2011, 11 languages).
  - One syllable: particles, pronouns, digits and a few dozen highly predictable words. Most sit in slots where syntax predicts their class, offsetting the higher mishearing risk of monosyllables.
  - Two syllables (CV.CV(n)): core content roots, about 140² ≈ 19,600 forms, far more than needed.
  - Longer: compounds and derivations, so transparent building aligns length with rarity automatically.
- **Distinctness (B2):**
  - Within one word class, no two of the ~2,000 most frequent words may differ by a single confusable feature in a single segment. Voicing and place among stops are the confusable features (Miller & Nicely 1955 noise confusions and the 2006 replication).
  - Minimal pairs are allowed across classes, because word order disambiguates them.
  - Words that context *cannot* disambiguate must be maximally distinct: digits, the negation particle, yes/no.
- **No sampling from big languages.** Root forms are assigned by these rules, not borrowed. Few word forms are truly world-shared, and drawing from major languages would weight cultures by population (N3) or favour one family (B5). Two exceptions follow convergence:
  - nursery words of the *mama/papa* type, which recur independently across families;
  - documented sound-meaning biases (Blasi et al. 2016, 4,000+ languages: e.g. nasals in "nose", /i/ in "small"), used as tie-breakers.
- C3 runs last: among equal-cost candidates, drop those resembling a common offensive word in a major language.

## 3. Basic words, growth and loans

**Which concepts get roots.** Candidates:
- the ~65 Natural Semantic Metalanguage primes (Goddard & Wierzbicka), claimed to be lexicalised in every language;
- the Leipzig-Jakarta 100, the meanings most resistant to borrowing across 41 languages (body parts, pronouns, demonstratives, basic verbs);
- concepts monomorphemic in most languages of a diverse sample.

A candidate becomes a root only if it passes the C1 test: paraphrase cost × lifetime frequency exceeds the cost of learning one more root. Estimate: 1,000-1,500 roots, well above minimalist systems (which fail B3/B6 through long paraphrase) and far below natural languages' tens of thousands of arbitrary words (B7).

**Growth: compounding plus a small set of regular derivational suffixes.**
- Compounds are head-first, consistent with the noun phrase (section 4), as in Vietnamese, Thai and Romance.
- Suffixes over prefixes: predominantly suffixing languages outnumber predominantly prefixing ones about 7:1 (WALS 26). About 15 suffixes: agent, instrument, place, quality, causative, diminutive, opposite, collective and similar.
- Every derivation is compositional by rule (B7); lexicalised drift in meaning is allowed later (N4).

**Loans (L4): only forms the world already shares:**
- international scientific terms, SI units and prefixes, element names, Latin binomials;
- widely shared words of the *taxi/internet/kilo* type;
- proper names.

Common loans are fitted to (C)V(n) by a fixed mapping (a cluster is split by an echo vowel, *r* becomes *l*, *h* is dropped) and spelled phonemically. Proper names keep their source Latin spelling or standard romanisation (pinyin, Hepburn, ISO 9), marked by a capital (L2, L3). A concept with no world-shared form is built from native roots, never borrowed from one language (B5, N3).

## 4. Core grammar

**Roles: subject-verb-object (SVO), no case marking, prepositions for other roles.**
- SOV is slightly more frequent than SVO (564 vs 488, WALS 81). SVO still wins, for two reasons.
- First, SOV languages overwhelmingly rely on case marking. Gibson et al. (2013) give a noisy-channel account: a verb between subject and object keeps roles recoverable without case when a noun is misheard (B1, B2).
- Second, native speakers converge on it. Bergen (2001) found native-speaking Esperanto children lost or modified the accusative and fixed their word order. Case is what natives shed (B9), and order needs no morphology (B7).
- Prepositions follow from the VO correlations (Dryer). A closed set of about 12 marks instrument, recipient, location, source, goal, cause, comitative and similar roles.
- **Precision on demand (B3):** any phrase may be fronted for topic or focus if it carries an explicit role marker; a dedicated object marker exists only for that.

**Word building: roots never change form, grammar is carried by separate particles, derivation is suffixal.** No obligatory word-class endings: Esperanto-style endings cost a syllable on nearly every word, a lifetime B8 cost. Function comes from position, and derivational suffixes make the class explicit when needed (B3). Particles are fully syllabic and always written (B4), and unlike fused inflection they don't erode into irregular paradigms later generations must memorise (B7, B9).

**Tense, aspect and mood: all optional.** An unmarked verb takes its time from context, as in Chinese, Indonesian and Yoruba; about half the languages in WALS 65 lack grammatical perfective/imperfective marking.
- Preverbal particles in fixed order: mood, tense, aspect, verb. This mirrors the cross-linguistic relevance order, aspect closest to the verb (Bybee).
- Tense: past, future. Aspect: completed, ongoing, habitual. Mood: ability, permission, obligation, intention, conditional/irrealis.
- Optional evidentials (witnessed, inferred, reported): cheap precision for B3, used only when it matters.

**Questions.**
- Yes/no: a sentence-final particle, the most common strategy (585 of 955, WALS 116), and visible in writing (B4), unlike intonation alone.
- Content questions keep the question word in place, as in 615 of 902 languages (WALS 93). No movement rule (B7).
- Question words are regular: question particle + generic noun ("which-person", "which-place", "which-time", "which-way", "which-reason", "which-amount"). This avoids the flaw of Esperanto's correlative table, a regular grid of words differing by one sound (easy to mishear, B2): here the distinguishing part is a full, distinct root. Indefinites ("some-person") and negatives ("no-person") follow the same pattern.

**Negation.** A preverbal particle, the most common type (502 of 1,157, WALS 112). Scope follows position: before any constituent, it negates that constituent (B3).
- The defined precise form is one negation per scope.
- Doubling for emphasis (negative concord) is left to use (N4); negators erode and get reinforced everywhere (Jespersen's cycle), so freezing them is futile (B9).

**Pronouns.**
- Persons 1, 2, 3, no gender: most languages lack it in pronouns (WALS 44), and it imposes no culture's scheme (L2).
- Plurals by rule: pronoun + plural particle. Inclusive "we" = I+you, exclusive "we" = I+they: the distinction about a third of languages make (63 of 200, WALS 39) at zero extra roots (B3, B7).
- One reflexive/logophoric "self" refers to the clause subject or reported speaker, resolving "she said she..." (logophorics are attested in West Africa).
- Politeness is not grammatical; it follows host-culture conventions (C2, N4).

**Nouns.** Number-neutral by default, with an optional plural particle. No obligatory articles; demonstratives and a "one/a certain" word carry definiteness when needed. No numeral classifiers.

**Modifiers: consistently head-first.**
- Noun-adjective (879 vs 373, WALS 87), noun-genitive with a linker "of", noun-relative clause. One headedness rule covers all phrases and compounds (B7).
- Demonstrative and numeral precede the noun: Dem Num N Adj, one of the two most frequent orders of these four.
- Bracketing ambiguity ("dark blue house", "old men and women") is resolved by an optional grouping linker (B3).

**Relative clauses.** Postnominal (579 of 824, WALS 90), introduced by one invariant particle. Subject and object may be gaps; any other role takes a resumptive pronoun. Natural languages use resumptives exactly where gaps get hard (low on the Keenan-Comrie hierarchy), and this lets any position be relativised unambiguously (B3).

**Scope and coordination.**
- Quantifier scope follows linear order: in "every child read a book", "every" scopes wide. To reverse it, front the phrase with its topic marker.
- Two "or"s, inclusive and exclusive. Separate "and" for noun phrases and for clauses.
- Each costs one word, guarantees an unambiguous form (B3) and costs nothing when unused.

## 5. Numbers and writing

**Numbers.**
- Strictly decimal (125 of 196 languages, WALS 131). Ten digit roots, then multipliers for ten, hundred and thousand, read large to small: 23 = "two-ten three".
- No irregular teens or tens: Miller et al. (1995) trace Chinese children's counting advantage to this transparency.
- Above a thousand, SI prefixes *kilo/mega/giga/tera* serve as world-shared forms (L3, L4), sidestepping the long/short-scale "billion" conflict.
- Digit words get the strictest distinctness rule: phone numbers and codes have no context to disambiguate them (B2).
- Ordinals and fractions by regular suffixes.
- Hindu-Arabic numerals, ISO 8601 dates (year-month-day), 24-hour time, SI units (L3).
- The decimal sign follows local convention, since ISO 80000 allows comma and point (L2); in speech it is one fixed word.

**Writing.**
- Latin alphabet, one ASCII letter per phoneme, no diacritics: a e i o u p t k b d g f s m n l w y. Usable on any keyboard, in URLs and online (L3, P4, B4).
- /j/ is written **y**, not IPA *j*: *y* is used across unrelated families (Malay/Indonesian, Swahili, Yoruba, Tagalog, Turkish, Hepburn, pinyin, Spanish, English), while *j* for /j/ is essentially European (B5, N3).
- Unused letters (c h j q r v x z) appear only in proper names.
- Capitals mark proper names; particles are written as separate words; stress is unwritten.

**Speech-writing parity (B4) is a grammatical rule, not just a spelling rule.** Anything that changes meaning is carried by a word, never by intonation or emphasis alone: questions, focus, contrast, and irony markers where wanted. Writing therefore loses nothing.

## 6. Left undefined (N4) and resisting drift (B9)

**Deliberately undefined:**
- **Phonetic detail inside each phoneme:** the l/r/ɾ range, s/ʃ, aspiration, vowel quality within its zone. Accents are expected and harmless because the margins are wide.
- **Fast-speech reductions and contractions.**
- **Idiom, metaphor, slang, registers, discourse particles, swearing and politeness.** These come from the host culture (C2).
- **Lexicalisation:** which compounds become words, which drift in meaning, which roots get clipped once frequent.
- **Usage choices:** whether speakers use the optional markers (tense, evidentials, plural, negative concord).
- **Vocabulary beyond the initial core.**

No academy: B9 declines to control change.

**How it survives change** (robustness, not control):
1. **Wide margins.** Few, well-separated phonemes with common confusions (l/r, s/ʃ) pre-merged, so likely sound changes destroy little.
2. **Nothing fragile to erode.** No case, agreement or fused inflection. Roles are carried by order, the state native Esperanto children converged to anyway.
3. **Nothing to regularise.** Children regularise unpredictable input (Hudson Kam & Newport 2005); a fully regular system gives that pressure nothing to do.
4. **Typologically common choices are attractor states.** Change tends to move languages toward frequent types, so the design starts near where drift would take it.
5. **A precise form always exists.** If casual forms blur, B3 still holds through the explicit, compositional forms.
6. **Phonemic spelling on a shared alphabet** keeps one written norm across accents (P4).

## 7. Least-sure decisions and what would settle them

1. **13 consonants vs about 20.**
   - A larger inventory shortens words, and natives learn 22 consonants easily (N2 warns against favouring adults).
   - The 39-bits/s finding suggests time cost evens out, but it was measured across natural languages, not at an inventory this small.
   - *Settle by:* modelling word length and confusability in noise for both inventories, then perception-in-noise and child artificial-language-learning experiments; /f/ and voicing first.
2. **The minimum-distance lexicon.**
   - Natural lexicons are *more* phonologically clustered than chance (Dautriche et al. 2017). Clustering may aid learning and memory, cutting against spreading words apart for B2.
   - *Settle by:* learning and recognition-in-noise experiments comparing clustered and dispersed artificial lexicons, with children as well as adults.
3. **Isolating particles and no class endings.**
   - Particles favour B7 and B9. Bound morphology may be processed faster by natives, and class endings add parsing redundancy (B1).
   - *Settle by:* comprehension studies on child learners of matched mini-languages, plus creole and native-Esperanto evidence on which morphology children keep or add.
4. **SVO over SOV.**
   - SOV is the commonest order and the default in improvised gesture studies.
   - The noisy-channel argument depends on having no case. A light, regular case marker plus SOV might be equally robust.
   - *Settle by:* noisy-channel experiments comparing caseless SVO with SOV plus one regular object marker, measuring role recovery and learning cost.
