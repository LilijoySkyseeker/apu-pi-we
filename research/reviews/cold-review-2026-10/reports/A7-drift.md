# A7: What native speakers would do to the language (drift review)

## 1. Severity totals

| Severity | Count | Findings |
|---|---|---|
| High | 3 | F1 optional markers won't stay optional · F2 the pause/comma is load-bearing · F3 grammar-word phonology fights reduction |
| Medium | 5 | F4 `pi` overload · F5 two-syllable operators truncate onto one-syllable ones · F6 `tin` imperative · F7 manner slot · F8 positional existential `gun` |
| Low | 10 | F9–F18 (mostly harmless innovations the design forbids on paper) |

Headline: **meaning rarely breaks** under the changes predicted here. The language is invariant-word, head-initial, SVO, particle-based, and it already looks like what three generations of native speakers turn pidgins and designed languages into. That is its real B9 strength. Where it is fragile is (a) in anything that relies on a *pause* or a *comma* to carry grammar, (b) in anything the reference calls *optional*, which children will make categorical, (c) in a crowded set of one-syllable grammar words whose contrasts rest on a single vowel or a glide, and (d) in one overloaded word, `pi`, that carries the count/ordinal and date systems alone.

### Top 8 findings

1. **F1 (high).** Children make optional markers categorical: `sin` becomes past, a future particle appears, `gun` drops before qualities and becomes a passive marker, `tin` drops. Harmless except `mu sin` "not yet" merging with "didn't".
2. **F2 (high).** The pause/comma closes relative clauses, topics, derived subjects and address; speakers won't produce it reliably and texters drop it. Natives will invent a spoken closer (Tok Pisin *ia*); give them one now.
3. **F3 (high).** Grammar words are told to stay unreduced while being unstressed and the most frequent words in the language. Glide-only pairs (`wun/un`, `wo/o`, `yen/en`) and voicing-only pairs (`tin/din`, `pu/bu`, `pen/ben`) will merge first.
4. **F4 (medium).** `pi` alone separates "seven days" from "Sunday" and "ten months" from "October"; a freq-8 linker erodes (Mandarin *de*, Spanish *de*). Mark ordinals and dates by order or a stressed word.
5. **F5 (medium).** `manni` > `man` and `konsin` > `kon` by ordinary clipping (*baimbai > bai*), landing on words in the same slot: habitual vs relative, "at least N" vs "N in 6". Rename both.
6. **F6 (medium).** Bare-verb commands are the universal; `tin` will drop and `!` will carry meaning the reference says it doesn't. Keep `tin` as the precise form, accept the bare one.
7. **F7/F8 (medium).** Manner will move after the object, making `nawi pasu yobe` ambiguous; subject drop will kill the positional "there is" and `ibe` will replace it. Both are regularizations; let them.
8. **F11–F13 (low, N4).** `lanno` > polite you, `me` generic, `keya` epistemic, `tan`/`fi` > articles, a yes-word, a 3-term confidence system: all near-certain, all harmless. Replace every "never" with "in the reference variety".

Scope note: the mandate is P3/B9/C2/N4. I did not search for the language online, and did not find it incidentally. All evidence cited is from the general literature.

## 2. Method and evidence base

I imagined a community of children acquiring the reference variety from fluent adult (L2) speakers, then two further generations of natives, speaking and writing (texting included). For each part of the design I asked: what do children do with this kind of input, and what do adult speakers do with this kind of form over time?

The evidence I lean on, with what each is good for:

- **Children regularize probabilistic input.** Hudson Kam & Newport 2005 (*Language Learning and Development* 1:151–195) and 2009 (*Cognitive Psychology* 59:30–66): children exposed to an artificial language whose post-nominal "determiners" appeared with probability 45–100% either used them always or never; adults reproduced the input probabilities. This is the single most important result for a design built on "optional when precision matters".
- **Nativization of Tok Pisin.** Sankoff & Laberge 1973 (*Kivung* 6:32–47): the future adverb *baimbai* became the obligatory, unstressed, preverbal particle *bai* in the speech of the first native generation (reduced, moved closer to the verb, used far more often). Sankoff & Brown 1976 (*Language* 52:631–666): speakers invented a relative-clause bracket out of the deictic *ia* "here", placed at both edges of the clause, because nothing else marked where the clause ended.
- **Native Esperanto.** Bergen 2001 (*J. Child Language* 28:575–595): eight native speakers aged 6–14 showed loss or modification of the accusative, **phonological reduction**, **attrition of the tense/aspect system** and **pronominal cliticization**. Corsetti, Pinto & Tolomeo 2004 (*Language Problems & Language Planning* 28:261–282): children over-regularize even a language that is already regular. Lindstedt 2006 ("Native Esperanto as a test case for natural language"): the result stays mutually intelligible with the standard; nativization does not creolize a language that is already simple.
- **Nicaraguan Sign Language.** Senghas & Coppola 2001 (*Psychological Science* 12:323–328); Senghas, Kita & Özyürek 2004 (*Science* 305:1779–1782): children under 10 *added* systematic grammar (spatial reference tracking, segmentation of holistic gestures) that their input lacked. Children do not only erode; they build devices where reference tracking is underdetermined.
- **Modern Hebrew.** Native speakers collapsed the pharyngeals, fixed SVO, built periphrastic modals and auxiliaries, and regularized the verb patterns (Ravid 1995, *Language Change in Child and Adult Hebrew*; Bolozky 1997; Zuckermann 2006). Good evidence that natives will not keep an inventory distinction their surrounding languages lack, and will regularize what a prescriptive grammar leaves irregular or stipulated.
- **Grammaticalization paths.** Bybee, Perkins & Pagliuca 1994 (*The Evolution of Grammar*); Heine & Kuteva 2002 (*World Lexicon of Grammaticalization*); Hopper & Traugott 2003. Used for: anterior > past; want/go > future; copula > passive marker; have > existential; give > dative; take > instrumental; say > complementizer; demonstrative > article; "one" > indefinite article; title > polite pronoun (Heine & Song 2010 *Language and Cognition*; 2012 *J. Linguistics*); deontic > epistemic (Sweetser 1990; Papafragou 1998 for the child order).
- **Reduction of frequent function words.** Zipf 1935; Bybee 2001 (*Phonology and Language Use*); Jurafsky, Bell, Gregory & Raymond 2001: the more frequent and predictable a word, the more it reduces, and function words lead. Intensifier renewal: Ito & Tagliamonte 2003; Tagliamonte 2008. Negation renewal: Jespersen 1917; Dahl 1979; Schwenter 2005 on Brazilian Portuguese *não … não*.
- **Prosody as a syntactic cue.** Listeners, including 4-year-olds, use prosodic breaks (Snedeker & Yuan 2008 *JML* 58:574–608; Snedeker & Trueswell 2003). But speakers produce disambiguating breaks unreliably: Allbritton, McKoon & Ratcliff 1996 found naive speakers rarely do; Kraljic & Brennan 2005 found they do when the ambiguity is situationally salient. The problem is production, not perception.
- **Writing and texting.** Tagliamonte & Denis 2008 (*American Speech* 83:3–34, 1M-word IM corpus): IM is grammatically conservative, but punctuation, capitalization and apostrophes are freely dropped; Baron 2008 (*Always On*); Ling & Baron 2007; Crystal 2008. Whatever the design makes a comma do, texters will not do.
- **Sound inventories.** Five-vowel /a e i o u/ systems are the most common and most stable (Crothers 1978; Maddieson 1984). Japanese *wo* > *o* merger (Frellesvig 2010, *A History of the Japanese Language*): a glide before a round vowel is a weak contrast. Japanese *heibanka* (accent flattening) in urban speakers under ~25 (Takada and others; NHK surveys): a pitch-only accent is being levelled in a large living speech community.
- **Imperatives.** Aikhenvald 2010 (*Imperatives and Commands*): the bare stem is the near-universal imperative; children's first directives are bare verbs. Early "no": Bloom 1970; Pea 1980; Nelson 1973 (among the first 50 words).
- **Question particles.** Dryer, WALS ch. 116: particles are the most common polar-question strategy, but intonation-only questions are the colloquial norm in many particle languages (Japanese casual *iku?*, Polish without *czy*, Brazilian Portuguese).
- **Word-order universals.** Greenberg 1963 Universal 20; Cinque 2005: N-A-Num-Dem is one of the two most common noun-phrase orders. Cysouw, WALS ch. 39: inclusive/exclusive "we" is common and stable where it exists. Tomlin 1986: verb–object bonding.
- **Numbers.** Miller, Smith, Zhu & Zhang 1995 (*Psychological Science*); Fuson & Kwon 1992: transparent Chinese-style numerals are acquired faster than English ones.
- **Evidentials.** Aikhenvald 2004: obligatory evidentials are small systems (2–5 terms), acquired early where obligatory (Aksu-Koç 1988 for Turkish); optional epistemic marking is a discourse strategy and is unstable.

## 3. Findings

Format: prediction · evidence · consequence · severity · direction. "Confusable after the change" lists the pairs that matter.

### F1. Nothing stays optional: children will make each optional marker categorical (HIGH)

**Prediction.** Principles 1 and 7 ("the common case costs nothing"; "a precise way always exists, but need not always be used") describe adult L2 behaviour, not native behaviour. Native children exposed to variable use of `gun`, `la`, `sin`, `le`, `tin`, `min`, `man`, `nun`, `kon` and the pauses will, per marker, either use it always or drop it. My best guesses, most likely first:

1. `gun` before a quality is **dropped** (`guyi kudu` = "the house is big" *and* "the big house", context decides, as in Indonesian and Mandarin). `gun` before an action word (undergoer state, `wan gun kuwi` "she is protected") is **kept**, because there it is contrastive with the active. Net: `gun` grammaticalizes from copula to **passive/resultative marker** (copula > passive is a standard path).
2. `sin` becomes an **obligatory past** marker, not "done". Anterior > past/perfective is the most travelled aspect path (Bybee et al. 1994; French *passé composé*, German *Perfekt*); Bergen 2001 found tense/aspect attrition in native Esperanto; Sankoff & Laberge found natives make a once-optional marker near-obligatory. The frame-setting rule ("the first `sin` sets the frame, later verbs stay unmarked") requires discourse bookkeeping children will not do; they will mark each past verb.
3. A **future marker** is created, reduced and made preverbal, exactly as *baimbai > bai*. Candidates: `momu` "want" (want > future: English *will*, Greek *tha*, Swahili *-ta-*), `isi` "go" (go > future), `buntu` "about to" (> *bun*), or `de` extended to all persons (F11).
4. `tin` is dropped from ordinary commands (F6). Sentence-initial `min` becomes optional in speech, with rising intonation; final `min` "right?" survives (final particles are acquired by age 2 in Mandarin and are very stable).
5. `la` stays optional but specializes to definite/specific plural, like Haitian *yo* (which is also the 3pl pronoun, cf. `wan la` "they"). Harmless.
6. `man` may become an obligatory attributive linker (`guyi man kudu`) in some communities, the way Tok Pisin fixed *-pela* on adjectives and Mandarin uses *de*. This would undo the brevity of `guyi kudu` (B8), not its meaning.
7. `sin le` "was doing / had been doing" will be said despite "sin and le don't combine" (anterior + nonpunctual stacking is a creole universal: Bickerton 1981, Guyanese *bin a*).

**Evidence.** Hudson Kam & Newport 2005/2009 (children regularize optional determiners); Sankoff & Laberge 1973; Bergen 2001; Bickerton 1981; child copula omission (Brown 1973; creole zero copula, Holm 2000).

**Consequence.** Mostly harmless for B9, with three real breaks:
- `mu sin` "not yet" (8.1) becomes "didn't", because `sin` is now past. "Not yet" will need a new form (Swahili *-ja-*, Malay *belum* and Tok Pisin *i no … yet* all have a dedicated one). Confusable: `to mu sin nawi` "I haven't eaten yet" vs "I didn't eat".
- The reference's reading of a bare `obu.` as a statement "(someone) comes" dies; bare verb = command (F6).
- `gun`-less predication and attribution merge in speech: `ewo niba` "the person walks" / "the walking person". The reference already allows this, so it is not a break, but the "clear forms" in 4.2 will be the forms *nobody uses* unless one is made obligatory.
- B8 cost rises (every past verb marked, maybe every adjective linked), which is the price natives pay for predictability. The reference should not fight it.

**Severity.** High, because it is the core design principle that fights acquisition, not because meaning breaks widely.

**Direction.** Reword principles 1 and 7 as predictions rather than norms: say which reading is the *default* when a marker is absent (the reference mostly does) and stop describing `sin`, `gun`, `tin` as "when precision matters". Define "not yet" with a form that does not depend on `sin` meaning "done". Expect a future marker and leave room for it (e.g. don't give `buntu` a second job).

### F2. The pause / comma is load-bearing grammar, and it will not be produced reliably (HIGH)

**Prediction.** Section 4.6 makes a pause do six jobs: close a relative clause (and all open ones), separate a derived-word subject from its verb, separate an opener/topic, mark address, break `yen`-joined clauses, and set off stance words. In speech, the pause is a planning boundary as much as a syntactic one: speakers pause where they hesitate and run on where they don't. In writing, the comma is exactly what texters drop first, and the reference itself licenses dropping it ("in casual writing, pauses that context makes obvious may be dropped"). The disambiguating commas are not the obvious ones.

Within a generation, speakers will invent a spoken **closer** for relative clauses and derived-word phrases, most likely from a demonstrative or the plural: `ewo man obu tan` / `ewo man obu la` ("the person who comes, that/those"), bracket-style. This is what Tok Pisin natives did with *ia* (Sankoff & Brown 1976), and what Mandarin *de*, Japanese *no* and Tok Pisin *ia* all show: a head-initial or head-final language with no relative pronoun grows an edge marker. Different communities will pick different closers unless the reference offers one.

**Evidence.** Sankoff & Brown 1976; Allbritton, McKoon & Ratcliff 1996 (speakers fail to produce disambiguating prosody); Kraljic & Brennan 2005 (they do only when the ambiguity is salient to them); Tagliamonte & Denis 2008; Baron 2008 (punctuation dropped in IM/SMS). Listener ability is not the issue (Snedeker & Yuan 2008: 4-year-olds use breaks).

**Consequence.** B4 (writing carries the same meaning as speech) fails in exactly the channel natives use most. Confusable after the comma is dropped:
- `to sin miya ewo man sigo nin leso` "I watched the person who came back in the morning" vs `…, nin leso` "in the morning I watched…" (4.4 makes this depend on a pause).
- `no isi se guyi(,) nawi` "the traveller eats" vs "one who goes from home eating".
- `ewo man obu(,) gun no tofo`: with no comma, `gun` could be read inside the relative clause ("the person who comes, is, a teacher" vs "the person who comes to be a teacher").
- Topic vs possessor: `pi me(,) gun kudu` "as for you, it's big" vs "(the one) of you is big".
- Address vs subject: `en mara(,) tin obu`.
- `, yu` choice tag vs `X yu` "which X".

**Severity.** High: this is both a B4 failure in the written channel natives will use, and the one place the reference says "no word does this job" (5.3 "No word closes a relative clause").

**Direction.** Give the relative clause and the derived-word phrase an *optional* spoken closer now (so communities converge on one), make the comma mirror it rather than replace it, and remove "a pause closes it" as the only mechanism. Keep the pause as the casual form.

### F3. Grammar-word phonology: "unstressed, low and quick, but keep every consonant and full vowel" is the opposite of what happens (HIGH)

**Prediction.** Frequent function words reduce first and most (Zipf; Bybee 2001; Jurafsky et al. 2001; Bergen 2001 found reduction and cliticization in native Esperanto). The reference licenses lenition, nasalization and palatalization "inside content words only" and says "grammar words keep their consonants". No speaker community keeps a phonetic process out of its most frequent words; the licence will leak, and the words it leaks into are the ones whose contrasts are thinnest. The 61 one-syllable grammar words plus 6 stressed digits fill 67 of the 140 possible (C)V(n) syllables; nearly every contrast among the top twenty is one vowel or one glide.

Four reductions, most likely first:

(a) **Glide loss before a like vowel** (`w` before `o`/`u`, `y` before `e`/`i`), as in Japanese *wo* > *o* (Frellesvig 2010) and Mandarin, where [wu]/[u] and [ji]/[i] are not contrastive. Confusable: `wo` "after" ~ `o` focus; `wun` "that" (freq 5) ~ `un` "but"; `yen` "and" (freq 9) ~ `en` name marker (freq 4). Examples: `wo wan obu, to isi` "after he came, I went" ~ `o wan obu, …` "*he* came"; `to pume wun wan obu` "I think that he's coming" ~ `to pume, un wan obu` "I think, but he's coming"; `mole yen wede` "mother and father" ~ `mole en wede` "mother, [name] Wede".

(b) **Intervocalic voicing** (Spanish-type lenition, which applies across word boundaries and hits function words hardest: *de* > [ð] > ∅). Grammar words are almost always between vowels. Confusable: `pi` "of" ~ `bi` "very"; `pu` place ~ `bu` "it" (`pu nawi` "eating place" ~ `bu nawi` "it eats"); `pen` "we incl." ~ `ben` "that" (`pen isi` "we go" ~ `ben isi` "that goes"); `tin` command ~ `din` "we excl." (`tin obu` "come!" ~ `din obu` "we're coming"); `ke` "more" ~ `ge` result marker; `te` 5 ~ `de` "I will"; `po` 0 ~ `bo` unspecified.

(c) **Vowel centralization** in communities embedded in stress-timed languages (English, Russian, European Portuguese). Five-vowel syllable-timed systems resist this (Spanish, Japanese), so this is medium likelihood; but the reference's own description of grammar words as "low and quick" is the recipe for it. If it happens, every onset consonant carries 3–5 grammar meanings: `me` you / `mu` not / `min` Q / `mon` at / `man` rel; `to` I / `tan` this / `tun` to / `tin` command; `wi` even / `wan` he / `we` all / `wo` after / `wun` that; `la` / `le` / `li` / `lu`; `pi` / `pen` / `pu`; `bu` / `ben` / `bi` / `ba` / `bo`. `me nagu` "you see" ~ `mu nagu` "(I) don't see" is the one that breaks communication.

(d) **Final -n loss** (via nasal vowel, which the reference already allows): **harmless**. The designer left every CV counterpart of a CVn grammar word empty (`min`→`mi`, `gun`→`gu`, `wan`→`wa`, `tan`→`ta`, `pen`→`pe`, `kon`→`ko` …: none exists). This is good work and should be kept as an explicit constraint when new grammar words are added.

**Evidence.** As above; plus Bergen 2001 (cliticization, reduction in L1 Esperanto); Modern Hebrew natives dropping the pharyngeals their input nominally had (Bolozky 1997).

**Consequence.** B2 ("words are hard to mishear") already fails for the pairs in (a) and (b), before any change; the changes make it worse. Meaning breaks in (a) and (c); (b) is mostly rescued by slot (`pi` takes a noun, `bi` a quality) except `tin/din` and `pu/bu`.

**Severity.** High by impact; (a) and (b) are likely within three generations in at least some communities, (c) less so.

**Direction.** (1) Audit grammar words in pairs by *slot*, not just by form: two words that can stand in the same position should differ by more than a vowel or a glide. The worst offenders are `wun/un`, `wo/o`, `yen/en`, `tin/din`, `me/mu`. (2) Let grammar words carry stress when contrastive (`o`, `mu`, `me` need it anyway). (3) Replace "grammar words keep their consonants" (a norm speakers can't obey) with a design constraint: no grammar-word contrast may rest on a process the reference calls a harmless variant.

### F4. `pi` carries too much, and it will erode (MEDIUM)

**Prediction.** `pi` (freq 8) is possession, topic (`pi X,`), label/ordinal (`dili pi ka` Wednesday), part-of (`fi pi ka`), "own" (`pi nen`), "whose" (`pi ewo yu`), dates (`dunan pi yawu, dili pi mape`), "last" (`pi fugi`), and the name of the language. A genitive linker this frequent cliticizes and elides (Mandarin *de* is routinely dropped: 我妈; Spanish *de* > *'e*; English *o'clock*, *cup o' tea*) and is later renewed by a fresh word. `apu pi we` > *apupiwe* > *apupwe* within a generation is the obvious first case.

**Consequence.** The count/label distinction rests on `pi` alone: `dili oma` "seven days" vs `dili pi oma` "Sunday / day 7"; `dunan yawu` "ten months" vs `dunan pi yawu` "October"; `gimu ka` "three times" vs `gimu pi ka` "the third time". When `pi` goes to [p] or ∅, the calendar and ordinal system merge with counting. That is a B9 break in a system people use daily. Possession survives (order and context carry it, as in Mandarin without *de*).

**Severity.** Medium (likely; breaks a daily subsystem; repairable).

**Direction.** Don't let the ordinal/label reading depend on an unstressed one-syllable linker. Use order (number before noun for labels: `ka dili`, which the quantity-reading grammar leaves free, since place words only follow digits) or a stressed word. Reduce `pi`'s jobs: topic could take its own word.

### F5. Two-syllable operators will truncate onto one-syllable grammar words (MEDIUM)

**Prediction.** As the two-syllable operators grammaticalize they will lose their unstressed second syllable (*baimbai > bai*; *going to > gonna*; Bybee et al. 1994 on form reduction with grammaticalization). Their stressed first syllables are existing grammar words:

| Operator | Truncates to | Collides with | Same slot? |
|---|---|---|---|
| `manni` usually | `man` | `man` relative "who" | yes: both directly before a verb after a noun. `ewo man niba` "the person who walks" ~ `ewo manni niba` "the person usually walks" |
| `konsin` at least | `kon` | `kon` confidence | yes: both take a number. `konsin te` "at least 5" ~ `kon te` "5 in 6, very likely" |
| `mesun` each | `me` | `me` you | partly (`guyi mesun` "each house") |
| `wanme` seen | `wan` | `wan` he/she | partly (`wanme wan obu` > `wan wan obu`, which the doubling rule reads as emphasis) |
| `inwun` told | `in` | `in` some | weakly |
| `wemen` inferred | `we` | `we` all | weakly |
| `donon`, `buni` | `don`, `bu` | `don` if, `bu` it | no: sentence-final after a pause |

**Consequence.** `manni`/`man` and `konsin`/`kon` are meaning-breaking: restrictive relative vs habitual statement; "at least N" vs "N in 6". The others are rescued by position.

**Severity.** Medium.

**Direction.** When a two-syllable operator is built, its first syllable must not be a grammar word with a compatible slot. `manni` and `konsin` are the two to rename (`konsin` is transparently `kon` + `sin`, which guarantees the clip).

### F6. The imperative requires `tin`; bare verbs will be commands anyway (MEDIUM)

**Prediction.** Children's first directives are bare verbs with imperative intonation; the bare stem is the imperative in nearly every language (Aikhenvald 2010). `tin` will be dropped from ordinary commands and kept as an emphatic or formal form (`tin mu` "don't" may survive as a unit, as prohibitives tend to be marked). The reference's claim that bare `obu.` is a statement "(someone) comes" will not be a reading natives have.

**Evidence.** Aikhenvald 2010; Tok Pisin, Haitian, Bislama imperatives are the bare verb; Hudson Kam & Newport (an optional particle goes categorical: here, to "never" in commands).

**Consequence.** Statement vs command rests on intonation, which the reference says "carries only intonation and emotion" (fine), so writing needs `!`, which the reference says "adds nothing". Natives will make `!` meaningful; the only loss is the reference's claim. Confusable in writing without `!`: `obu.` come / (someone) comes; `me nawi` you eat / eat!

**Severity.** Medium: the design fights an acquisition universal at the point children learn first; the meaning shift is small.

**Direction.** Accept the bare-verb command with intonation and `!`; keep `tin` as the precise, unambiguous form (B3) and the form of prohibitions.

### F7. Manner between verb and object (`nawi yobe pasu`) will move after the object (MEDIUM)

**Prediction.** The verb and its object bond (Tomlin 1986); in SVO languages manner adverbs sit before the verb or after the object, not between (English *eat food quickly*; Mandarin manner before V). Children fix verb–object adjacency very early (Hirsh-Pasek & Golinkoff 1996). Speakers will say `nawi pasu yobe` for "eat quickly", which the reference reads as "eat fast food".

**Consequence.** The attributive/manner reading after the object becomes ambiguous (`oyi musu dibon` "say things loudly" / "say loud things"). Meaning degrades rather than breaks; the clear forms (`man` for attribution) remain.

**Severity.** Medium.

**Direction.** Let manner follow the object as the default; `man` (or the predicate forms) marks the attributive reading when it matters. Don't assign the post-object slot a reading that is the less frequent one.

### F8. Existential `gun` by position will not survive subject drop (MEDIUM)

**Prediction.** 4.5/4.7 make a subjectless `gun` mean "there is" only at a sentence or clause start, while "(it) is X" must keep its subject. Pro-drop generalizes: in null-subject languages the copula drops its subject like any other verb (Spanish *es dinero* "it's money"; Mandarin 是钱). Children will say `gun nimo` for "it's money" by analogy, and the existential will be renewed from `ibe` "have" (have > existential: Heine & Kuteva; French *il y a*, Mandarin 有, Haitian *gen*, Tok Pisin *i gat*) or from a locative (`mon`).

**Consequence.** `mu gun pasu` "there is no food" vs "(it) is not food" merge until the renewal lands. Medium: a frequent construction; harmless once `ibe` takes over.

**Direction.** Give existence a word (or bless `ibe` for it) rather than a position.

### F9. Unmarked recipient + "a determiner belongs to the preceding noun" is a garden path (LOW)

**Prediction.** `to iko wan ben` "I told that-him" and the required repair `to iko ben tun wan` is the kind of rule children replace: either `tun` becomes obligatory on recipients, or `neke` "give" serializes into a dative marker (give > dative: Mandarin 给, Thai *hây*, Saramaccan *da*). Harmless; B8 cost.

**Direction.** Accept `tun` as the normal recipient marker when the thing is a pronoun or determiner; say so.

### F10. Pitch is the only cue that separates content words from strings of grammar words (LOW)

**Prediction.** Dozens of content words are exact concatenations of grammar words, distinguished only by the first-syllable pitch rise: `obu` come = `o bu`, `oyi` say = `o yi`, `wanme` seen = `wan me`, `yidu` take = `yi du`, `inwun` told = `in wun`, `muwo` happen = `mu wo`, `tanbu` cheap = `tan bu`, `lafi` understand = `la fi`, `lugo` know = `lu go`, `penso` smoke = `pen so`, `gewan` trust = `ge wan`, `sunbu` count = `sun bu`, `feme` feel = `fe me`, `goyu` sit = `go yu`, `donbo` squeeze = `don bo`, `ladon` cup = `la don`. Fixed initial stress is an excellent segmentation cue (Vroomen, Tuomainen & de Gelder 1998 for Finnish; Jusczyk, Cutler & Redanz 1993) and infants also use transitional probabilities (Saffran, Aslin & Newport 1996), so children will learn it. The risk is production: a pitch-only accent with steady declination is being flattened by young urban speakers of Japanese (*heibanka*), and the reference forbids the usual reinforcements (length, loudness).

**Consequence.** Misparses only where the grammar-word string is itself a valid parse (`wan wanme obu` / `wan wan me obu`); syntax rescues almost all. Low, but it argues against the "pitch only, never longer" stipulation (see N4).

**Direction.** Let stress be realized however speakers realize it; audit new content words against grammar-word strings the way digits are already audited (section 2's last rule).

### F11. The reference's "never" rules on person and modality will be overturned, harmlessly (LOW)

Each of these is a near-universal path; the reference forbids the endpoint on paper and natives will reach it anyway:

| Rule in reference | Predicted change | Path and evidence |
|---|---|---|
| `lanno` "never means you" | `lanno` > polite "you" | title/honorific > 2nd-person pronoun: Spanish *usted* < *vuestra merced*, Portuguese *você*, Dutch *u*, Malay *Bapak/Ibu* (Heine & Song 2010, 2012). Standalone address `lanno, so nun …` is the breeding ground. Communities inside T/V cultures (C2) will do it first. |
| `me` "is only ever the listener and is never generic" | generic `me` | 2nd person > impersonal in English, French, German, Spanish, Hebrew, Mandarin (Kitagawa & Lehrer 1990). `go` survives as "people". |
| `de` "only for the speaker's own commitment" | `de` > future for all persons | commissive > future (English *will* < "want", *shall* < "owe"); children overgeneralize person restrictions. |
| "must in the sense of probably is `kon te`" | `keya` > epistemic "must be"; `lenu` > "could be" | deontic > epistemic is universal (Sweetser 1990); children acquire deontic first, epistemic by ~4 (Papafragou 1998). |
| "there are no articles" | `tan` > definite article; `fi` > indefinite | demonstrative > article (Greenberg 1978); "one" > indefinite article (Heine 1997). Postnominal position is fine (Romanian, Scandinavian). |
| `momu` "want" is a verb | `momu` (or `isi`) > future auxiliary | want/go > future (Bybee et al. 1994); see F1. |
| `mu mu` "is always emphasis" | fine as stated; but `mu … fa` / `mu … musu` reinforcement | Jespersen's cycle; Brazilian Portuguese *não … não* (Schwenter 2005). Stage III (loss of `mu`) is beyond three generations. |

**Consequence.** None of these breaks meaning more than the world's languages already tolerate; each precise form stays in the reference (B3). The cost is only credibility: a reference that says "never" about things natives then do will be read as prescriptive, which is what B9 forbids.

**Direction.** Replace "never" with "in the reference variety"; list the expected extensions as such.

### F12. No "yes" word, and `mm` means "heard, not agreed" (LOW)

**Prediction.** "No" is among children's first 50 words (Nelson 1973; Bloom 1970); a child at the one-word stage cannot echo a verb it hasn't parsed. Echo-answer languages all keep a negator usable alone (`mu` already is) and most keep a "yes" particle too (Welsh *ie*; Finnish *kyllä*; Mandarin 对/嗯; Holmberg 2016, *The Syntax of Yes and No*). Natives will make one from `feko` "true", `upe` "good", `gun` "is", `tayu` "agree", or `mm`. The last is the most likely in speech (English *mm-hm*), which collides with the reference's "`mm` says you heard".

**Consequence.** Small B1 risk during the transition (`mm` = heard vs agreed). Harmless after.

**Direction.** Don't define `mm` against agreement; expect a yes word and give it a home (N4).

### F13. The 7-point confidence scale, evidentials, half-steps, senary (LOW)

**Prediction.** Natural epistemic/evidential systems are 2–5 terms (Aikhenvald 2004); optional ones are discourse strategies and decay. `kon ka` lexicalizes as "maybe" (`konka`), `kon da`/`kon te` as "probably", `kon po` as a counterfactual marker (via `don kon po`); `kon fi`, `kon sa`, half-steps and `kon bo` go unused. Of the source words only `inwun` "told" is likely to live, as "apparently" (hearsay is the one evidential that keeps surfacing in European languages: German *soll*, French conditional). The senary system (`senali`, `nobi`…`genwi`, the unit roots) will be used by nobody not told to.

**Consequence.** Harmless. N4 over-definition.

### F14. Writing habits (LOW on their own; the comma is F2)

- Names will be capitalized within a generation (every Latin-script language does it; L3 "follows the international standard" argues the same). Once the capital marks the name, `en` may drop in writing, then split from speech (B4): written *Mara*, spoken `en mara` > `mara`. Low.
- `?` and `!` will be meaningful (F1, F6), contradicting "add nothing". Harmless.
- `ba`, `yo`, `genso` compete with emoji and `/s`; `genso` may survive in text as the native "/s". Harmless.
- Spacing follows segmentation: `no tofo` > *notofo*, `gi tan` > *gitan*, `mon kemu yu` > *kemuyu* "where", `wan la` > *wanla*; the reference's "keeps its spaces until a fused form changes sound" is unenforceable and nobody will apply it (Bergen 2001: pronominal cliticization appears in natives' Esperanto).
- `₆` will never be typed; `_6` maybe. Harmless.

### F15. Reference tracking: `yi`, `sun`, `nen` (LOW)

**Prediction.** `sun` (logophoric, freq 1) is dropped; reported-speech reference becomes as ambiguous as in English. `yi` (obviative) goes one of two ways: dropped to a plain "the other one", or, if Senghas & Coppola's pattern holds, *strengthened* by children into a categorical switch-reference device because the language otherwise leaves third-person reference underdetermined (no gender, no agreement, optional plural). I'd bet on strengthening in the first native cohort and simplification later. `nen` reflexive/intensifier and `nen nen` reciprocal are on well-attested paths (König & Siemund 2000; Heine & Kuteva: reflexive > reciprocal) and are robust. The Principle-B style rule ("a pronoun never means the subject of its own clause") is acquired late in every language that has it (Chien & Wexler 1990) and children will say `to miya to` for a while; harmless.

### F16. Sound changes in content words (LOW)

The five-vowel system is the most stable there is (Crothers 1978; Maddieson 1984): no vowel mergers expected. Likely within three generations and harmless: k/g > [tʃ]/[dʒ] before i/e (already licensed); final high-vowel devoicing after voiceless consonants (Japanese pattern: `pasu` > [pasɯ̥]), which quietly reintroduces closed syllables; nasal vowels for Vn. Later and worth watching: f > h > ∅ (Spanish *filius* > *hijo*; Japanese p > ɸ > h) would empty the f-series (`fe`, `fa`, `fu`, `fon`, `fi`), colliding `fa` "none" with `a`. Beyond three generations.

### F17. Intensifiers and discourse words churn (LOW)

`bi` "very" will bleach and be replaced every generation (Ito & Tagliamonte 2003; Tagliamonte 2008: *very > really > so*); candidates here are `feko` "true", `kudu` "big", `konke` "strong", `fafin` "dead". `gi tan` "so", `un` "but", `a`, `eda` "now" become discourse markers. Harmless and expected; the reference should not fix `bi` as *the* intensifier.

### F18. Serial verbs are the seedbed of the next grammar (LOW, positive)

V+V with role by order is exactly where creoles grow new grammar: `neke` give > dative (F9), `yidu` take > instrumental/object marker (Yoruba *fi*, Mandarin 把), `oyi` say > complementizer competing with `wun` (Ewe *bé*, Bislama *se*), `piyi` stay > progressive competing with `le` (Tok Pisin *i stap*), `sigo` return > "again", `lewe` become > inchoative, `muge` do > causative, `se` from > passive agent (`wan gun kuwi se wabun` "protected by law"; the reference has no agent phrase for `gun`+V, and this fills it). None of these collides with an existing grammar word. This is the design's best B9 feature: it already has the shapes natives would have built.

## 4. Unstable vs robust; what fights acquisition

**Robust (will survive three generations in recognisable form):**
- Five vowels, (C)V(n), initial stress, no inflection, no agreement: nothing to erode but the function words.
- CVn grammar words with their CV counterparts left empty (F3d). Keep this as a rule.
- SVO, head-initial, N-A-Num-Dem (Universal 20's most common postnominal order), relative clause after the noun, operators before their scope, preverbal negation with scope by position.
- Inclusive/exclusive `pen`/`din` (stable wherever it exists; Tok Pisin *yumi*/*mipela*).
- Wh-in-situ with `yu`; sentence-final stance particles; initial question particle surviving at least as the tag.
- Transparent base-10 numerals with order-based tens (`yawu te` 15 / `te yawu` 50): acquired faster than English numerals.
- Reduplication for intensity/plurality: children do it unprompted; creoles keep it productive.
- `ke … se` comparison (ablative comparative), `mon` as the one locative, `pi` possession (apart from its load), `man` relatives, the derivation markers `no`/`ge`/`du`/`pu` as prefixes on frequent bases.
- Serial verbs (F18).

**Unstable (will change within one or two generations):**
- Everything optional: `gun` with qualities, `sin`/`le` as aspect rather than tense, `tin`, initial `min`, `la`, `nun` placement, `kon`, source words (F1, F13).
- The pause/comma as grammar (F2).
- Glide-only and voicing-only contrasts among grammar words (F3).
- `pi` for ordinals, labels and dates (F4).
- `manni`, `konsin` (F5).
- Manner between V and O (F7); positional existential (F8); unmarked recipient (F9).
- `lanno` ≠ you, `me` ≠ generic, `de` speaker-only, `keya` deontic-only, no articles, no yes-word, `mm` ≠ yes (F11, F12).
- Two clock styles, both "always understood"; digit-reading vs quantity-reading of the same string (years, labels ≥100): one will win per community.
- `bi` as the intensifier (F17).

**Design choices that fight acquisition at its core:**
1. "Optional when precision matters" as the organizing principle (F1). Children don't do optional.
2. Grammar carried by pauses and no closer for relative clauses (F2).
3. Commands needing a particle; statements as the bare default (F6).
4. No yes-word; echo answers only (F12).
5. "Grammar words keep their consonants / are unstressed, low and quick" (F3): asks speakers to keep the most frequent words the least reduced.
6. Topic-tracked `wan`/`yi` and the own-clause pronoun rule (F15): late-acquired, discourse-dependent.
7. Confidence in sixths with half-steps (F13): numeracy, not grammar.

Choices that *help* acquisition and should be protected: invariant words, SVO with role by position, N-A-Num-Dem, transparent numerals, reduplication, final particles, clusivity, the empty-CV rule.

## 5. Where the design over-defines what natives will decide (N4)

Each line is a decision the reference makes that real use will make instead. Deleting it costs nothing now; the add-back trigger is given.

| Over-definition | Add-back trigger |
|---|---|
| "`?` and `!` add nothing"; "capitals carry no meaning"; names in lowercase | If a community actually keeps names lowercase and `!` meaningless, document it then. |
| "A phrase said fast keeps its spaces until a fused form actually changes sound" | Never: spelling follows segmentation; nobody can test "actually changes sound". |
| "Stress is pitch only; it does not make the syllable longer"; "the rhythm is syllable-timed"; declination described in detail | If two communities' prosody stops being mutually intelligible. |
| "Grammar words keep their consonants"; harmless variants "inside content words only" | Never as a norm; replace with the design constraint in F3. |
| "There are no words for yes and no"; "`mm` says you heard, an echo says you understood" | Never; natives will pick a yes word. |
| "`lanno` never means you"; "`me` is never generic"; "`de` is only for the speaker" | If the extended uses actually cause misunderstanding in a corpus. |
| "`sin` and `le` don't combine"; "`mu mu` is always emphasis" | If natives combine them with a meaning the reference needs to describe. |
| "If two stance words occur, `min` comes last"; the full 14-slot template with "`genso` or `ba` may come before the particle" | If a real corpus shows a different order; the template is a description that belongs in a grammar *of* native speech, not ahead of it. |
| "Both clock styles are always understood"; labels under 100 quantity-read, over 100 digit-read; years digit by digit | Keep the ISO rule (L3); drop the parallel styles and let each community's style stand. |
| `kon` half-steps (`kon te mimi te`), `kon bo`, exact percentages with `pasentu` | If anyone says one in speech. |
| The senary calendar, `senali`, `nobi`…`genwi`, the 19 senary unit roots | If a community adopts it. (This is a doghouse part: its only source is another designed part.) |
| "Noun + noun is used only for gender and for the compounds in compounds.tsv" | Never as a closed list; natives will compound freely (`tiwu futa` is the model). Keep `pi` as the precise form. |
| Fixed greetings and routines (10.2) | Never; routines are the first thing a community makes its own. |
| "A quote is its own sentence with its own speech-act particle" | If natives actually do otherwise. |

## 6. How to measure B9 ("it survives its speakers changing it")

B9 is a guarantee, so it needs tests that can fail. The measure is *survival of meaning under change*, not survival of forms. Six methods, cheapest first; each with a pass mark. Methods M1–M5 can be run now on the reference and the UDHR text; M6 needs speakers.

**M1. Erosion simulation (automatic, hours).** Apply each attested reduction to grammar.tsv, numbers.tsv and lexicon.tsv as a string rewrite, one at a time and then all together: (R1) final -n > ∅; (R2) w > ∅ before o/u, y > ∅ before e/i; (R3) intervocalic p t k > b d g; (R4) unstressed vowels > ə in grammar words; (R5) two-syllable operators > first syllable; (R6) k/g > tʃ/dʒ before i/e, then > s. For each, list new homophone pairs and classify by whether the two can occupy the same slot in the 4.1 template. Weight by frequency. **Pass:** per single rule, no new homophone pair between two grammar words of frequency ≥ 3 that share a slot; no content word of tier 4–5 merges with another of tier ≥ 3; and under all rules together, no more than 5 such grammar pairs. (Today the design fails R2 on `wun/un`, `yen/en`, `wo/o` and R5 on `manni/man`, `konsin/kon`.)

**M2. Deletion test on a corpus (readers, days).** Take the UDHR translation plus the lesson sentences. Produce variants with one optional class removed throughout: all commas; `gun` before qualities; `la`; `sin`; `tin`; initial `min`; `nun`; `kon` and source words; `man` where a bare noun+word would do. Readers who know the reference but have not seen the English back-translate; score each sentence for truth-conditional change (who holds the right, who bears the duty, polarity, tense, number). **Pass:** ≥ 95% of sentences unchanged for any single class; ≥ 90% with commas *and* `gun` removed together; and the mean repair (words needed to restore the original meaning) ≤ 1 per 20 sentences. An LLM judge may be used for a first pass only if calibrated on 50 human-scored sentences with ≥ 90% agreement.

**M3. Iterated learning (lab, weeks).** Build a 40–60-word mini-language that keeps the design's structural bets (optional `gun`, optional `sin`, `tin`, count vs label by `pi`, recipient by order, relative clause closed by pause) and run 10-generation diffusion chains with adult learners (Kirby, Cornish & Smith 2008 *PNAS*; Smith & Wonnacott 2010 on regularization through iteration); if a child-study partner is available, a Hudson Kam & Newport-style single-generation study with 5–7-year-olds on the optional markers. Score, at generation 10, whether each core distinction is still *expressed somehow* (any form counts): negation, question, past/non-past, count vs ordinal, recipient vs thing, command vs statement, attribution vs predication. **Pass:** each distinction survives in ≥ 80% of chains. A distinction that is lost in most chains is one the design carries in a form learners can't keep; redesign that form, not the learners.

**M4. Innovation-collision audit (desk, a day).** Take the grammaticalizations predicted in F1, F5, F11, F18 (future from `momu`/`isi`/`buntu`/`de`; `gun` > passive; `tan`/`fi` > articles; `lanno` > polite you; `me` generic; `keya` epistemic; `neke` > dative; `se` > agent; `oyi` > complementizer; `piyi` > progressive; `ibe` > existential; `manni` > `man`; `konsin` > `kon`). For each: does a precise form still exist in the reference (B3)? Does the new form collide with an existing grammar word in the same slot? **Pass:** zero high-severity collisions (meaning changes in a frequent construction); every medium one has a documented repair that doesn't require speakers to be corrected.

**M5. Channel stress test (readers, days).** Render the UDHR text as natives will text it: no commas, no capitals, no `?`/`!`, frequent grammar words fused with neighbours as in F14, and the F3(a)/(b) reductions applied as spellings. Comprehension as in M2. **Pass:** ≥ 90% article-level fidelity (right-holder, duty-bearer, polarity). This is the B4 half of B9.

**M6. Once there are speakers (longitudinal).** (a) Cross-cohort intelligibility: standard comprehension tasks between the reference variety and second- and third-generation speech, using dialect-intelligibility methods (Gooskens 2007); **pass:** ≥ 90% comprehension both ways, with no "correction" of speakers allowed before or during the test. (b) A "never-list" tracker: count the reference's "X never means Y" rules that natives have overturned. This is not pass/fail; it is the add-back heuristic in reverse: if after one native generation *none* has been overturned, the speakers are not native or the community is being policed, and B9 is being faked. Expect several.

What B9 should *not* be measured by: stability of forms, conformity to the reference, or the absence of change. A language whose natives change nothing has no natives.

## 7. What works (said plainly)

- The language is already in the state three generations of natives would push a pidgin or a designed language toward: invariant words, SVO, preverbal particles, serial verbs, reduplication, final particles, zero copula tolerated. Drift has less distance to travel here than in Esperanto, and Esperanto's natives stayed intelligible (Lindstedt 2006).
- The empty-CV rule for CVn grammar words is a real, deliberate robustness feature and nearly unique among designed languages.
- Five vowels and thirteen consonants leave huge room for harmless allophony; the inventory itself will not drift into mergers for centuries.
- Transparent numerals, clusivity, wh-in-situ and N-A-Num-Dem are all on the acquisition-friendly side of the typological evidence.
- The reference mostly says what the *default* reading is when a marker is absent. That is the right instinct; F1 asks only that it stop calling the marked forms optional.
