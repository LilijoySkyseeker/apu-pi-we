## 1. Inventory (detail)

- **Roots/lexicon:** 524 native roots + 23 loans, tiered 1–5 by frequency (76/237/176/43/15 at tiers 1–5). Reasonable size — smaller than most natural-language core vocabularies, and the design leans on 52 established compounds plus open derivation (§5.4: no/ge/du/pu) rather than minting new roots. This is the part of the inventory that looks *right-sized*.
- **Grammar words:** 61 one-syllable function words (9 speech-act, 10 pronoun, 9 relation, 8 phrase-operator, 8 linker, 7 determiner, 4 derivation, 2 aspect, 2 degree, 1 copula, 1 name-marker).
- **Two-syllable operators:** 15 more function words, stressed like content words, that sit outside the above table and are (inconsistently) catalogued in `lexicon.tsv`: genso, wanme, inwun, wemen, konsin, buntu, tason, nedin, mesun, manni, buni, donon, lanno, anko, nuwen.
- **Numbers:** 10 digits, 2 place words (yawu, loki), 3 group words (munno, tonen, siwin), 3 signs (mimi, guti, unun), and a **parallel senary (base-6) subsystem**: 3 senary group words (nobi 6, miko 36, niyu 216) + 6 senary powers (yiyo, gife, yuli, kufe, bafo, genwi) = 9 roots, plus 20 dedicated native unit roots (time, mass, charge, temperature, angle, frequency, force, pressure, energy, power, voltage, resistance, capacitance, inductance, magnetic flux density, temperature scale, information, entity-count, particle mass, length) — i.e. a hand-built SI-equivalent, all at tier 1, pointing at `measurement.md` (out of scope here). That's **29 roots (≈5% of the entire lexicon)** for a measurement system that duplicates the borrowed-SI-name system §9.3 already specifies as the actual practice ("Measurements use borrowed international unit names").
- **Rules:** ≈176 top-level bullets/numbered items across 14 sections of `language.md`, with several bullets bundling multiple sub-cases (§4.6 alone has 8 distinct comma-placement rules in one bullet list). A realistic count of discrete "facts a learner must retain" is ≈220–260, not counting the word lists.
- **Optional/grammaticalized systems**, matched against the task's example list — all ten are present in some form: speech-act particles (8), confidence/epistemic scale (kon × 8 graded terms + fractional combination + pasentu + "don't know" + a counterfactual special-case with don), evidentiality/source (3: wanme/inwun/wemen), stance (4 dedicated + explicitly open-ended "other words used in this position are stance words too"), politeness (nun/nun nun), respect title (lanno + ordering rules), inclusive/exclusive "we" (pen/din), logophoric (sun) and obviative (yi) third-person reference, aspect (sin/le/buntu/manni with frame-dependent readings), and productive doubling with **six lexically-specific, non-compositional meanings** rather than one general rule.

## 3. Arbitrary rules and unpredictable interactions

1. **Doubling is advertised as one rule, but is six.** Principle 5 states "doubling widens or strengthens any word" and gives six examples, each with a *different, non-compositional* meaning: mu mu = emphatic not (and the text goes out of its way to say "never not-not" — an explicit patch to stop the general rule from producing the logical reading); nun nun = more deference; yo yo = emergency (a different speech-act, not "more warning"); genso genso = "the whole turn is not literal" (scope change, not intensity); nen nen = reciprocal "each other" (a different grammatical function — self → reciprocal — not intensification at all); feko feko = "exactly so". A learner cannot predict nen nen from nen + "doubling intensifies," because it doesn't intensify; it changes meaning category. This is the opposite of B7 ("predictable... with little to memorise").
2. **"gun" ellipsis has a carved-out exception.** §4.5 states anything context supplies may be dropped, then immediately carves out: dropping gun's subject is fine only in specific slot positions (start of sentence/clause after an opener or don/gi/nin/wo/ya/wun), otherwise it changes meaning ("there is" vs needing an explicit subject for "(it) is X"). This is a rule whose entire content is "the general rule doesn't apply here."
3. **Determiner attachment needs a patch word.** §5.2: a determiner right after a noun/pronoun attaches to it; to give a *recipient* a free-standing determiner, the grammar requires inserting tun purely to block the default attachment (to iko ben tun wan vs to iko wan ben). This is a rule that exists only to undo another rule's default.
4. **Bare noun+word is genuinely ambiguous, by the language's own admission** (§4.2: "may be a description or a statement. Context decides"), and the fix is a second, longer "clear form" (gun/man) that a learner must also learn and that a speaker must remember to reach for. Two ways to say the same two things, with no rule for which to pick beyond "when it matters."
5. **The comma (pause) rules are a dense, interacting patch set.** §4.6 lists 8 distinct syntactic positions that require a comma, precisely because the position-only, case-free grammar (principle 2) otherwise creates attachment ambiguity (relative-clause scope, which conjunct a trailing clause belongs to, recipient vs. thing). §4.4 adds: relative clauses attach to the nearest noun, time/reason/condition clauses attach differently depending on sentence-initial vs. sentence-final position, and closing a relative clause has no dedicated word — only a pause does it, "along with every relative clause still open" (so one comma can silently close several nested clauses at once). A learner cannot derive comma placement from "heads first, position carries role" (principle 2); it is a second grammar bolted on to rescue the first.
6. **sin is lexically one word with three readings chosen by discourse frame** (done / had-done / will-have-done), and is explicitly stated not to combine with le, so "was doing" requires a different construction (past frame + le) than "is doing" (le alone) or "did" (sin alone). This is workable for a fluent speaker but is three memorised facts, not one rule, and the sample text's 18 uses of sin can't by inspection confirm which reading applies without tracking the surrounding frame — exactly the kind of context-tracking cost B7 is supposed to minimise.
7. **Homonymy with grammaticalized functions across categories**: bi "very" (degree) sits one vowel away from bo (UNSPEC determiner/pronoun) and bu ("it"), and ba is also listed in principle 6 ("ba and bi for emphasis") *and* separately as the EXCLAIM speech-act particle in §7.1 — i.e. the same word is pulled into two stated roles (general social-pressure emphasis, and a specific speech act), and its practical effect on a sentence's reading differs by which role applies, decided only by position/context.

## 4. What the goal requires that looks missing (brief)

- **No shown cost/benefit trace for individual rules.** C1 states costs are "minimised together" and that nothing arbitrary is kept "unless it pays for itself in everyday saving," but no document in scope shows this accounting being done — not for the senary/unit system, not for the six doubling meanings, not for the 19 minimal-pair grammar words. The design method the goal names isn't demonstrated anywhere visible.
- **No frequency evidence behind the lexicon's tier numbers.** Tiers 1–5 are asserted per entry with no stated corpus or method (vocabulary-method.md is out of scope here, so this can't be checked from the allowed files) — B8 brevity claims can't be independently verified from what's given.
- **No minimal/core subset or staged grammar for B10 ("any adult can learn it").** `learn.md` sequences lessons well (credit below), but `language.md` presents all ~220–260 rules and 10 optional subsystems as one undifferentiated reference; there's no marked "this much is enough to be understood" floor distinct from "this is the full precise system," even though principle 7 gestures at exactly that distinction ("a precise way always exists, but it need not always be used").
- **No sunset/review mechanism for underused machinery**, despite B9 ("survives its speakers changing it"). A living design that explicitly expects to be changed by use would benefit from flagging low-uptake machinery (confidence scale, senary units) as provisional rather than fixed reference grammar.

## 5. Size compared with natural and designed languages

- **Esperanto**: Zamenhof's original Fundamenta Gramatiko is **16 rules** total (articles, nouns, adjectives, numerals, pronouns, verbs, adverbs, prepositions, pronunciation, accent, compounds, negatives, direction, the multi-purpose preposition, borrowing, elision), with ~900 roots and productive affixation doing the rest. [Wikiversity: Esperanto/Grammar Rules](https://en.wikiversity.org/wiki/Esperanto/Grammar_Rules); [UCSD: The Sixteen Rules](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~dkjordan/eo/Fundamento.html). This language's core reference grammar runs to an order of magnitude more discrete rules (≈220–260) than Esperanto's canonical 16, even though both are designed-for-regularity languages.
- **Toki Pona**: deliberately minimal, **120–137 words** total (the 2014 guidebook's 120/123, the 2021 dictionary's 137 "major" words, up to 187 counting variants), with grammar compressed to the point that tense, evidentiality, politeness and obviation are simply not grammaticalized — left to context or paraphrase. [sona.pona.la: How many words does Toki Pona have?](https://sona.pona.la/wiki/How_many_words_does_Toki_Pona_have); [Glossika: Toki Pona ... 137 words](https://ai.glossika.com/blog/toki-pona). This language's ≈614 simple word forms is ~4.5–5× Toki Pona's count, and — unlike Toki Pona — it grammaticalizes exactly the categories (evidentiality, stance, politeness, obviation, confidence) Toki Pona chose to leave to use, which is the live tension with this language's own N4 ("define everything... come from real use").
- **Natural-language baseline for "how much vocabulary does everyday use need"**: frequency studies put **95% coverage of casual spoken discourse at roughly 2,000–3,000 word families** (the General Service List tradition; Nation's vocabulary-size research). [lextutor.ca: Nation & Waring](https://www.lextutor.ca/research/nation_waring_97.html); [Nation, "How Large a Vocabulary is Needed..."](https://www.scienceguide.nl/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/nation-2006-vocabulary.pdf). By that yardstick this language's 524 native roots + 52 compounds is reasonably lean on the *content-word* axis — the size problem here is concentrated in grammar machinery and the senary/unit side-system, not in the root inventory.

## Findings (part / evidence / problem / severity / direction)

1. **Minimal-pair grammar words.** Evidence: 19 of 61 one-syllable grammar words (31%) cluster into 3–4-way sets differing only by vowel — ba/bi/bo/bu (EXCLAIM/VERY/UNSPEC/IT), la/le/li/lu (PLURAL/ONGOING/FOR/OR), ya/yi/yo/yu (BEFORE/OBVIATIVE/WARN/WH), we/wi/wo (ALL/EVEN/AFTER), ge/gi/go (RESULT/BECAUSE/GENERIC), fa/fe/fu (NONE/ALSO/LIKE) — all unstressed, uncushioned by case or agreement. Problem: directly undercuts the guarantee B2 ("words are hard to mishear"); a vowel slip swaps grammatical categories, not just near-synonyms. Severity: **high**. Direction: simplify — redesign the vowel inventory of the highest-frequency grammar words so same-consonant sets don't share a CV skeleton, or add a coda/consonant contrast to the highest-traffic members (la, le, fe, we).

2. **Parallel senary number + native SI-equivalent unit system.** Evidence: 9 senary-specific number roots + 20 dedicated unit roots, all tier 1, ~5% of the whole lexicon, 0 attestations in the sample text, while §9.3 already specifies borrowed international unit names as the actual practice. Problem: a second, unused precision system, pointing outside the files available for this review (measurement.md) — exactly the "precision machinery nobody needs often" N4 warns against. Severity: **high**. Direction: delete the native unit roots in favour of the already-specified loan-name system; keep the senary numeral words only if measurement.md shows a concrete, frequent use this review couldn't see.

3. **Doubling is one stated rule hiding six memorised meanings.** Evidence: principle 5's six examples (mu mu, nun nun, yo yo, genso genso, nen nen, feko feko) each do something different — intensify, add deference, change speech act, change scope, change grammatical function (reflexive→reciprocal), or confirm exactness — plus an explicit carve-out ("doubled mu is always emphasis and never means 'not not'") needed to stop the general rule from overgenerating. Problem: violates B7's own definition of predictable ("made and connected by rule, with little to memorise"); a learner who knows the rule still can't predict nen nen. Severity: **high**. Direction: merge/simplify — keep doubling as pure intensification (mu mu, nun nun, feko feko) and give nen nen (reciprocal) and genso genso (scope) their own non-doubled words, since they aren't instances of the same operation.

4. **Confidence/evidentiality/stance/politeness/title apparatus is unattested in a 2,714-word real sample.** Evidence: kon (8 graded terms + fractional + counterfactual case), wanme/inwun/wemen, buni/donon, nun, lanno all occur 0 times in the UDHR translation. Problem: substantial grammaticalized machinery (≈17+ words plus ordering/placement rules) whose everyday payoff can't be confirmed from available evidence, and whose cost (new slots, interaction rules, a counterfactual special case for kon+don) is paid regardless of use. Severity: **medium** (not high, because UDHR is a narrow register and this may under-sample conversational/epistemic-hedging speech — flagged, not proven). Direction: leave to use (N4) for the rarer tail (nun nun, lanno's ordering rule, the exact-fraction kon combinations) pending a multi-register frequency check; keep the base kon scale and wanme/inwun/wemen as-is if a casual-speech sample confirms real uptake.

5. **The comma/pause system is a dense patch set for a self-created ambiguity.** Evidence: 8 distinct mandatory-comma positions in §4.6, plus nearest-noun relative-clause attachment, plus "a pause... closes every relative clause still open" (one mark closing multiple nested structures), plus position-dependent attachment of time/reason clauses at sentence-start vs. sentence-end. Problem: this entire apparatus exists only because principle 2 (position-only, no case marking) otherwise can't disambiguate; it's a second grammar layered on to rescue the first, and in speech a "pause" is phonetic, not written, raising real ambiguity in spoken use that the written comma papers over. Severity: **medium**. Direction: simplify — a small number of these (e.g., closing a relative clause) could use a short dedicated closing particle instead of overloading the pause with multiple jobs.

6. **gun-ellipsis and determiner-attachment "patch" rules.** Evidence: §4.5's carve-out for dropping gun's subject only in specific slot positions; §5.2's tun inserted solely to block default determiner attachment to a recipient. Problem: rules whose entire content is "undo the default established elsewhere" — the signature of over-building by patching rather than redesigning. Severity: **medium**. Direction: simplify the underlying default (e.g., let a bare determiner after a verb always default to the thing, not the preceding noun) so the patch word becomes unnecessary.

7. **sin's frame-dependent three-way polysemy plus a combination gap.** Evidence: sin reads as done/had-done/will-have-done depending on discourse frame, sin and le explicitly don't combine, so "was doing" needs a different construction than the parallel present/future forms. Problem: three memorised facts dressed as one aspect marker; real cost given sin is actually the most-used aspect/evidentiality-adjacent word in the sample (18 occurrences). Severity: **medium**. Direction: simplify — document sin's three readings as the single most-important high-frequency irregularity to teach early (learn.md should flag it, not just language.md), rather than folding it into the general aspect section.

8. **Two overlapping data lists for grammar words.** Evidence: the 15 two-syllable "operators" named in language.md §13 (genso, wanme, lanno, etc.) are catalogued in lexicon.tsv, not grammar.tsv, despite being grammatically functional; 21 of numbers.tsv's 27 forms duplicate entries already in lexicon.tsv. Problem: not a flaw in the language itself, but it makes the grammar-vs-content-word boundary harder to audit or teach consistently from the source files, and risks the two files drifting out of sync. Severity: **low**. Direction: merge — fold the two-syllable operators into grammar.tsv (they're grammar by function), and keep numbers.tsv as the single source of truth for number forms rather than duplicating them into lexicon.tsv.

9. **Inclusive/exclusive "we" (pen/din).** Evidence: 2 extra pronouns, cross-linguistically common (B5-aligned), cheap (low ordering/interaction cost), attested at least once in the sample (din: 2). Severity: **low** — flagged only because it's one more thing to learn, but it earns its place. Direction: leave as-is.

10. **Logophoric (sun) / obviative (yi) pronouns.** Evidence: a genuinely rare category for most learners, but both are attested in the sample (yi: 12, sun: 3) doing real disambiguating work (B3). Severity: **low**. Direction: leave as-is; this is the one "exotic" system with the best evidence of earning its keep.

11. **ba/bi overload across two stated roles.** Evidence: principle 6 assigns ba and bi to general "social pressure... emphasis," while §7.1 separately gives ba a specific speech-act function (EXCLAIM) and §6.2 gives bi a specific degree function (VERY) — the same two words serve both a general category and a specific grammatical slot. Severity: **low**. Direction: simplify the stated description so each word has one named job, even if the surface form doesn't change.

12. **No shown rule-level cost accounting.** Evidence: C1's stated method ("nothing arbitrary unless it pays for itself in everyday saving") isn't demonstrated for any individual rule in the available files. Severity: **low** (a documentation/process gap more than a language defect). Direction: for a redesign pass, require a one-line justification per optional subsystem citing a frequency source, mirroring the measurement methods proposed below.

## Proposed measurement for a separate evaluation document

**Learning effort (B7):**
1. *Rule-count audit* (used here): count distinct statements/exceptions a learner must memorise beyond the stated general principles; track cumulative count per lesson in learn.md. Pass mark: no single lesson introduces more than ~15 new atomic facts (rule-of-thumb from language-pedagogy chunking, not a hard citation); total core-reference rule count should be stated and tracked over revisions rather than left open-ended.
2. *Naive-rule-follower test*: give someone (or an LLM) only the stated general principles (not the exceptions) and a held-out set of forms (new compounds, numbers, doubled words); measure what fraction they produce/parse correctly from the rule alone. Pass mark ≥90% for anything billed as "predictable... by rule" (B7); every miss is a candidate arbitrary rule.
3. *Minimal-pair/confusability audit* (used here): for every grammar word, check same-CV-skeleton neighbors differing only by vowel/single consonant; pass mark: no two high-frequency (top-20-by-use) grammar words share a skeleton with unrelated functions.
4. *Time-to-competency trial*: run volunteer learners through learn.md, measure hours to a defined competency bar (e.g., correctly produce/understand N held-out sentences spanning all 14 lessons). Needs a comparison benchmark (e.g., published Esperanto or Toki Pona onboarding-time figures) gathered at evaluation time, not asserted here.

**Everyday effort (B8):**
1. *Corpus brevity metric*: translate a multi-register benchmark set (not just UDHR — add casual dialogue, narrative, instructions) and measure words/syllables per proposition against a sample of typologically diverse natural languages. Pass mark: not meaningfully longer than the cross-linguistic median for the same propositions.
2. *Machinery-usage audit* (used here, on one register): in that same multi-register corpus, count real usage of each optional system. Pass mark: a system with ~0 usage across *all* sampled registers is a deletion/leave-to-use candidate; a system used only in one register should be taught/marked as register-specific rather than core.
3. *Ambiguity-cost audit*: measure how often the "clear form" (gun, man, aspect marking) must actually be invoked vs. left implicit in natural samples, as a check on whether "the common case costs nothing" (principle 1) holds in practice rather than just in theory.
4. *Processing-load proxy*: measure garden-path/backtrack rate on held-out sentences for a parser (human or model) given only the grammar spec, as a stand-in for the real-time cost the comma/attachment rules impose in fast speech where pauses are phonetic, not written.
