# A2 — Language Design Sketch

## 1. Sound system

**Vowels: five, /a e i o u/.** Five-vowel systems are the single most common vowel inventory size across the world's languages (roughly a third of surveyed languages), and the overwhelming majority of those are exactly /a e i o u/ — peripheral, maximally dispersed vowel qualities. This is the strongest cross-linguistic convergence available (**B5**), and maximal dispersion in vowel space is the direct mechanism for **B2** (hard to mishear): adjacent vowels are acoustically far apart, so confusion between them is rare even with noisy transmission or non-native articulation. It is also the system most children acquire fastest, serving **P3**.

**Consonants: a small, perceptually robust set** drawn from the handful that occur in over 90% of languages (m, k, n, j-type glide) plus enough additional places/manners to be expressive: roughly p t k, b d g, m n, s f h, l r, w j (~16). Two choices follow from evidence: (a) keep the inventory near the low-to-middle end of attested sizes, since every extra phoneme is one more thing to produce and resolve (**B8/B7**); (b) avoid leaning on a voiced/voiceless contrast as the *sole* distinguisher where avoidable — noise-discrimination studies find voicing cues (e.g., b/v) among the least robust under real acoustic conditions, while place/manner contrasts (t, g, tʃ) survive noise better. Where a stop/continuant distinction is needed, manner is preferred over voicing, in service of **B2**.

**Syllable shape: (C)V(C), with codas restricted to a short, perceptually salient set (nasals, /s/).** CV is the one syllable type attested in every human language and the canonical first-syllable shape children produce — about as close to a true universal as phonology offers (**B5**), and the simplest possible shape, minimizing production and parsing cost (**B7, B8**). One optional coda (no clusters) buys brevity for frequent short words without reopening cluster-perception problems. Banning clusters entirely — the configurations most vulnerable to misperception and the biggest source of cross-linguistic acquisition difficulty — is a direct, low-cost way to serve **B2** and **B10**.

**Stress: fixed and fully predictable (e.g., always the next-to-last syllable), no lexical tone.** Lexical tone is demonstrably harder for adults whose first language does not use pitch contrastively, and this is most of humanity's living adult learners (**B10**: *any* adult can learn it). A fixed-position stress rule costs nothing to memorize — one rule applied mechanically, not a property stored per word — the literal content of **B7**. Dropping tone also removes a channel of potential mishearing (**B2**), at the cost of not using pitch for lexical contrasts — accepted because **N5** says expressiveness need not come from sound.

## 2. Word forms: length, distinctness, frequency

Word length is generated by rule, not assigned arbitrarily, grounded in the robust cross-linguistic finding (986 languages, independent of family) that frequent words are shorter — Zipf's "law of abbreviation": concepts that recur as most frequent across unrelated languages' corpora (pronouns, "be," "want," "go," "good/bad," small numbers, basic kin/spatial terms) get the shortest available forms — bare monosyllables, CV or CVC; everything else is built by regular derivation/compounding, growing longer as it gets rarer and more specific. This optimizes **B8** (brevity, weighted by actual use) while staying **B7**-predictable: length is a *consequence* of a rule, not a memorized fact per word.

Distinctness constrains the same short, high-stakes core: the handful of most-frequent roots must differ from each other by more than one weak, easily-confused feature (not by voicing alone, which the noise-perception evidence above shows is fragile). Where natural languages let their most frequent words collapse into near-homophones (English "to/too/two"), that is a direct hit against **B2**, so the frequent-and-short zone is deliberately kept phonologically spread out, even at the cost of a segment of brevity. **B2** is a guarantee (**C1**), so it outranks brevity, a cost criterion, when the two conflict.

## 3. Vocabulary: basic concepts, growth, loans

The irreducible vocabulary core is modeled on semantic primes — the roughly five dozen meanings (e.g., *someone, something, do, say, want, know, good, bad, because, can, big, small*) that recur, by independent cross-linguistic analysis (the Natural Semantic Metalanguage program), as expressible by a single, non-decomposable word in every language studied. These get single unanalyzable roots: they are the most convergent evidence available of what "basic" means across humanity (**B5**), and since everything else can be defined in terms of them, they anchor **B1** (meaning gets through, for any thought).

Beyond the primes, vocabulary grows by rule, not by minting arbitrary new roots: a fixed, small set of derivational devices (compounding of roots; affixes for agent, instrument, place, negation/opposite, diminutive/augmentative, and so on) produces new words transparently from existing ones. This is what lets the language "carry whatever its speakers need to say, modern life included" (**B6**) without multiplying the amount that must be memorized (**B7**) — a new concept is a new *combination*, not a new *fact*.

Loans are used only for the narrow case the goal licenses: a form the world already shares (**L4**) — e.g., names for internationally standardized things (units already following SI per **L3**, scientific nomenclature, place and personal names). A loan is never admitted in its donor-language phonology; it is reshaped onto the fixed sound and syllable inventory by one adaptation rule (closest sound, closest syllable shape, no new phonemes, no clusters), mirroring how every natural language nativizes loans to its own phonotactics. This keeps a loan from reopening the phonological design (**B2, B7, B10**) while letting the lexicon absorb genuinely global forms (**B6, B5**). Everyday, non-global, culture-specific coinages are left to speakers via the derivation toolkit, not to loaning — consistent with **N4** and **N6**.

## 4. Core grammar

**Roles: fixed word order, no case marking.** Languages with rich case systems tend to have flexible word order; languages with little or no case tend to have fixed order (the attested word-order/case trade-off). The design takes the fixed-order, caseless side: one rule tells a listener who did what to whom — **B3** (unambiguous) achieved through **B7** (no case paradigms) rather than redundant marking. The order chosen is Subject–Verb–Object. SOV and SVO together cover about 88% of the world's languages and are themselves close to tied (47.5% vs 41%), so this is a genuine judgment call (flagged in §7); SVO is favored because contact situations that build a grammar from scratch under real pressure — pidgins and creoles — converge on SVO with fixed order and minimal inflection far more often than SOV, the closest thing to a natural experiment in what grammar speakers converge on with nothing inherited (**B5, B9**).

**Word building: agglutinative, not fusional.** One morpheme carries one meaning, concatenated without altering neighboring morphemes or triggering irregular stem changes. Agglutination gives segmentable, rule-following words, which is **B7** stated directly ("made and connected by rule, with little to memorize"), and transparent morpheme boundaries are also what the semantic-primes/derivation design in §3 depends on structurally.

**Tense/aspect/mood:** grammaticalize only the handful of categories that recur, by independent diachronic study of grammaticalization paths across unrelated languages, as the dominant types the world's languages re-invent (perfective, imperfective, progressive, future, past, perfect) — again **B5**'s convergence test. Each gets one invariant, stackable particle (agglutinative, §above), so a bare, unmarked verb is still a grammatical, maximally brief default (**B8**) and a speaker adds precision only when the message needs it (**B3**, costed per **C1**: precision is available, not mandatory).

**Questions:** yes/no questions use a single, fixed-position question particle rather than word-order inversion or intonation alone. Intonation is the single most common strategy worldwide, but it has no robust, universal written counterpart, and **B4** requires writing to carry the same meaning as speech; a particle is written and spoken identically, so it is the strategy that actually satisfies **B4**, while remaining a one-rule addition (**B7**) that doesn't disturb the fixed SVO order. Content questions are built from one interrogative root plus the ordinary nominal/locative/temporal/causal derivational affixes from §3 (so "who/what/where/when/why/how" are a regular family, not six things to memorize separately) — **B7** again.

**Negation:** one invariant particle, fixed immediately before the verb. This matches the strong cross-linguistic preference for single-marker, verb-adjacent negation (the dominant pattern in roughly 83% of languages) and gives negation one placement rule and one unambiguous scope (**B3, B7**).

**Pronouns:** person (1/2/3) × number (singular/plural), the obligatory minimum every language makes in some form (**B5, P3**), plus one further distinction: first-person inclusive vs. exclusive "we" (whether the addressee is included). Clusivity is attested, independently, in both Austronesian and northern-Australian languages — unrelated families converging on the same distinction (**B5**) — and its absence is a concrete, everyday source of exactly the ambiguity **B1/B2/B3** exist to prevent (English "we" is systematically ambiguous about whether "you" is included). The cost is one extra, regularly-derived pronoun — cheap enough to clear the **C1** bar. No grammatical gender or noun-class system: not convergent across enough unrelated families to pass **B5**, and the agreement they require mostly repeats information already recoverable from the noun — memorization that fails **C1**'s cost test.

**Modifiers:** invariant words (no agreement in case/number/gender with their head), in one fixed position relative to the noun. Agreement is redundant marking that must be memorized per noun class; the fixed-order design already supplies the predictability agreement would add, so adding agreement would be exactly the kind of "nothing arbitrary unless it pays for itself" violation **C1** warns against.

**Relative clauses:** one uniform relativizing construction, usable all the way down the independently-derived Keenan–Comrie Accessibility Hierarchy (subject > direct object > indirect object > oblique > genitive > object of comparison) rather than a different device per role. The hierarchy is itself cross-linguistic evidence (derived from fifty unrelated languages) about which relativization strategies are actually learnable and attested (**B5**); a single strategy reaching every rank keeps **B3** (anything sayable precisely) without multiplying rules (**B7**).

**Precision vs. economy (B3/C1):** the pattern repeats deliberately across TAM marking, clusivity, and relative-clause marking — each is an optional add-on to a working bare default. A speaker pays the extra morpheme only on the occasions the extra precision is worth it to them, which is **C1**'s cost principle applied directly to grammar rather than left as an abstract design value.

## 5. Numbers and writing

**Numbers: base ten**, transparently and agglutinatively built from ten digit-roots plus power roots (ten, hundred, thousand), with no irregular forms (no English-style "eleven," no French-style "quatre-vingts"). Base ten is both the international standard the design must follow (**L3**, SI and Hindu-Arabic numerals) and, independently, the most frequent base across the world's languages (roughly 60%), traced to the convergent, embodied source of counting on two hands — satisfying **B5** as well as **L3**. Full transparency in the numeral-building rule is **B7** applied to a domain (numbers) where natural languages are disproportionately irregular.

**Writing: Latin alphabet (L3, a hard limit), as shallow an orthography as the alphabet allows** — one letter or fixed digraph per phoneme, one phoneme per letter, no silent letters, no context-dependent pronunciation. Orthographic depth has a measured, large effect on how fast children learn to read (first-grade accuracy around 94% in a shallow orthography versus 59% in a deep one, in published comparisons), bearing directly on **B4** (writing carries the same meaning as speech — trivially true under a 1:1 mapping) and **P3/B10** (children and any adult learn it with minimal extra memorization). Where two conventions for representing a sound conflict internationally, the more broadly shared one is adopted, per **C2**'s instruction to follow the international standard when cultures conflict.

## 6. What is deliberately left undefined, and resistance to drift

**Left undefined, by design (N4):** the lexicon beyond the semantic-prime core and the derivational toolkit — new coinages are for speakers to make, by the existing rules, as needed; idiom and figurative extension of literal meanings; register and politeness beyond the single clusivity pronoun; which loanwords get adopted and when (only the *adaptation rule* is fixed, per **L4**); regional accent and phonetic realization within the broad phoneme categories; everything **N4** calls style, idiom, and change.

**Resistance to drift (B9):** the design deliberately does not rely on an academy, a frozen reference grammar, or any mechanism that "controls how it evolves" — **B9** explicitly says it must survive change, not prevent it. Instead, resistance comes from the generative machinery itself being maximally regular and exceptionless: agglutination with no allomorphy, one fixed word order, invariant TAM/question/negation particles, and a 1:1 orthography. The evidence for why this matters comes from how children and creole-forming communities actually change languages: both are documented to regularize noisy input by dropping irregular, infrequent variants and reinforcing the dominant regular pattern, rather than inventing new irregularity from nothing. A language that starts with essentially no irregularity gives drift very little surface to erode toward anything *other* than the existing rule — the regularizing tendency that usually attacks exception-ridden natural languages has nothing to attack here except by extending the rule further, which is the kind of change **B9** is written to tolerate. Because the protected core (phonology, the small prime set, the grammar machinery) is also small and individually justified by convergence (**B5**), speakers are free to drift vocabulary, idiom, and style (**N4**) without that drift threatening the structural core that keeps **B1** working.

## 7. Least-sure decisions

1. **SVO vs. SOV.** The two orders are nearly tied worldwide (41% vs. 47.5%); the argument above leans on creole/pidgin convergence, which is evidence about language formed under contact-and-urgency conditions, not necessarily about what is best for children acquiring a language from birth (**P3**). What would settle it: controlled psycholinguistic comparisons of real-time production/parsing cost for SVO vs. SOV specifically under a fixed-order, no-case-marking condition, rather than raw frequency counts across existing languages (which carry their own confounds, e.g. case-marking correlations).

2. **Whether to exclude a systematic voiced/voiceless stop contrast.** The noise-perception evidence used in §1 and §2 specifically examined *English* consonants in particular maskers, not a general cross-linguistic claim that stop voicing is fragile in every language's phonetic implementation. What would settle it: cross-linguistic perceptual-confusability studies of stop-voicing contrasts specifically, under realistic degraded conditions (phone audio, multiple talkers, non-native accents), rather than one language's data.

3. **Whether clusivity earns its place.** Only about a third of the world's languages mark inclusive/exclusive "we," so it is a real but minority convergence, and **C1** warns against anything arbitrary that doesn't pay for itself — "found independently in two unrelated families" is suggestive, not conclusive, evidence that it is worth its cost. What would settle it: a direct measure of how often the lack of this distinction (as in English) actually causes communication failures or require costly repair in practice, weighed against the measured learning/production cost of the extra pronoun.

4. **Fixed stress position vs. free/lexical stress.** Fixed stress was chosen for its zero-memorization property (**B7**), but the evidence gathered compares tone to stress in adult perception, not fixed-position stress to lexical/free stress in child acquisition specifically. What would settle it: developmental timelines comparing children's acquisition speed in languages with predictable, rule-governed stress versus lexical, per-word stress.

No existing project built from this specific goal was deliberately searched for, and none was encountered incidentally during the typological/phonetic/acquisition research conducted for this sketch.
