## 2. Findings

Each finding gives the choice, the evidence, the problem, the severity and a suggested direction.

### High

**F1. Loans are English words in English pronunciation, and many aren't shared by the world at all (L4, B5).**
- *Choice:* §11 says loans are "used only where the world shares a form, and are taken in that international form". The 23 loans in lexicon.tsv include **awuwa** hour (tier 4), **sekandu**, **minitu**, **kilamita**, **pasentu**, **intalunetu**, **imeyilu**, **tuleyin** train, **layisansu** licence, **ilekutulisiti** electricity, **osupitalu** hospital, **bayisikalu** bicycle, **diguli** degree, **kontakutu** contact and **senali** senary.
- *Evidence (forms from standard dictionaries, checked against 9 languages from different families and areas: Mandarin, Japanese, Arabic, Hindi, Swahili, Indonesian, Russian, Spanish, Turkish):*
  - *hour:* xiǎoshí / jikan / sāʿa / ghaṇṭā / saa / jam / chas / hora / saat. That's 0/9; no shared form exists, and **awuwa** is English /aʊə/.
  - *electricity:* diàn / denki / kahrabāʾ / bijlī / umeme / listrik / elektrichestvo / electricidad / elektrik. That's 3/9 elektr-, and none of them has English's -tricity.
  - *bicycle:* 2/9 (Swahili baisikeli, Turkish bisiklet).
  - *hospital:* 3/9 (Hindi aspatāl, Swahili hospitali, Spanish hospital).
  - *degree:* 2/9 (Hindi ḍigrī, Swahili digrii). Russian has *gradus* and Spanish *grado*.
  - *minute:* 4/9, and *second:* 4/9.
  - *senary:* a rare English technical word that no other language shares.
  - Where a form *is* shared, the adaptation follows English vowels rather than the international form: **tuleyin** follows /treɪn/ where tren / treni / tren are the shared forms; **layisansu** /laɪsəns/ beside lisensi / licencia / litsenziya; **kilamita** with English schwa beside kilometro / kilometr / kilomita; **pasentu** beside persen / protsent / porcentaje; **intalunetu** with English "inta-".
- *Problem:* L4 is a hard limit, and it fails for roughly half the loans. Those that pass are borrowed through English phonology, so the "international form" in practice means "English as heard by an English speaker". This is the most direct case of one-language sourcing in the language, and these words set the precedent for every future loan.
- *Severity:* **high**: a hard-limit breach that defines the loan policy for the whole language.
- *Direction:* Drop **awuwa**, **bayisikalu**, **osupitalu**, **diguli**, **senali**, **kontakutu** and **ilekutulisiti** as loans and build them from native words (e.g. hour = a native unit word + the SI symbol `h` read by culture, hospital = **pu** + "heal"). For the loans that stay, adapt from a cross-linguistic median form rather than from English: *tuleni*, *lisensu*, *kilometa*, *pelsen(tu)*, *internetu*, *minutu*, *sekundu*. Measure this as in §3 (L4).

**F2. Epistemic modality is a numeric scale in sixths (`kon` + digit) (B5, L3, C1 cost).**
- *Choice:* §6.1. "Might", "probably" and "must be" are all **kon** + a number of sixths. Learn.md maps "maybe" to **kon ka**, "even odds".
- *Evidence:* Languages grammaticalise epistemic possibility as verbal constructions, verb affixes or particles (WALS 75A: 65 / 84 / 91 of 240), with 2–3 coarse grades (possible / probable / certain). None of them does it with numerals. A de facto international standard for verbal probability *does* exist: the IPCC calibrated likelihood scale (Mastrandrea et al. 2010: "very likely" 90–100%, "likely" 66–100%, "about as likely as not" 33–66%), which descends from Kent's 1964 "Words of Estimative Probability" for intelligence analysis. Sixths match neither. They come from the same base-6 preference as F3.
- *Problem:* B5 asks for convergent solutions, and here a designer's invention replaces one of the most frequent categories in everyday speech. It also buys false precision: ordinary "maybe" doesn't mean p = 0.5, but the only short way to say "maybe" claims exactly that. The design doesn't have a vague middle ("possibly") that's cheaper than a number. **kon bo** "don't know how sure" is the nearest thing, and it means something else. Counterfactuals are built on it too (**don kon po** = "if, with probability 0"). Languages mark counterfactuals with irrealis or "fake past" morphology (Iatridou 2000), not with zero probability.
- *Severity:* **high**: it affects a large share of everyday sentences and the conditional system.
- *Direction:* Use 2–3 vague grades as content words or particles: *possibly* / *probably* / *surely*, plus the existing unmarked "believed". Keep numeric precision as an optional add-on with percentages (**pasentu**, international) and drop sixths. Give counterfactuals a dedicated irrealis particle. Don't derive them from **kon po**.

**F3. A base-6 measurement system and calendar sit inside the core language (L3).**
- *Choice:* 34 of the 547 lexicon entries (6%) belong to it: 20 native SI-parallel unit roots (**nane** unit.time, **fomi** unit.mass …), 12 senary powers (**nobi** … **genwi**), **boyo** "36 days (senary)", **senali**. On top of those come a senary calendar (measurement.md, not provided), the `₆` notation, and a phonotactic rule that serves digit names.
- *Evidence:* SI (BIPM) and ISO 8601 / the Gregorian calendar are the international standards, and L3 names exactly this case. Decimal is the majority numeral base (125/196, WALS 131A). True senary systems are confined to a few New Guinea languages (e.g. Ndom, Kanum), and WALS 131A files them under "other base" (5 languages).
- *Problem:* It breaches a hard limit. Calling it "optional" doesn't fix that, because it occupies lexicon, notation and documentation space in a language that otherwise follows L3 well (ISO 8601 weekdays and dates, SI symbols, grouping digits with spaces). It looks like the designer's own project inside the language (a doghouse), and it also leaks into F2.
- *Severity:* **high** (hard limit). Its everyday reach is limited, though.
- *Direction:* Move the senary system out of the language into a separate, optional project. Delete the 34 entries. *Add-back trigger:* a real community of speakers who use base-6 units in daily life.

### Medium

**F4. Oblique relative clauses strand the preposition, an English and Scandinavian pattern (B5).**
- *Choice:* §5.3: **kemu man to nume mon** "the place I live at", **yetu man to fonsu gan** "the tool I cut with".
- *Evidence:* Productive preposition stranding under relativisation is attested essentially only in English and North Germanic, and in Kru (Gbadi). It's absent from Romance and restricted in German and Dutch (Abels 2003; summary in en.wikipedia "Preposition stranding"). For obliques, WALS 123A lists gap 55, pronoun-retention 20, relative pronoun 13, non-reduction 14 and not possible 10 (of 112). The gap languages leave the adposition out (Mandarin 我住的地方, "I live DE place") rather than stranding it. Keenan & Comrie (1977) show that pronoun retention rises down the accessibility hierarchy, and the language already uses it for genitives (**ewo man guyi pi wan gun kudu**).
- *Problem:* This is English syntax in a structural slot, and an English speaker designing "naturally" would produce exactly it.
- *Severity:* **medium**.
- *Direction:* Use pronoun retention, consistent with the genitive rule: **kemu man to nume mon bu** "place that I live at it". Alternatively, allow a bare gap when the role is obvious.

**F5. `fa` "none" negates without verbal negation, the core SAE pattern (B5).**
- *Choice:* §5.2 lets **fa** stand alone as "none". The UDHR uses it as English uses *nothing* or *no one*: **musu fa kubo tan** "nothing changes this" (Art. 2), **fa pi kemin tan yofu wun …** "nothing in this declaration means …" (Art. 30).
- *Evidence:* In WALS 115A, 170 of 206 languages keep predicate negation with negative indefinites; only 11 (5%) don't, and those are mostly European. Haspelmath (2001) lists "negative pronouns and lack of verbal negation" as one of the nine SAE features.
- *Problem:* It's English leakage, and it also leaves open what **mu … fa** means (double negation or concord?), which hurts B3.
- *Severity:* **medium**.
- *Direction:* Express "nothing" or "no one" as **mu** + verb + indefinite (**musu bo mu kubo tan**, **ewo bo mu …**). Restrict **fa** to quantity answers ("how many?" → **fa**), and state the meaning of **mu … fa** explicitly.

**F6. Adjective predication needs the copula, and `gun` + verb is a participial passive (B5).**
- *Choice:* §4.2/4.7: a clear state is **guyi gun kudu**, and **gun** + action word gives the undergoer's state (**gun tapi** "is closed", **gun efon** "is allowed"). The UDHR leans on it heavily (**go mu gun efon …** ×10+, **gun kuwi**, **gun yema**).
- *Evidence:* WALS 118A: verbal encoding of predicate adjectives 151/386 (39%) against nonverbal 132 (34%). The isolating SVO languages the language itself cites as models (Thai, Indonesian; learn.md lesson 3) use verbal adjectives: Indonesian *rumah itu besar*, with no copula, and *yang* (= **man**) for attribution. A passive exists in only 162/373 languages (43%, 107A), and the copula-plus-participle passive is an SAE feature (Haspelmath 2001).
- *Problem:* The "clear form" of the most common predicate type copies English *is big* / *is closed*. Its proclaimed models do the opposite. And the experiencer trap the reference documents (**to gun nuta** "I am feared" vs **to nuta** "I'm afraid") exists only because English maps "be + feeling word" one way.
- *Severity:* **medium**.
- *Direction:* Make bare **N + quality** a statement (verbal adjectives, as in Thai and Indonesian), use **man** for attribution when it's ambiguous, and keep **gun** for nominal and locative predicates. Mark the undergoer's state with a resultative aspect word ("done-to") rather than with the copula.

**F7. Third-person reference stacks three rare systems: obligatory incl./excl., obviative `yi`, logophoric `sun` (B5, B7).**
- *Evidence:* Inclusive/exclusive appears in 63/200 languages (32%, 39A). Obviation is "a hallmark … typologically rare … predominantly Algonquian", with marginal cases in Kutenai and Salish. Logophoric pronouns are "almost exclusively found in African language families" (Culy 1994; Niger-Congo, Nilo-Saharan, neighbouring Chadic).
- *Problem:* Each piece is locally defensible: it disambiguates, which serves B1 and B3. But they come from three different rare areal types, and together they require every third-person mention to be tracked for topicality ("a newcomer is **yi** until the speaker makes them the topic") and for reported-speech perspective, all on top of the reflexive rule. That's additions begetting additions. Most languages without gender solve the same problem with zero anaphora, demonstratives ("the latter") or repeating the noun, and the language already has **ben** "that one" for things.
- *Severity:* **medium**.
- *Direction:* Keep **sun** (the cheapest, and it fixes the commonest ambiguity, "she said she …"). Replace **yi** with **ben** or demonstrative reference for "the other one". Make **pen**/**din** optional refinements of a neutral "we" (as colloquial Mandarin does with 咱们 / 我们). *Add-back trigger for **yi**:* corpus evidence of frequent two-referent confusions that **ben** and naming fail to resolve.

**F8. The overall profile is one areal type: Mainland Southeast Asian / creole isolating (B5 "not from one").**
- *Evidence:* Exclusively isolating languages are 16/165 (10%, WALS 20A); little affixation 141/969 (15%, 26A); a plural word 16% (33A); preverbal TAM particles, serial verbs, sentence-initial particles. Together this is the "creole prototype" (McWhorter 2001) and the Thai/Vietnamese/Yoruba type. 85% of languages have productive affixation, mostly suffixing (406 + 123 suffixing vs 58 + 94 prefixing, 26A).
- *Problem:* Taking the majority value for every feature separately would produce an incoherent language (OV with prepositions occurs in 14 languages, 95A), so choosing one coherent type is legitimate. But the design never says so: it presents "words never change form" as if it were simply best. The cost of the isolating choice is that every category is a separate syllable (a 14-slot template), which works against B8. It also favours adult learners (N2 says native speakers come first, and children acquire agglutinative suffixes without trouble).
- *Severity:* **medium**: it's defensible, but undocumented, and it's in tension with B5's wording.
- *Direction:* State the choice explicitly ("one coherent head-initial isolating type, because majority-per-feature is incoherent") and change B5's measure to match (§3). Allow and document the expected cliticisation of unstressed grammar words (B9) rather than forbidding fusion "until a new word".

**F9. Pragmatics is prescribed: "Being polite never means hinting", and every act gets a compulsory particle (C2, L2, N4).**
- *Choice:* Learn.md lesson 8 and §10.2 say a polite warning is **yo nun** + the bare problem. There are 7 obligatory illocution particles (advice vs instruction vs request vs commitment vs warning), and Principle 6 reads "every social pressure has its own word".
- *Evidence:* Request strategies vary widely across cultures. The Cross-Cultural Speech Act Realization Project (Blum-Kulka, House & Kasper 1989) found conventional indirectness to be the dominant request strategy in most of the languages studied. Directness norms differ systematically (Wierzbicka 1991). Sentence-type particles exist in many languages (Tagalog *po* for politeness; Korean promissive; apprehensive "lest" moods in Austronesian and Australian languages), but they are usually sentence-final and few. A five-way directive/commissive split mirrors Searle's taxonomy, an Anglo-American philosophical theory, rather than any language's grammar.
- *Problem:* N4 says style comes from use, and C2/L2 say the language carries its cultures' conventions. Mandating directness imposes one culture's norm (low-context, Northern European / engineering). The fine directive distinctions are a designer's analytic scheme, not a convergent solution.
- *Severity:* **medium**.
- *Direction:* Keep **min**, **tin**/**so** and **nun**, which are attested and cheap. Make **fon**, **de** and **yo** optional, and delete "never hint" from the teaching materials. *Add-back trigger:* evidence that speakers misread advice as orders.

**F10. C1's method is not applied on the page: almost no choice cites cross-linguistic evidence (C1, B5).**
- *Evidence:* In language.md and learn.md, the only convergence claims are "as in Thai or Indonesian" (N-Adj), two glosses in compounds.tsv ("as Mandarin 计算机, Arabic حاسوب"; "as Spanish cuenta") and ISO 8601. §14 claims **apu** "word; language" is a lumping "most language families" make. CLICS⁴ shows LANGUAGE–WORD in 117 links but LANGUAGE–TONGUE in 147, and 117 of 586 varieties is about 20%, not "most".
- *Problem:* Convergence is *the method* (C1), yet it's asserted rather than shown, and the one checkable claim is overstated. Undocumented choices are exactly where F1–F9 crept in.
- *Severity:* **medium**: my scorecard shows most choices would pass if they were documented.
- *Direction:* Give each structural choice one line with its WALS/PHOIBLE/CLICS value and count (a scorecard like §1). Correct the **apu** claim, or switch to the commoner TONGUE–LANGUAGE lumping.

### Low

**F11. Some lexicon concept boundaries copy English.** I checked about 15 roots against CLICS⁴. Most are fine:
- **nume** "live / dwell" is attested (DWELL–BE ALIVE: 126 links).
- **pani** "aged / worn" is very common (OLD(AGED)–OLD(USED): 2,847 links).
- **gala**/**kima** split TREE and WOOD; the two are colexified in only 323 links, so splitting is the majority.
- **muyi**/**gimu** split English "time" correctly.
- **kiti**/**inyi** split hand and arm; **dunan** lumps month and moon, which is common.

Three don't hold up:
- **tinli** "key, button" is used for the house key (**tinli pi guyi**, lesson 7) and for keyboard keys. CLICS has KEY–BUTTON: 0 links (KEY's colexifications are LOCK, LATCH). This is purely English *key*.
- **yebin** "call, phone or call to" is English *call* (Mandarin 打电话 ≠ 叫, Japanese 電話する ≠ 呼ぶ). CLICS CALL colexifies with SHOUT, CALL BY NAME and INVITE, not "telephone".
- **nosen** "god", with religion as **lite pi nosen** "way of God" and **so nosen kuwi me** "may God keep you", frames religion as theistic. That excludes Buddhist, Jain and Confucian traditions, which goes against C1's "fair to every culture".

*Direction:* split **tinli** (lock-key vs button), split **yebin**, and gloss religion as "way of belief" or "the sacred".

**F12. Sibling terms document sex, not relative age.** **faba** "sibling" is neutral, and the documented modifier is gender (**tiwu futa** etc.). Relative age is the main sibling axis across East and Southeast Asia, Africa and Oceania. CLICS has OLDER BROTHER as a separate concept in about 1,660 varieties, colexified with YOUNGER BROTHER in only 813 links. *Direction:* document **faba pani** / **faba loyen** "elder/younger sibling" as the standard pattern, like gender.

**F13. The polar-question particle is clause-initial.** Final position is 59% of particle languages and initial 24% (WALS 92A). The departure serves the language's own "operators first" principle and early parsing, so it's acceptable. *Direction:* say so in the rationale. The final tag **min** already covers the final-position habit.

**F14. `me` "you" is number-indifferent, like English *you*.** Only 9/261 languages (3%) have number-indifferent pronouns (35A), and first person here *does* distinguish number (**to** vs **pen**/**din**), so the system is lopsided. *Direction:* make **me la** the expected plural, as **wan la** is.

**F15. Indefinites are noun-based (`ewo bo`, `musu bo`), the European type.** The pattern is 26% worldwide, against 60% for interrogative-based (46A, e.g. Mandarin 谁 "someone", Russian *kto-to*). It may be justified by avoiding confusion with wh-in-situ questions (B3). *Direction:* keep it, and record the reason.

**F16. `gan` "with" is both comitative and instrumental, like English *with*.** Identity is 76/322 (24%) against 213 for differentiation (52A). That's cheap to keep, but it isn't convergent. *Direction:* consider "use" (**bone**) as a serial verb for instruments ("use knife cut"), which is the Mainland Southeast Asian and West African pattern and is already available in the language.

**F17. The consonant selection prefers /f/ and /d/ over /ŋ/ and /h/.** PHOIBLE: f 44%, d 46%, ŋ 63%, h 56%, r 44%. /f/ is absent natively from much of South and East Asia, Australia and Africa, yet it carries the digit **fi** "1" and 22+ roots. The small inventory (16% of languages, 1A) is justified by B2 and B10. *Direction:* if any consonant is revisited, /f/ is the weakest-supported. At least document the selection criterion.

**F18. Root forms ignore the strongest sound–meaning biases.** Blasi et al. (2016, PNAS, 6,452 word lists) found nasals favoured in personal pronouns, and t, p, b, s, l, r, u disfavoured in "I". The language has **to** "I", whose /t/ is disfavoured and which has no nasal. "breast" (**egu**) lacks the favoured /m/. Some forms do fit: **danki** "nose" has /n/, **lisun** "tongue" has /l/, **meli** "small" has /i/, **mole** "mother" has /m/ (Murdock 1959). Also, **me** = "you" inverts the Eurasian m-T pattern (first person m; WALS 136A: 30/230) and matches the N-M pattern of the Pacific Rim (137A: 36/230), which is why learn.md warns English speakers about **me**. *Severity:* low, because C1 allows arbitrary roots. *Direction:* when roots are next revised, apply the Blasi associations at no cost.

**F19. Weekday numbering clashes with existing ordinal-weekday cultures.** **dili pi sa** is Tuesday (ISO, Monday = 1), but in Portuguese, Vietnamese, Greek, Hebrew and Arabic the "second day" is Monday. L3 justifies ISO, but "day two" is a guaranteed mis-hearing (B1) for those speakers. *Direction:* use a weekday construction that isn't the ordinal "day N", e.g. weekday names, or "week-day N" with an explicit **nelon**.

**F20. Commands require `tin`.** Isolating languages use the bare verb as the imperative (Mandarin, Thai, Yoruba). Here a bare **obu.** is a statement, and the most frequent directive costs an extra syllable (B8). 22% of WALS 70A languages have no dedicated second-person imperative form. *Direction:* allow a subjectless bare verb + object as an imperative, and keep **tin** for clarity.

**F21. The manner slot contradicts itself, and V-Adv-O is a minority order.** language.md §4.1 puts manner (slot 9) *before* objects (slot 10), while learn.md lesson 14 puts "who gets it, then what" (9) before "how" (10). V-Adv-O also separates the verb from its object, against the verb–object adjacency preference seen in most VO languages (Thai and Indonesian put manner after the object). *Direction:* fix the contradiction. Consider V-O-Adv with **gan**/**fu**-phrases for manner, and drop the "position decides adverb vs adjective" rule.

**F22. Two phonetic prescriptions will not survive B9:** stress realised as "pitch only, not longer", and "every syllable keeps its full vowel". Stressed syllables lengthen in most stress languages, and unstressed function words reduce in every spoken language. *Direction:* describe these as targets rather than rules, as B9 implies.

## 3. Proposed measurements for the evaluation document

**B5 (built from what many languages share)**
- *Method:* Fix a feature list in advance: the ~45 rows of §1, drawn from WALS chapters 1–144 plus 115A, 118A, 123A. For each feature, record the language's value and the world distribution counted **by genus** (Dryer's method), not by language, to correct for family size. Classify each choice as: (a) majority or plurality value; (b) minority value harmonic with the chosen type (e.g. NGen with VO, per Dryer 1992); (c) minority with a written justification citing another criterion; (d) rare (<10% of genera) or confined to one macro-area or family.
- *Pass:* ≥ 70% in (a)+(b); every (c) carries a cited trade-off; **zero (d) without an explicit trade-off note**. Current estimate: about 29–30 of 46 rows in (a)+(b) (about 63%; the plural word and the initial Q particle count as harmonic), so this fails. F2, F4, F5 and F7 fall in (d). Fixing the 9 failed rows along the suggested directions would bring it to about 80%.
- *SAE leakage test:* Haspelmath's (2001) 9 core SAE features. Pass ≤ 1, and that one only if it is also the world majority. Current: participial passive (F6) and negative-pronoun-without-verbal-negation (F5) give 2. There are no articles, no 'have'-perfect, and intensifier and reflexive are identical.
- *Areal balance:* every precedent cited in the docs is tagged with its macro-area (Africa, Eurasia, SE Asia–Oceania, Australia–New Guinea, North America, South America). Pass: no area supplies > 40% of the cited precedents, and no structural choice is precedented only in Europe.

**C1 (convergence as the method)**
- *Method:* audit the reference. Every structural choice and every lumped or split gloss must cite its convergence evidence (WALS/PHOIBLE/CLICS value and count, or a named paper).
- *Pass:* 100% of section-level choices cited, and spot-checks of 10 random citations reproduce. Claims like "most families lump X and Y" need a CLICS family count above half the families sampled.

**B5 / C1 for the lexicon (concept boundaries)**
- *Method:* take 100 concepts from Concepticon (the Leipzig–Jakarta list + 50 modern-life items). For each lumping or splitting in the lexicon, look up the CLICS⁴ colexification. Compute how often the language agrees with the cross-linguistic majority and how often it agrees with English.
- *Pass:* every lumping is attested in ≥ 10 families across ≥ 3 macro-areas (KEY–BUTTON fails). Agreement with English **beyond** agreement with the world majority is at most 5 points (i.e. where English is in the minority, the language follows English in ≤ 5% of those concepts).
- *Optional:* the Blasi et al. 2016 associations, checked for the ~30 concepts they cover. Pass: no root contains a significantly *disfavoured* segment.

**L3 (international standards)**
- *Method:* list the fields with a formal or de facto standard: SI (BIPM) units and symbols, ISO 8601 dates, times and weeks, ISO 4217 currencies, ISO 80000 number formatting, Unicode Latin script, the IPCC/ICD-203 verbal probability scale, ISO 3166 country names, and the IUPAC and WHO INN naming systems. For each, check the language's default.
- *Pass:* 100% follow the standard by default, and **no parallel native system in the core lexicon** (the senary system and **kon** sixths fail now). Where a standard conflicts with a large culture's existing habit (F19), the conflict is documented with a disambiguating form.

**L4 (borrow only shared forms)**
- *Method:* a fixed panel of 20 languages, at least 3 from each of 6 macro-areas, e.g. Mandarin, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese, Indonesian, Tagalog, Hindi, Bengali, Tamil, Persian, Arabic, Turkish, Russian, Spanish, Swahili, Hausa, Yoruba, Amharic, Quechua, Guaraní. A loan qualifies if a cognate form is the everyday word in ≥ 12/20 panel languages, covering ≥ 4 macro-areas. The adapted form is then derived from the **median** panel pronunciation, not from English. Test it by having raters without English score the loan's recognisability, and compare the English-sourced form with the median-sourced one.
- *Pass:* 100% of loans meet the threshold, and no loan's form is closer to English than to the panel median. Currently about half fail the threshold (F1).

**Leakage, blind test (cheap, high-yield)**
- Give the reference to 5 linguists, each a native speaker of an unrelated language (e.g. Mandarin, Arabic, Swahili, Hindi, Quechua), and ask them to flag constructions that "feel English or European".
- *Pass:* fewer than 3 constructions flagged by ≥ 2 raters. I'd expect F4, F5 and F6 to be flagged.
