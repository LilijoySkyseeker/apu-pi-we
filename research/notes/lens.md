# The lens: how much a language's cheap distinctions shape perception, attention and memory

Research note, 2026-10-08. A sub-pass of the efficient-semantics research, which arrived after that note was written. It verifies the "lens" claims that `efficient-semantics.md` left unverified.

**Short answer:** lexical categories reliably change what people can *track, remember and communicate*. The clearest case is exact number. Effects on perception itself are small, often vanish under verbal interference, and several famous ones fail recent replications. Making a distinction cheap mainly buys faster, more consistent naming, shared reference, and memory that holds up over time. It buys little raw perceptual discrimination.

| Domain | Finding | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Colour | Russian blues: 124 ms advantage across the boundary, gone under verbal load (Winawer 2007, n = 21 + 21). Not replicated for siniy/goluboy (Martinovic et al. 2020). The right-visual-field claim (Gilbert 2006) failed in 230 observers (Witzel & Gegenfurtner 2011) and in a Bayesian replication (Brederoo 2019). The Greek vMMN effect (Thierry 2009) is attributed to contrast adaptation (Martinovic et al. 2026). | Online-labelling effects modest but replicated; perceptual and lateralized claims contested and failing |
| Space | Dutch 82% egocentric vs ‡Akhoe Hai‖om 54% geocentric (Haun 2006); apes and preschoolers default to environment-based coding. But the pattern can be flipped by task cues (Li & Gleitman 2002), environment predicts it (Shapero 2017), and Tseltal speakers solve left/right tasks easily (Li 2011). | Language and spatial preference covary (established); that language causes it is contested |
| Number | Pirahã: exact matching fails above about 3 once memory or rearranging is needed (Frank 2008); Mundurukú exact arithmetic fails above 4–5 while approximation stays intact (Pica 2004); English speakers under verbal interference match like the Pirahã (Frank 2012). | Well established: number words are a tool for exact tracking above about 4 |
| Smell | Jahai name odours as consistently as colours, English speakers don't (Majid & Burenhult 2014). Hunter-gatherer vs horticulturalist split within one family (Majid & Kruspe 2018). Culture explains only 6% of odour-pleasantness rankings (Arshamian 2022). | Naming effects large and established; perception effects unshown |

**General:**
- "Whorf was half right" (Regier & Kay 2009).
- Labels make perception more categorical under hard conditions (Lupyan et al. 2020).
- Eye movements differ by language only when people are preparing to speak (Papafragou 2008).
- There is no meta-analysis of colour or space effects.

**Implications for the vocabulary method:**
- The lens is a lever on **communication and memory**, which is exactly the north star, not on perception.
- **Exact number:** making it cheap is the best-supported choice; already done.
- **Both spatial frames cheap:** justified as communication of shared reference, not as a perception effect.
- **No extra colour roots for lens reasons:** confirmed.
- **Smell vocabulary:** would improve smell *communication* (large naming effect). Add if tests show smell talk losing meaning.
