# Efficient semantics: the frontier, communicative need, and the lens

Written 2026-10-08 for step 1 of `docs/vocabulary-plan.md` (the "Target" and "The lens" rows). Labels: **[S]** read in the cited source this session; **[I]** my inference; **UNVERIFIED** = recalled or seen only second-hand. Numbers in brackets point to the source list.

**Bottom line.** The frontier models are real and replicated across a dozen domains, but they are *filters, not generators*: they rule out inefficient systems and say where the curve's sweet spot is, but they never pick one system for you. The dial that picks a point on the curve is communicative need, and need is measurably culture-specific. For a designer the most useful result is not any single "optimal system" but a rule that falls out of every model: **give short roots to high-need meanings and build low-need meanings compositionally.** Lens effects are real but mostly modest; the one large effect is exact number.

## 1. The framework and where languages sit

**Set-up.** A speaker has a referent *t*, says word *w*, the listener reconstructs a distribution. Communicative cost = Σ need(t) × information lost(t); cognitive cost = size of the lexicon's mental description (term count, description length, or bits). Attested systems should lie on or near the Pareto frontier; cultures where a domain matters more should sit further toward the informative, complex end [1]. **[S]**

**Limits that matter for a designer** [I unless marked]:
- Near-optimality is tested against rotations or permutations of real systems, which is a low bar. Many systems sit near the curve.
- Complexity is measured differently in each paper. The IB complexity measure has "counterintuitive consequences" (Bruneau-Bongard et al. 2025) [12] **[S]**. A note on complexity gives the conditions under which description length and IB agree [13] **[S]**.
- History matters: an existing vocabulary biases which efficient solution a language moves to next (Twomey et al. 2024) [14] **[S]**. A designed language has no history, so it can sit anywhere on the curve.
- The simplicity pressure comes from learning and the informativeness pressure from communication (Carr et al. 2020, iterated learning) [15] **[S]**. For a language children are imagined to learn natively, learnability is the simplicity term.
- Kinship terminologies track phylogeny, with "only patchy support" for social drivers (Passmore & Jordan 2020, 176 societies) [16] **[S]**.

## 2. What the models let you read off

- **Colour.** Published optimal systems exist for 3–6 terms (RKK figure; reproduced in [1]), and the IB curve gives a full sequence of phase transitions with code on GitHub [3]. The six-category point (black, white, red, yellow, green, blue) matches RKK's "six universal focal colors" [2] **[S]**. WCS distribution (WALS 133A, 119 languages): 5 categories is modal (56, 47%); 3 to 6 covers all [17] **[S]**. Green/blue (WALS 134A, 120 languages): grue 68 (57%), separate green vs. blue 30 (25%), the rest merge blue with black or yellow [18] **[S]**. Caveat: the WCS samples non-industrialized societies only, and is not balanced by lineage.
- **Kinship.** No table of "the optimal system at complexity k" exists in the papers I read, only frontier plots, including separate ones for grandparents, siblings, aunts/uncles and so on [4] **[S]**. With 410 different systems among 487 languages, there is no majority system to copy [4] **[S]**; majorities exist only per sub-domain. Robust need patterns: near > distant relatives, ascending > descending generations [4, 19] **[S]**.
- **Numerals.** Any regular recursive base is on the frontier [5]. Choosing the base needs other criteria, such as regularity and processing ([20], arXiv 2025) **[S]**. That is already settled in `base-*.md`.
- **Space.** Topological terms and demonstratives have models but no published "recommended" system. **Frames of reference (left/right vs. north/south) have no efficiency model** that I could find **[searched, none found]**. That choice falls to the lens evidence.

## 3. Communicative need

- **Colour need is non-uniform and varies.** Some regions of colour space have "30-fold greater demand" than others, peaking at salient-object colours such as ripe fruit. Need differs across 130 languages, partly by geography and biogeography (Twomey et al. 2021) [21] **[S]**. Warm colours are communicated more efficiently than cool ones in every WCS language, and objects tend to be warm while backgrounds are cool (Gibson et al. 2017) [22] **[S]**. Industrialization raises colour use: Tsimane' naming is less informative (4.88 vs. 3.80 bits average surprisal for English) and improves for artificially coloured objects [22] **[S]**. Counterpoint: the warm advantage may come from perceptual structure, yet some need effect remains beyond perception (Zaslavsky et al. 2019) [23] **[S]**. Need is better predicted by "capacity constraints and linguistic usage" than by image statistics [24] **[S]**.
- **Kin need is culture-specific.** Hindi's system is more efficient under Hindi need than under 99% of permuted Hindi distributions and 100% of permuted English ones. English: 97% and 93%. Hindi has more need for the father's elder male line and a separate term for father's older brother (Anand & Regier 2023) [19] **[S]**.
- **Domain-level need sets precision.** Across 9 domains in 7 languages, more frequent domains are lexically more precise, except kinship (Bradford et al. 2022) [25] **[S]**. Dictionary data confirm 147 of 163 published lexical-elaboration claims (Khishigsuren et al. 2025) [26] **[S]**.
- **A neutral, human-general need distribution** [I]. No paper constructs one. The workable recipe follows the plan's lineage weighting: estimate per-language need (Twomey's max-entropy inference for colour [21]; "my X" corpus counts for kin [4, 19]), then average one vote per lineage. Use only patterns that hold everywhere (near > far kin; warm > cool; small numbers more frequent) to set roots. Leave culture-specific peaks to composition. Note the tension: "everyone's native language" implies a world of dyes, screens and wiring, so modern need is legitimately part of human need. That pushes colour toward the industrialized end, as Gibson's Tsimane' result predicts.

## 4. The lens

**Status:** a separate research pass on the relativity literature hadn't returned when this was written. Only the items marked [S] were read this session. Everything marked UNVERIFIED is recalled and needs checking before anyone relies on it.

- **Mechanism (sourced).** Cibelli et al. 2016 model language effects as Bayesian inference. Categories bias memory most "when perceptual information is uncertain", and the model reproduces cross-language colour-discrimination-from-memory data [27] **[S]**. Implication [I]: the lens works mainly through memory and attention under uncertainty, not raw perception.
- **Space, topology (sourced).** English obligatorily marks support/non-support and Korean doesn't. Native English speakers showed categorical perception for support; late-English-learning Koreans tested in a Korean context didn't; early learners in an English context did (Holmes, Moty & Regier 2017) [28] **[S]**. The effect depends on the immediate linguistic context.
- **Colour.** Russian speakers discriminate light vs. dark blue faster across their category boundary, and verbal interference removes the advantage (Winawer et al. 2007). The effect is on the order of tens of ms and depends on on-line labelling. **UNVERIFIED**
- **Spatial frames.** Speakers of absolute-frame languages (Tzeltal, Hai//om) solve non-verbal rotation tasks absolutely, and Dutch speakers relatively (Majid et al. 2004; Haun et al. 2011). Contested: Li & Gleitman 2002 attribute it to task context and environment. **UNVERIFIED**
- **Number.** The one large, well-replicated effect. Without exact number words (Pirahã, Mundurukú), exact matching of sets above about 3–4 fails, while approximate magnitude stays intact (Gordon 2004; Pica et al. 2004; Frank et al. 2008). See also `base-cognition.md`. **UNVERIFIED here**
- **Smell.** Jahai speakers name odours as consistently as colours; English speakers don't (Majid & Burenhult 2014). This shows codability, but not that smell perception changes. **UNVERIFIED**

**Verdict [I]:** exact number is a large effect (a cognitive tool). Frames of reference are probably real but confounded with culture. Colour and topological effects are small, label-dependent and context-dependent. So making a distinction cheap is worth doing for its *communicative* value. Count the lens as a modest bonus, except for number.

## Recommendation for the vocabulary

1. **One rule across domains** [I, from 1, 5, 7]: lexicalize (short root) where need is high; compose (root + modifier, or a relation phrase) where need is low. Composition is what recursive numerals do. It buys near-full informativeness for little lexicon, and pays in length only on rare meanings, the same form/frequency trade Mollica et al. find in grammar.
2. **Colour: keep the six roots** already in `lexicon.tsv` (black, white, red, yellow, green, blue). That is the RKK/IB six-category point, near the steep part of the curve. **Flag:** the WCS majority is *grue* (57%), so splitting blue is a deliberate deviation. The justification is modern need (Gibson, Twomey) and not lineage majority, so record it as an evidence line in the concept table. Brown, orange, pink, purple and grey become compounds, warm ones first (higher need) [22]. Define terms by focal colour and accept soft boundaries, as IB predicts [3]. Add-back trigger: blind tests losing meaning on these colours.
3. **Kinship: small root set plus composition** (as the roadmap already says). Roots for mother, father, child, sibling (sex-neutral) and spouse, with cheap optional elder/younger and male/female modifiers. Everything else (mother's brother, father's elder brother, cousins) is built compositionally, so any culture's distinctions are expressible at modest cost and none is forced. Evidence: near and ascending kin have the highest need [4, 19]; there is no majority whole-system [4]; need is culture-specific [19]. Add-back trigger: a frequent composed form (e.g., grandmother) proving long in tests. Then it earns a root, exactly as the "lexicalize high need" rule predicts.
4. **Numerals:** no change. Any regular recursive base is frontier-optimal [5, 20]. Keep approximate words (few, many) as separate cheap words, since approximate and exact systems coexist [5].
5. **Grammar-level checks on existing choices:** seen/told/inferred matches the model's preferred major-cluster evidential split [7]. For roadmap B2 modality, keep force and flavour as separate morphemes (IFF) [10]. For indefinites, the "some/none/any" set can be checked against Denić et al.'s meaning space [8] if it ever causes trouble.
6. **Space:** use the lineage-weighted majority for topological terms (no model gives a better answer). For frames of reference, make both relative (left/right) and absolute (cardinal) cheap, per the lens evidence in section 4. Demonstratives: the 2- or 3-way distance systems that dominate typology are all near the frontier [6].
7. **What to make cheap (lens):** exact number (already done); both left/right and cardinal directions as short roots, so neither frame is the default for every speaker's attention; confidence, source and inclusive "we" (already done, and the model results above support source); smell-quality words only if the seed tests show they're missed (add-back trigger), since the evidence shows codability rather than a perceptual gain. Don't add a light-blue root for its lens value: the colour effects are too small to pay for a root.
