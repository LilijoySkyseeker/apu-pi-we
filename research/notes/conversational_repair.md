# Conversational repair across languages: where everyday conversation breaks down

Scope: other-initiated repair (OIR) and related corpus work used to measure what goes wrong in real spoken and signed conversation. Verification legend: **[primary]** means the figure was read in the primary text (PDF or full HTML). **[secondary]** means it comes from a search-engine summary or a citing paper and was not checked against the primary text. **[unverified]** means it is from background knowledge and no source was found this session.

## Q1. How often does repair happen in everyday conversation across languages?

### Takeaway
Across 12 languages (11 spoken and 1 signed), someone signals a problem with the previous turn about once every 1.4 minutes of informal conversation (2,053 cases in 48.5 hours). In English, clarification requests make up roughly 3–4% of sentences. Breakdown is routine, not exceptional. The paper reports no significant differences in rate between languages.

### Cited Findings
- **Sample** **[primary]**: Dingemanse et al. 2015 (PLOS ONE) used "spontaneous, naturally occurring conversations between families and friends", about 4 hours per language. The 12 languages come from 8 families on 5 continents: Dutch, English, Icelandic, Italian, Lao, Mandarin, Russian, Siwu, Cha'palaa, Murrinh-Patha, Yélî Dnye and **Argentine Sign Language (LSA)**. — [Dingemanse et al. 2015, PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4573759/)
- **Rate** **[primary]**: "In the 48.5 exhaustively sampled hours of conversation we find 2053 cases" of OIR. "There is a repair initiation about once every 1.4 minutes across all languages." "95% of repair initiations happen within 4.13 minutes of the last one." (That is about 42 per hour.) — [Dingemanse et al. 2015, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0136100)
- **Per-language counts** **[primary, absence]**: Table 1 gives only language, family, location and researcher. Hours and case counts per language are not in the article, and it reports no significant cross-language difference in rate. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4573759/)
- **English** **[primary]**: Kendrick 2015 analysed 15 recordings totalling 411 minutes of informal UK/US conversation. A broad collection of 278 candidates was reduced to a core of 227 OIRs, about one every 1.8 minutes. Uses of OIR formats for actions other than repair, such as surprise or disagreement, were excluded. — [Kendrick 2015, Open Linguistics (PDF)](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf)
- **British National Corpus** **[primary]**: Purver, Ginzburg & Healey 2001 used a subcorpus of about 150,000 words (200-turn sections from 59 transcripts). "CRs were found to make up just under 4% of sentences when calculated over the demographic [everyday] portion, or just under 3% when calculated over all domains." Only one expert coder was used, with no reliability statistic. — [Purver et al. 2001, SIGDIAL (PDF)](https://aclanthology.org/W01-1616.pdf)
- **Locality** **[primary]**: in the BNC, "the vast majority of CRs" target the immediately preceding sentence. Over 96% have a distance of 4 sentences or less, and the maximum seen was 15. — [Purver et al. 2001](https://aclanthology.org/W01-1616.pdf)
- **Finnish, with hearing impairment** **[primary via fetch]**: Pajo & Laakso 2023 recorded 14 dyads over 12 h 24 min. Three normal-hearing dyads (4 h 7 min) produced 60 verbal OIRs, about 1 per 4 minutes. Of these, 10 were open and 50 restricted (17% open). Dyads with severe hearing impairment produced 89 OIRs, 30 of them open (34%). Per-group durations were not extracted, so per-group rates cannot be computed. — [Pajo & Laakso 2023, Frontiers in Communication](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2023.1173179/full)
- **The "Huh?" study** **[primary]**: Dingemanse, Torreira & Enfield 2013 collected 196 OIR interjections in 10 languages: Cha'palaa, Icelandic, Spanish, Siwu, Dutch, Italian, Russian, Lao, Mandarin and Murrinh-Patha (mean 19.6 per language, sd 7.5). A literature survey found similar interjections in 31 languages. All tokens are a single syllable: optionally [h] or [ʔ], then a low, front-central vowel, with questioning intonation. The authors attribute this to convergent cultural evolution under the shared demands of repair. — [Dingemanse et al. 2013, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0078273)
- **Timing** **[secondary]**: Kendrick found that OIRs tend to come after gaps of about 700 ms, longer than typical turn transitions. — [Kendrick 2015, Frontiers in Psychology (timing)](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/116178/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR_timing.pdf) (figure from search summary, not checked)

### Inferences
- These rates count only troubles that someone noticed and flagged. They are a lower bound on how often transmission fails.
- The rate is similar across languages that differ a lot in phonology, morphology and modality. That suggests the main drivers are not features of any one grammar. Noise, attention and the cost of reference are the likelier drivers (see Q2). A designed language should not expect to remove the need for repair, only to change its mix and cost.
- Counting method changes the numbers. The 2015 cross-linguistic study excluded non-repair uses of OIR formats. Purver et al. counted all clarification requests in transcripts, including "Why?" and surprise readings. The two rates are not directly comparable.

### Gaps
- No per-language rates or confidence intervals were found in the open text of Dingemanse et al. 2015. They may be in its supplementary data, which was not checked.
- Colman & Healey 2011 ("The distribution of repair in dialogue", CogSci) reportedly gives BNC rates for self-repair and other-initiated repair by context. Its full text was not retrievable, so no figures are given here.
- Enfield et al. 2013, "Huh? What? – A first survey in twenty-one languages" (book chapter), was not accessed.

## Q2. What trouble sources trigger repair, and how often does each occur?

### Takeaway
**No large cross-linguistic study gives a clean frequency breakdown of trouble-source types (hearing vs. word meaning vs. reference vs. unexpected content).** This is a deliberate methodological choice. Conversation analysts code what the repair *format* does, because the same "huh?" can serve a hearing, understanding or acceptability problem, and speakers often present one kind as another. The best quantified evidence is indirect:
- Hearing and attention problems push people toward open formats. Open formats go from 39% at baseline to about 76–78% under noise, overlap or distraction.
- Restricted formats that target reference (who/what/which/where) are common, and person, thing and place are the main categories.
- In English, about half of clarification requests query the whole message, about a third query whether a word was heard right, and about 15% query what a constituent means or refers to.

### Cited Findings
- **Trouble-prone contexts, in 12 languages** **[primary]**: Dingemanse et al. 2015 modelled the probability that a repair initiator is open (e.g. "huh?"). Baseline, with no trouble-prone factor present: P(open) = 39%. Noise interference: 78%. Overlap: 76%. Recipient busy with a parallel activity: 76% (all p < 0.0001). When all three are present, P(open) "approaches 1 in all of the languages." When the trouble source is an *answer* rather than a question, P(open) drops to 9%. In that case the recipient knows what they asked and can pinpoint the problem. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4573759/)
- **No mapping from format to trouble type** **[primary, absence]**: the 2015 paper does not code trouble types (hearing vs. understanding vs. reference). It works with trouble-prone *contexts* instead. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4573759/)
- **Why trouble types resist coding** **[primary]**: Dingemanse, Blythe & Dirksmeyer 2014 argue that the classic "speaking, hearing, understanding" triad (Schegloff, Jefferson & Sacks 1977) "conflate[s] some important distinctions". For example, "the problem of recognising a word as a name, and that of knowing who this name refers to, are two distinct problems of understanding." They use Clark's action ladder instead: (1) attending to the signal, (2) identifying the words, (3) interpreting meaning and reference, (4) recognising the intended action. Each level "can be a locus of trouble". Evidence of success at a higher level is evidence of success at all lower levels ("downward evidence"). — [Dingemanse, Blythe & Dirksmeyer 2014, Studies in Language (PDF)](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_3048557_7/component/file_3149644/content)
- **An earlier four-way typology** **[primary, cited within Dingemanse et al. 2014]**: Selting 1987, working on German, distinguished four trouble types: "acoustic", "reference", "local meaning" and "local expectation". These map closely onto the requested categories (mishearing, reference, word meaning, unexpected content). No frequencies were found for them. — [Dingemanse et al. 2014, note 2](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_3048557_7/component/file_3149644/content)
- **Troubles get disguised as hearing problems** **[primary, cited within Dingemanse et al. 2014]**: Svennevig 2008 studied a mixed informal and institutional Norwegian corpus. He found that "problems of acceptability and understanding may often initially be addressed as hearing problems" (a preference for "trying the easiest solution first"). Coding trouble type from the repair format therefore over-counts hearing. — [Dingemanse et al. 2014](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_3048557_7/component/file_3149644/content)
- **Question-word categories in repair** **[primary]**: across the languages in the MPI sample, "the categories person, thing, and place seem to predominate" in restricted repair. These are also the most frequent question-word categories in conversation generally (Enfield, Stivers & Levinson 2010). No percentages were given. — [Dingemanse et al. 2014](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_3048557_7/component/file_3149644/content)
- **BNC proxy for trouble level** **[primary]**: Purver et al. 2001 coded the "reading" of each clarification request. Nearly 50% were *clausal*: "are you asking/asserting X?", querying the whole message or intent. About 35% were *lexical*: "did you utter X?", querying whether a word was identified correctly, which is roughly a hearing or word-identification problem. About 15% were *constituent*: "what/who do you mean by X?", querying meaning or reference. Only 0.5% fell outside the scheme, and those expressed surprise, amusement or outrage. — [Purver et al. 2001](https://aclanthology.org/W01-1616.pdf)
- **English OIR inventory** **[primary]**: Kendrick 2015, Table 1 (n = 227):

  | Format | Share of OIRs |
  |---|---|
  | Open | 18.5% (what? and huh? 17 each; what's that? 4; apology 3; what did you say? 1) |
  | Category-specific interrogatives (who?/what?/which?, alone or with a repeat) | 18.1% |
  | Repeats | 17.6% (partial 65%, full 22.5%, incomplete 12.5%) |
  | Copular interrogatives ("who's X?", "what's X?") | 4.0% |
  | Candidate understandings ("you mean X?") | 28.2% |
  | Other-corrections | 5.7% |
  | Other / multiple | about 7% |

  "Who's X?"-type copular interrogatives are suited to "troubles with the recognition of noun phrases, especially proper names". — [Kendrick 2015 (PDF)](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf)
- **English, collapsed to the cross-linguistic scheme** **[primary]**: n = 175. Open 23.4%, restricted 76.6% (of the restricted: request type 44.8%, offer type 55.2%). — [Kendrick 2015, Table 2](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf)
- **Hearing loss shifts the mix** **[primary via fetch]**: in Finnish, the share of open OIRs was 17% in normal-hearing dyads and 34% in severe-hearing-loss dyads. This fits open formats tracking perception problems. — [Pajo & Laakso 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2023.1173179/full)

### Inferences
- A rough ranking from the proxies, not from direct trouble-type coding:
  1. **Perception and attention failures** account for a large share of open repair. Open repair is about 18–25% of OIR in English and roughly a third of OIR overall, and it rises sharply under noise, overlap and distraction. These are cheap to fix: a repeat usually resolves them, and the trouble-source speaker repeats verbatim.
  2. **Reference identification** (who/which/where; "who's X?") is the most clearly named *understanding* problem in the literature. In English about 15% of clarification requests query constituent meaning or reference. Many candidate understandings (28% of English OIR) are checks of who or what was meant.
  3. **Lexical-identity checks** ("did you say X?", about 35% of BNC clarification requests) mix hearing problems with surprise at the word chosen.
  4. **Expectation and acceptability problems** are real but systematically disguised as hearing or understanding problems (Svennevig). They are also excluded from OIR counts when they read as disagreement or surprise. They are therefore *under*-counted by every method found.
- Requirement implication for a faithful-transmission language: the problems that come up often and are hard to localise are acoustic or attentional loss and referent identification. Unfamiliar *words* appear much less as a named category in natural conversation among intimates. That last point may not hold between strangers or across expertise gaps.

### Gaps
- **No primary study found that gives cross-linguistic frequencies of trouble-source types** (hearing / lexical / reference / expectation). Dingemanse, Kendrick & Enfield 2016 ("A coding scheme for other-initiated repair across languages", Open Linguistics) may include a trouble-source variable. It was not accessed.
- A 2025 RLSI article, "Negotiating shared understanding: Coding repair in social interaction", looks directly relevant to trouble-type coding but returned HTTP 403. Authors and figures are unverified. — [T&F link](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/08351813.2025.2528495)
- Rodríguez & Schlangen 2004 (German task-oriented dialogue, clarification requests classified by Clark-ladder level) and Schlangen 2004 ("Causes and strategies for requesting clarification in dialogue") are known to classify clarification requests by source level. Their figures were not retrieved.

## Q3. Which repair formats are used, and what do they show about the kind of information lost?

### Takeaway
In every language studied, people use three basic formats: open request ("huh?"), restricted request ("who?") and restricted offer ("you mean X?"). Together these make up a mean of 92% of OIR cases. People choose the most specific format they can. Restricted formats outnumber open ones by roughly 2–3 to 1, which means the listener can usually pinpoint which element was lost. The sequence also redistributes effort: more specific initiators cost the initiator more and the speaker less.

### Cited Findings
- **Formats** **[primary]**: "Three main practices for repair initiation recur across all of the languages in our sample: (I) open request…'Huh?'; (II) restricted request…'Who?'; (III) restricted offer…'she had a boy?'". They make up "the vast majority of cases of other-initiated repair (mean 92% σ 4.5%)". — [Dingemanse et al. 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0136100)
- **Cost by format** **[primary]**: mean length in orthographic characters is open 3.7, restricted request 10.4, restricted offer 13.0. "B pays more of the cost as repair initiators become more specific" (division of labour). Participants "choose the most specific repair initiator possible" (specificity). — [Dingemanse et al. 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0136100)
- **Repetition** **[primary]**: 48.3% of repair-initiator tokens repeat part (42.6%) or all (5.7%) of the trouble-source turn. Repeating pinpoints the trouble at the cost of length. — [Dingemanse et al. 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0136100)
- **Conservation** **[primary]**: a two-turn repair sequence is about as long as the one-turn trouble source (model estimate 1.2:1). — [Dingemanse et al. 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0136100)
- **English** **[primary]**: "only 18.5% are open, in line with the preference for specificity". Candidate understanding is the most frequent single format (28.2%). Open OIRs almost always come straight after the trouble turn (90.5%), while "who's X?" copular interrogatives rarely do (22.2%). Reference problems often surface turns later. — [Kendrick 2015](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf)
- **Implicature by contrast** **[primary]**: Sidnell 2007 found three "who" formats in Bequia Creole. Bare *huu* ("who?") is treated as a hearing problem. *huu X* ("who X?") flags a referring expression that picks out more than one person. *huu neem so* ("who is named so?") flags failure to recognise the person. Because the more specialised formats exist, choosing bare "who?" implies the problem is lower on the action ladder, i.e. hearing. — [Dingemanse et al. 2014](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_3048557_7/component/file_3149644/content)
- **BNC form distribution** **[primary]**: conventional forms ("What?", "Pardon?", "Eh?") and reprise fragments each make up over 25% of clarification requests. Non-reprise clarification requests and reprise sluices ("Who?" or "What X?" echo questions) each make up over 10%. — [Purver et al. 2001](https://aclanthology.org/W01-1616.pdf)

### Inferences
- Open requests flag problems at the signal or attention level ("I lost the whole thing"). Restricted requests flag one element whose identity didn't transfer, most often a person, thing or place. Restricted offers flag that the listener has a hypothesis but wants it confirmed, i.e. an inference gap. That restricted formats dominate (about 75%) means most trouble is *local* to one constituent, usually a referring expression.
- A language aiming for faithful transmission could build in what the repair system already does: cheap ways to point at a single constituent, and formats that give a candidate reading. Natural languages have independently converged on both.

### Gaps
- Per-language format proportions for the 12-language sample were not in the open text.
- Enfield et al. 2013 (21-language survey) was not accessed.

## Q4. Do signed conversations fail differently?

### Takeaway
Repair in Argentine Sign Language follows the same principles: it is one of the 12 languages in the universal-principles study, and its restricted formats are about twice as frequent as open ones. It also has a common *implicit* format: freezing in place while holding gaze on the signer (10% of cases). Between signers with no shared language ("cross-signing"), open repair nearly disappears. Almost all repair targets specific signs whose meaning is not shared.

### Cited Findings
- **Argentine Sign Language corpus** **[primary via fetch]**: Manrique & Enfield 2015 used 1 h 50 min of conversation with 59 signers (ages 20–80). Of 213 OIR cases: explicit open, non-manual 51 (23%); explicit open, question-sign 13 (6%); explicit restricted 126 (59%); implicit "freeze-look" 23 (10%). "Restricted type repair initiators are nearly twice as frequent as the open type." About half of freeze-looks (11 of 23) were followed by explicit repair when the freeze failed. Freeze durations ranged from 0.3 to 6.3 s, with 69% between 0.5 and 3 s. — [Manrique & Enfield 2015, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01326/full)
- **Rate** (derived): 213 cases in 110 minutes is about 1 every 0.5 minutes. That is roughly 2.7 times the 12-language average. It may reflect corpus selection, multi-party settings or counting differences rather than modality. The paper was not checked for whether it states this comparison. — computed from [Manrique & Enfield 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01326/full)
- **LSA in the universal sample** **[primary]**: LSA is among the 12 languages in Dingemanse et al. 2015, and the three-format system and specificity principle hold across the sample. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4573759/)
- **Manrique 2016** **[secondary]**: describes signers' troubles as "troubles of signing, seeing and understanding", with the same 213-case collection. — [MPI record](https://www.mpi.nl/publications/item2263023/other-initiated-repair-argentine-sign-language)
- **Cross-signing** **[secondary]**: Byun et al. 2018 studied deaf signers of different sign languages meeting for the first time. Only 3 of 51 OIR sequences were open, a strong preference for restricted initiators. Signers often mark a sign with "try markers" (repetition, gestural holds, prosodic lengthening, gaze at the addressee) to invite recognition, and this speeds up responses. — [Byun et al. 2018, Topics in Cognitive Science](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/tops.12303) (full text returned 403; the 3/51 figure is from a search summary)
- **Cross-signing clarification strategies** **[secondary]**: borrowing a sign from another sign language, fingerspelling, mouthing, drawing or writing in the air, and circumlocution with highly iconic signs or gestures. — [Byun, de Vos, Zeshan & Levinson 2020, in *Sign Multilingualism*](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9781501503528-002/html?lang=en)

### Inferences
- In same-language signing the failure profile looks similar to speech. The visual channel adds a cheap, silent "freeze" initiator, and "seeing" stands in for "hearing" as the perception-level failure.
- Cross-signing is a natural experiment in a lexicon that is *not* shared. When the signal arrives clearly but the code is not shared, nearly all repair becomes restricted and targets specific lexical items (about 94% in Byun's 51 cases). In same-language conversation, unknown words are a minor source of trouble. When there is no shared convention, they become the main one. That is relevant to how a designed language should be adopted, not only to its design.

### Gaps
- Byun et al. 2018 (Wiley) and the 2020 chapter could not be read in full. Rates per minute, trouble-source breakdowns and the size of the corpus were not verified. A search summary gave "340 minutes", but that may describe a different paper about colour terms, so it is not used here.
- No study comparing OIR rates in signed vs. spoken conversation under matched conditions was found.

## Q5. Reference failures (person and place)

### Takeaway
Reference is the best-documented *understanding* trouble. Several languages have dedicated repair formats for it ("who's X?", Bequia Creole's three "who" formats, Murrinh-Patha noun-class-specific "what?"). Repair on person, thing and place dominates restricted repair. No cross-linguistic study giving percentages for reference repair was found.

### Cited Findings
- Person, thing and place are the main categories of restricted repair across the MPI sample. — [Dingemanse et al. 2014](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_3048557_7/component/file_3149644/content)
- Three distinct ways person reference fails, each with its own Bequia Creole format: not heard; a referring expression with more than one candidate referent; the referent not recognised. — [Dingemanse et al. 2014, citing Sidnell 2007](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_3048557_7/component/file_3149644/content)
- In Murrinh-Patha, "what?" carries noun-class marking (e.g. *thanggumi* for the vegetable class, *thanggugu* for the animate class). The repair initiator itself narrows down the category of the referent that went missing. — [Dingemanse et al. 2014](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_3048557_7/component/file_3149644/content)
- In Siwu, a place reference built on a kin-based person reference ("his sibling's lot's house") got a repair initiation showing "that a person reference was heard but that it was insufficient to achieve reference". — [Dingemanse et al. 2014](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_3048557_7/component/file_3149644/content)
- In English, "who's X?" and "what's X?" copular interrogatives (4% of OIR) are "well suited to resolve troubles with the recognition of noun phrases, especially proper names". Unlike open OIR, they usually come after one or more intervening turns. "What"/"which" used as determiners locate a definite reference as the trouble, and "which" specifically flags failure to identify a *unique* referent. — [Kendrick 2015](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf)
- In the BNC, about 15% of clarification requests have a constituent reading ("who/what do you mean by X?"). — [Purver et al. 2001](https://aclanthology.org/W01-1616.pdf)

### Inferences
- Reference fails in three separable ways: perception of the form, uniqueness of the form, and recognition of the referent. Recognition failure tends to be detected *late*, after intervening turns, so it is costlier than a mishearing. A design aimed at faithful transmission could mark explicitly whether the speaker presumes the listener knows the referent, and could make referring expressions unique by construction.

### Gaps
- No percentages were found for person- vs. place- vs. thing-reference repair across languages. Stivers, Enfield & Levinson (eds.) 2007, *Person Reference in Interaction*, may hold per-language data. It was not accessed.

## Q6. Misunderstandings that go undetected

### Takeaway
Corpus repair research cannot by design count undetected misunderstandings. Experiments show the gap is substantial: speakers think they were understood more often than they were, even when they know the sentence was ambiguous. Third-position repair (correcting a misunderstanding revealed by the listener's response) is well described qualitatively. **No reliable figure for its corpus frequency was found.**

### Cited Findings
- **Keysar & Henly 2002** **[secondary]**: 40 speaker–listener pairs, 12 syntactically and 4 lexically ambiguous sentences. Speakers overestimated how well they had been understood. Syntactic ambiguity: thought 77%, actual 67%. Lexical ambiguity: thought 69%, actual 57%. An overhearer who knew the intended meaning did not overestimate in the same way. — [Keysar & Henly 2002, Psychological Science](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/1467-9280.00439) (percentages from a search summary, not checked against the paper)
- **Schober & Conrad 1997** **[secondary]**: in a Bureau of Labor Statistics lab study with scenarios whose correct answers were known, letting interviewers clarify concepts reduced comprehension errors in survey answers. This was especially so for "complicated mappings", i.e. cases where respondents did not know they needed help. Accuracy figures were not retrieved. — [Conrad, FCSM 1999](https://nces.ed.gov/FCSM/pdf/V_B_Conrad2_FCSM1999.pdf); [Conrad, FCSM 2001](https://nces.ed.gov/FCSM/pdf/2001FCSM_Conrad.pdf)
- **Healey et al. 2018** **[secondary]**: an experiment that replaced specific clarification requests with generic "huh?" signals ("amplification") pushed participants to quickly switch to more abstract referring expressions. The kind of repair feedback shapes how reference conventions form. — [Healey, Mills, Eshghi & Howes 2018, Topics in Cognitive Science](https://research.rug.nl/en/publications/running-repairs-coordinating-meaning-in-dialogue/)
- **The repair counts exclude some disagreement** **[primary]**: the BNC "other" readings were all surprise, amusement or outrage. Kendrick's core collection excluded OIR formats used as disagreement. OIR formats are "apt and suitable instruments for addressing disagreement" (Schegloff 2007, quoted). — [Purver et al. 2001](https://aclanthology.org/W01-1616.pdf); [Kendrick 2015](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/116184/1/Kendrick_2015_OIR.pdf)

### Inferences
- If Keysar & Henly's figures hold, about 10–12 percentage points of "I was understood" are illusory on ambiguous input. Natural repair cannot catch these, because neither side notices. A design goal of faithful transmission should therefore target *undetectable* ambiguity, not only the detectable kind that repair already handles cheaply.

### Gaps
- No corpus frequency of third-position repair (Schegloff 1992, "Repair after next turn") was found. Background knowledge says it is far rarer than OIR, but that is **[unverified]** and no figure is given.
- No cross-linguistic study of undetected misunderstanding in natural conversation was found. The available evidence is English-language lab experiments.
