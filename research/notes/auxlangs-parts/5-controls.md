# 5. Controls: Tok Pisin, ICAO English, Simplified Technical English, Hebrew, Indonesian

Working notes for the Apupiwe auxlang report. Controls = natural or institutional cases that test what design/planning can and cannot do. Every claim cited inline; [unverified] = not checked against a source in this run.

## 5.1 Tok Pisin (a reduced contact language that children took over)

**Why it is a control:** the nearest natural experiment to "give children a deliberately small, regular, mostly-isolating language and see what they do with it". Nobody designed it, but its pidgin stage had the properties many engineered languages aim for (small root lexicon, compounding/circumlocution, invariant words, few obligatory categories).

### Origin
- Early Pacific Pidgin English used on Sydney-based trading/whaling ships from the early 19th c.; a distinctly Melanesian variety crystallised with the Pacific labour trade from 1863 (Queensland plantations), then Samoan plantations 1879-1912; labourers returning to New Britain/New Ireland spread it as a lingua franca in German New Guinea, absorbing German and Tolai (Patpatar-Tolai group) words and likely substrate structure. Source: Smith, Geoff P. & Jeff Siegel. 2013. "Tok Pisin". In Michaelis et al. (eds.), *Atlas of Pidgin and Creole Language Structures* (APiCS Online), survey ch. 22, https://apics-online.info/surveys/22

### Lexicon: size and sources
- English is the main lexifier; also Portuguese (*pikinini* 'child', *save* 'know'), Malay (*binatang* 'insect', *lombo* 'chilli'), Samoan (*malolo* 'rest'), German, and Tolai/Kuanua. APiCS gives no percentages (Smith & Siegel 2013, https://apics-online.info/surveys/22). German examples *beten* 'pray', *raus*, *borim* 'drill' and Kuanua *lapun* 'old' are cited from Mühlhäusler 2003 by Tung (student paper, Boston College *Lingua*, https://ejournals.bc.edu/index.php/lingua/article/view/5419).
- Lexicon size: I found no reliable count. The often repeated "Mihalic's basic vocabulary is only about 2000 words" comes from a Swedish undergraduate essay that gives no count method (Feldmanis, diva-portal, https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:828733/FULLTEXT01.pdf). **[unverified: treat as folklore until checked against Mihalic 1971 itself.]**
- Standard dictionary: Mihalic, Frank. 1971. *The Jacaranda Dictionary and Grammar of Melanesian Pidgin*. Milton, Qld: Jacaranda. Its spelling is the most influential orthography, spread through the SVD mission's Word Publishing and its weekly *Wantok Niuspepa* (Smith & Siegel 2013, https://apics-online.info/surveys/22). Full Bible (*Buk Baibel*) by the Bible Society of PNG, © 1969-2008 per eBible (https://ebible.org/find/details.php?id=tpiOTNT). New Testament 1966, full Bible 1989 per bookseller listings only **[unverified]**.

### Grammar (selected)
All from Smith & Siegel 2013 (APiCS, https://apics-online.info/surveys/22):
- Isolating/analytic. Predicate marker *i* between subject and predicate: *Ol man i bin pait-im garamut* 'The men beat the slit gong'.
- Transitive suffix *-im* (< *him*): *taim ol luk-im ol, ol kil-im ol* 'when they saw them they killed them'.
- Inclusive/exclusive 1pl: *yumi* 'we incl. you' vs *mipela* 'we excl. you'.
- TAM: *bin* past (< *been*), *bai* future/irrealis (< *by and by*, earlier *baimbai*), *pinis* completive (< *finish*).
- Relative clauses: *husat* 'who' and *we* 'where' extended to relative pronouns, first in texts translated from English.

### What happened when children acquired it natively
- **Sankoff & Laberge** (Sankoff, Gillian & Suzanne Laberge. 1973. "On the acquisition of native speakers by a language". *Kivung* 6(1): 32-47; reprinted in DeCamp & Hancock eds. 1974 and in Sankoff 1980 *The Social Life of Language*): *baimbai* > *bai* > *ba*/*bə*. It moved next to the verb, lost stress and shrank phonologically, ending up as a tense marker rather than an optional sentence adverb (summarised in Smith & Siegel 2013, https://apics-online.info/surveys/22). Children gave *bai* less stress than adults; the comparison was nine child native speakers and nine adult non-native speakers. Counts of "395 tokens, 5 *baimbai*, none from children" come from a search-engine summary, not the paper **[unverified]**.
- Sankoff & Laberge's general claim, quoted in Samarin 1993: children "speak with much greater speed and fluency, involving a number of morphophonemic reductions", and native speakers "tend to have little patience with ... a language with few grammatical categories, limited lexicon, virtually no morphophonemic reduction rules, insufficient redundancy, and little opportunity for stylistic manoeuvre" (Sankoff & Laberge 1974: 76, quoted in Samarin, W. J. 1993, "The creolization of pidgin morphophonology", SPCL Amsterdam conference paper, U Toronto repository, https://utoronto.scholaris.ca/server/api/core/bitstreams/3bbd3c9c-c094-4147-9ffb-9b25f6d18d40/content).
- **Counterpoint, Samarin 1993 (same source):** in Tok Pisin some of these changes **predate** nativisation. The driver is people using the language as their *primary* means of communication, not childhood acquisition as such. The claim that native speakers talk faster had not been systematically measured. Relevance for Apupiwe: heavy adult use may be enough to start reduction, before any children are involved.
- **Smith 2002** (Smith, Geoff P. *Growing up with Tok Pisin: Contact, Creolization and Change in Papua New Guinea's National Language*. London: Battlebridge): L1 speakers' fast speech reduces citation forms, causing new **homophony**. It reduces adjectival *-pela* and the complex pronouns (*mipela*, *yupela*...) and drops the final consonant of transitive *-im*, especially in the Momase region (summary of review, https://www.researchgate.net/publication/263574036; Cambridge SSLA review, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/growing-up-with-tok-pisin-contact-creolization-and-change-in-papua-new-guineas-national-language-geoff-p-smith-london-battlebridge-2002-pp-xii-244-1800-paper/81EE00B49D83F9292359B91EB274E6B0). Plural *-s* is "by now common in the speech of first language speakers, even if not yet consistently used" (Smith 2002: 75f., quoted in Zimmermann 2010: 184, below). Smith & Siegel also note "increasing use of obligatory marking of plurals with -s among some speakers" (APiCS). Native speakers **added an obligatory category**, plural, that the pidgin left optional (pidgin plural was *ol* + N).
- **Romaine 1992** (Romaine, Suzanne. *Language, Education and Development: Urban and Rural Tok Pisin in Papua New Guinea*. Oxford: OUP): change among young urban and rural native speakers. Reported to find 195 words taking *-s* plural and *ov* beginning to replace *bilong* in urban speech. Both come from a search summary and are **[unverified against the book]**. Back-formations like *traus* from *trausis* (Romaine 1992a: 238, cited in Zimmermann 2010).
- Mühlhäusler (1985, below): **educated speakers replace reduplicated forms** such as *tingting* 'think', *toktok* 'talk' and *lukluk* 'look' with unreduplicated ones. His reason is that reduplication feels childish or lighthearted. Zimmermann (2010: 185) disagrees: reduplication is still productive in *Wantok*, with 61 constructions.
- APiCS adds that L1 speakers reduce stressed syllables considerably, e.g. *ol* severely reduced in fast speech (https://apics-online.info/surveys/22).

### Circumlocution vs borrowing
- **Mühlhäusler, P. 1985.** "The scientific study of Tok Pisin: language planning and the Tok Pisin lexicon". In Wurm & Mühlhäusler (eds.), *Handbook of Tok Pisin*, Pacific Linguistics C-70: 595-664. DOI 10.15144/PL-C70.595, open PDF: https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/b72400ac-f17c-41f5-80db-710196c7b1a2/content. Key points:
  - "Circumlocutions thus are best regarded as a temporary stage in lexical expansion ... Circumlocutions involving five or more existing words are not felt to stand for single concepts and thus act as distractors rather than facilitators to communication" (p. 603-4).
  - He rejects the strong claim that "Pidgin can express anything that English can express, fully and without ambiguity" (Dietz 1956). Example: officials could not translate *majority rule* precisely; the compromise *bihainim tingting bilong planti pipel* means only 'follow the opinion of many people' (Wolfers 1971: 416, quoted p. 603).
  - Against Sadler's (1974) purist native-coinage proposals, e.g. *wok bilong kamapim diwai* for 'forestry', *man bilong narapela kantri* for 'expatriate', *man i mekim hanwok* for 'artist': such circumlocutions "fail to provide the degree of exactitude required for such technical terms", and fixing them as lexical items "would lead to a drastic increase in the complexity of Tok Pisin surface structure"; long compounds "put considerable strain on the speaker's short-term memory" (p. 658).
  - Second-language users need a regular lexicon "favouring derivation and compounding over borrowing", but bilingual urban speakers favour borrowing (p. 596). Planning by "untrained naive speakers" is "hit-and-miss" (p. 595).
- **Zimmermann, Janna Lisa. 2010.** *The Increasing Anglicisation of Tok Pisin: An Analysis of the Wantok Corpus*. PhD, Univ. Regensburg. 250,482-token corpus of *Wantok* 1972-2004, https://epub.uni-regensburg.de/19705/4/Diss_JZ_20-02-2011.pdf. Findings (pp. 182-186):
  - Adapted English loans displace transparent constructions: *wokim haus* > *bildim*; *kamap bikpela* > *gro*; *haus mani* 'bank' no longer occurs, replaced by *bank/beng/benk* (89 tokens).
  - Circumlocutions are now few.
  - 25 English prefixes found (*eks-*, *ova-*), though the language was described as suffix-only.
  - Loans cause **homonymy**: *hat* = 'hard', 'hot', 'heart'; *pes* = 'face', 'first', 'page'.
  - Opaque loans confused non-English speakers: readers thought *nesenel senses* 'national census' meant *nesenel ileksen* 'national election'.
  - No language planning institute exists.
  - Romaine (1990: 198): "English is borrowed simply because English has more prestige" even when pidgin words exist.

### Speakers and status
- 3-5 million speakers, mostly L2, with "a considerable population of first language speakers (up to 500,000)" (Smith & Siegel 2013, APiCS, https://apics-online.info/surveys/22). Ethnologue (25th ed., 2022, via Wikipedia) gives 130,000 L1 (2004-2016) and 4,000,000 L2 (https://www.ethnologue.com/language/tpi/). The L1 estimates differ about fourfold, and neither is a census count.
- Status: usually called one of three official languages with English and Hiri Motu, but the Constitution "does not state what the national language or languages ... should be". It mentions Pisin for literacy goals, for citizenship (conversational ability in Tok Pisin, Hiri Motu or a vernacular) and for parliamentary debate (Volker, Craig Alan. 2023-05-05. "What does the Constitution say about language?" *The National*, https://www.thenational.com.pg/what-does-the-constitution-say-about-language/). English remains the main language of education (Zimmermann 2010: 182).
- Standardisation is informal: the Mihalic spelling plus *Wantok* and church publishing. *Wantok*'s own stated aim was "to try and standardize tok pisin" (quoted in Zimmermann 2010: 186). No academy.

### Takeaways (Tok Pisin)
- Children and heavy primary users do the predictable things: phonological reduction of frequent function words, which become clitics or affixes (*baimbai* > *ba-*); **new obligatory marking** (tense via *bai*, plural *-s*); and new homophony from reduction.
- Circumlocution is a bootstrap. Once a concept is frequent, speakers replace a long description with a short form, usually a **prestige-language loan** where one is available, not with a native compound. A designed compounding lexicon will meet the same pressure.
- Planned purist coinages (Sadler 1974) failed against borrowing. The successful "standardisers" were a dictionary, a newspaper and Bible translation, i.e. widely read text, not an academy.
