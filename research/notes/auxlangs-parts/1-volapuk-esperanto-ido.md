## 1. Volapük (Schleyer, 1879-80)

**Aim and principles.**
- Schleyer's stated aims, as reported by his main English-language exponent: "first to produce a language capable of expressing thought with the greatest clearness and accuracy; second, to make its acquisition as easy as possible"; for grammar "the first requisite is regularity, and the second is simplicity" ([Sprague 1888, Introduction](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Hand-book_of_Volap%C3%BCk/Introduction)).
- Garvía calls it "a solution in search of a problem": Schleyer first designed a world phonetic alphabet (submitted to the Universal Postal Union, 1878), then a language to carry it, conceived in a night in March 1879 that he described as a quasi-revelation (Garvía 2015, pp. 21-23).
- Target users: Schleyer's first handbook was subtitled for the *Gebildete* (the educated), "scholars, travelers, and merchants"; meant as a written "new Latin" (Garvía 2015, p. 26; p. 158). Kerckhoffs and the French wing wanted a commercial code language instead: "a dictionary of 15,000 words, neither pleasant, nor unpleasant, but easy to learn for whoever has the key" (Kerckhoffs, quoted in Garvía 2015, p. 45). The movement never agreed on what the language was *for*.

**Designer and method.** One person, Johann Martin Schleyer (1831-1912), Catholic priest in Litzelstetten near Konstanz; first draft published in his Catholic poetry journal *Sionsharfe* (Garvía 2015, pp. 21-25). Schleyer knew nothing of the 17th-century philosophical languages and did not care to (Garvía 2015, p. 23).

**Sounds and writing.**
- Latin alphabet without q and w, plus ä, ö, ü; eight vowels a ä e i o ö u ü; c = /dʒ/, j = /ʃ/, z = /ts/; stress always on the last syllable ([Sprague 1888, Sounds and Letters](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Hand-book_of_Volap%C3%BCk/Sounds_and_Letters)).
- Root selection excluded "difficult and unusual sounds and combinations"; h avoided "because unpronounceable by some nations" ([Sprague 1888, Introduction](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Hand-book_of_Volap%C3%BCk/Introduction)). Schleyer used r sparingly; de Jong's 1931 revision "rehabilitated" /r/ [secondary: [Wikipedia, Arie de Jong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arie_de_Jong); not checked in de Jong's *Gramat Volapüka*].
- The umlauts were a target of French ridicule: "Was not Volapük, with all its inflections and umlauted vowels, a broken Teutonic language?" (Garvía 2015, p. 36, paraphrasing the French critics).

**Grammar type.** Agglutinative, heavily inflected, German-modelled.
- Four cases marked by vowel suffixes, nom. Ø, gen. -a, dat. -e, acc. -i; plural -s (*fat, fata, fate, fati; fats...*) ([Sprague 1888, Cases](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Hand-book_of_Volap%C3%BCk/Cases)).
- One conjugation; tense by vowel prefix (a- present, ä- past, e- perfect, i- pluperfect, o- future, u- future perfect) and person by suffix: *löfob* "I love", *älöfob* "I loved", *elöfob* "I have loved" ... ([Sprague 1888, Tenses](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Hand-book_of_Volap%C3%BCk/Tenses); Garvía 2015, p. 23). Plus passive, infinitive, participle, imperative, conditional, subjunctive, potential, interrogative, frequentative, reflexive forms (Sprague 1888, table of contents).
- Scale of the verb system: the stenographer Karl Lenze "had calculated that there were 505,440 ways of using the language's verbal modes", which some Volapükists took as proof of expressive power (Garvía 2015, p. 28). (Popular sources quote varying totals; this is the one with a traceable source.)
- Roots had to begin and end in a consonant so affixes (vowel-initial/final) were always separable (Garvía 2015, p. 23). This is a parsing-transparency rule, and the main cause of the root distortion below.

**Vocabulary.**
- Source: mostly English and German, some Romance. Sprague: from English "he took ... more root-words, with or without modification, than from any other, or about 40 per cent. of the whole" ([Sprague 1888, Introduction](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Hand-book_of_Volap%C3%BCk/Introduction)).
- Constraints (consonant-initial and -final, short, preferably English, collision-free in an 8-vowel/19-consonant inventory) made coining "a combinatorial challenge": *pük* (from "speak") because *pik* was taken; *vol* from "world" (Garvía 2015, p. 23). The result was recognisability loss: the American Philosophical Society committee (1888) judged Volapük "too inflectional and its vocabulary unrecognizable" (Garvía 2015, p. 42; report in [Proc. APS 25 (1888)](https://archive.org/details/sim_proceedings-of-the-american-philosophical-society_january-june-1888_25_127/sim_proceedings-of-the-american-philosophical-society_january-june-1888_25_127)).
- Root count at launch or later: **[unverified]**; I found no reliable count. Kerckhoffs spoke of a 15,000-word dictionary (Garvía 2015, p. 45), which counts words, not roots.
- Word formation: rich prefixing and suffixing, compounds joined by the genitive vowel (*vol-a-pük* "language of the world"); prefixes like *ne-* (negation), *le-* (intensive), *lu-* (pejorative/diminutive), *ge-* (back/re-) ([Sprague 1888, Prefixes](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Hand-book_of_Volap%C3%BCk/Prefixes); Garvía 2015, p. 23).

**Precision and ambiguity.** Schleyer aimed for "a complexity that would leave room for the smallest nuances and subtleties of human cognition"; Kerckhoffs wanted a code with "one-to-one functions" for unambiguous translation and argued the complexity "could ruin the language and the movement" (Garvía 2015, pp. 44-45). So precision-by-inflection was a design goal; the reformers' answer was to cut forms (four conditional tenses, gen./dat. suffixes replaced by prepositions) (Garvía 2015, p. 45).

**Learnability.** No experimental evidence found. Claims were rhetorical ("simplicity", Kirchhoff "captivated by the language's simplicity", Garvía 2015, p. 27). The APS (1888) and London Philological Society reports disagreed on its quality; the London society found the flaws minor but supported Volapük only for expediency, since it had "the ear of the public" (Garvía 2015, pp. 42-43).

**Speakers who changed it.** No native speakers. Users (Kerckhoffs's *Cours*, the most successful handbook, translated into 7 languages) simplified the grammar in practice before the Academy existed (Garvía 2015, pp. 30, 45).

**Governance and schism.**
- Built-in autocracy: the bylaws on the membership diploma made Schleyer "supreme leader"; a majority vote counted only if he voted with it (Garvía 2015, p. 25). Leader for life from the 1884 Friedrichshafen congress (~30 attendees) (Garvía 2015, pp. 32-33).
- Munich congress, Aug. 1887 (~200 delegates): an Academy was set up with Kerckhoffs as director, but Schleyer kept a veto (Garvía 2015, pp. 45-46).
- May 1888, Schleyer: "Any resolution of the Academy that has not been accepted by the Inventor is null, even if the whole of the membership united against the Inventor" (Garvía 2015, p. 47).
- Paris congress, Aug. 1889 (held in Volapük): the Academy could override his veto by a two-thirds vote. Schleyer rejected this, claimed the language as intellectual property and set up a rival academy of loyalists (Garvía 2015, pp. 47-48).
- After the split: Kerckhoffs received "nine other reform proposals" instead of answers and resigned in July 1890; the Academy stalled until 1893 (Rosenberger), then shut out the rank and file and eventually produced Idiom Neutral (1902) (Garvía 2015, pp. 48, 157). Meanwhile Schleyer purged dissenters and himself kept changing grammar and vocabulary, "which obliged his loyal supporters to purchase new dictionaries and unlearn old words" (Garvía 2015, p. 50).

**Adoption: rise and collapse.**
- Peak 1889: "253 local clubs, 14 journals, and almost 900 certified teachers" (Garvía 2015, p. 47). Kerckhoffs estimated about 210,000 people had *studied* it ([Sprague 1888, Introduction](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Hand-book_of_Volap%C3%BCk/Introduction)). The popular "nearly a million adherents" figure is a movement claim (Wikipedia attributes it to period sources); Garvía does not use it. Treat it as **[unverified/inflated]**.
- Membership profile (sample of 1,709 names, 1887-92): one-third German, 10-15% women, mostly Catholic, upper-middle-class, about a tenth of those reporting occupations held doctorates (Garvía 2015, pp. 25-26).
- Collapse: 50 of ~250 clubs active in 1894; 9 in 1905; the last two dissolved in 1909 (Garvía 2015, p. 50). The Nuremberg club under Leopold Einstein defected to Esperanto as early as 1888 (Garvía 2015, p. 47).
- Today: tiny. "An estimated 20 Volapük speakers" in 2000 [secondary: Paul LaFarge, "Pük, Memory", *Village Voice* 2000, via Wikipedia; not read].

**Design or governance? What historians say.**
- Garvía (the most careful history): governance first. "Those most responsible for the language's collapse were the Volapükists themselves" (p. 43); Schleyer's "top-down, authoritarian strategy ... proved suicidal"; reformers then spent years on a language that did not yet exist, "there was no point in propagating, learning, and teaching a language that did not yet exist", while Esperanto offered an alternative (pp. 156-157). He also rejects ranking auxlangs by learnability at all: their "proof of the pudding" is whether a community keeps using them (p. 154).
- Zamenhof changed his mind. In 1889 he said Volapük was doomed by its vocabulary and unpleasant sounds; at the 1911 congress he said it died not "because of its strange sounds ... since everybody can get used to anything" but because the quarrelling Volapükists did not let it evolve (Garvía 2015, p. 155).
- But design was the *content* of the quarrel: the reform fight was about the inflection and the unrecognisable roots (Kerckhoffs: "all its exuberance of grammatical forms and lacking clear word-formation rules", Garvía 2015, p. 45), and outsiders (APS, French press) attacked exactly those features (pp. 36, 42). My reading: design made reform pressure inevitable; governance turned reform pressure into a fatal split. Volapük also lacked an ideology to hold people through a crisis (Garvía 2015, p. 158).
