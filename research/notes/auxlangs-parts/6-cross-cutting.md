# Cross-cutting evidence: learnability, speaker counts, rise/fail sociology, coinage acceptance: working notes

Part 6 of the auxlangs report (`../auxlangs.md`). Started 2026-10-13. Labels: claims carry an inline link or full reference; **[unverified]** = not confirmed in a source I read; **[secondary]** = reported by a source I read, original not read.

## 1. Learnability studies of planned languages

### 1a. Esperanto "propaedeutic" studies (does learning Esperanto first speed up a later language?)

Quality key: **A** = peer-reviewed, has a control group, sample of a reasonable size, independent authors; **B** = peer-reviewed but small sample or weak controls; **C** = advocacy-published, uncontrolled, or the author has a conflict of interest; **?** = I couldn't read the original.

| Study | Design / N | Finding | Quality |

| Springboard to Languages (S2L), 2006-11 | Primary schools in England (Bishop Auckland area and others). Evaluated in 5 phases by Barton & Bragg (Univ. of Manchester), e.g. [Phase 5 report, 2011](https://research.manchester.ac.uk/en/publications/springboard-to-languages-project-evaluation-phase-5/), a commissioned 18-page report using think-aloud interviews. **Funded by the Esperantic Studies Foundation**, as was the evaluation ([ESF grant list](https://www.esperantic.org/en/grants/grant-recipients/)). Summary book: Tellier, A. 2012, [*Esperanto as a starter language…*](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/5989/), Esperanto UK, Stoke-on-Trent (publication funded by a $2,000 ESF grant) | Pupils "more prepared to engage with other languages". They saw Esperanto as "a tool for decoding other languages" | **C**. Descriptive, with no proper control group. Tellier and Roehr-Brackin themselves call it "encouraging but not scientifically conclusive" because it lacked one ([Roehr-Brackin & Tellier 2018](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/24243/)) |
| **Roehr-Brackin & Tellier, Essex, 2013-17** | [Roehr-Brackin & Tellier 2018, *LPLP* 42(1):89-111](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/24243/); [OASIS summary](https://ripl.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/RoehrBrackinTellierFinal.pdf). **Study 1**: 28 children aged 8-9 learned Esperanto or French, 45 min/week for a year. **Study 2**: 225 children aged 11-12 who had earlier learned Esperanto, French, German, Italian, Japanese, Latin or Spanish. **Study 3**: 178 children aged 8-9 did German, Italian, Esperanto, or Esperanto+grammar focus for 20 h over 16 weeks, then the same French course for 20 h | Study 1: Esperanto group gained 45% vs 14% for the French group in proficiency in their own target language (different tests, so a rough comparison only). Study 2: **no differences** between language groups in metalinguistic awareness. Study 3: **no differences in French outcomes**; Esperanto groups were "less differentiated" (a levelling effect); the grammar-focus group scored higher on language awareness. Conclusion: "learning Esperanto could not compensate for low language learning aptitude" | **A-/B+**. Peer-reviewed, has controls, the largest N. One author (Tellier) came from the ESF-funded S2L project. Short exposure: no study ran longer than a year |

**Verdict.** The only modern controlled work (Essex) finds Esperanto **easier to learn than French in the first year**. It finds **no measurable head start for the next language**. It also finds a levelling effect: weaker learners keep up better. The older positive results come almost entirely from movement-run or movement-funded studies with weak controls. The best old study (Halloran 1952) found no average benefit, only a benefit for lower-ability pupils. Independent reviews agree: Maxwell 1988, and Kunimune's 2022 survey (a blog, not peer-reviewed): "current science remains inconclusive".

### 1b. The "150 hours vs 1,500 hours" claim
- Widely repeated wording: "The Institute of Cybernetic Pedagogy at Paderborn has compared… French-speaking high-school students… 2,000 hours German, 1,500 English, 1,000 Italian, 150 Esperanto." English Wikipedia carried this tagged **[unreliable source?]** with no citation ([archived copy of the Esperanto article](https://static.hlt.bme.hu/semantics/external/pages/deontikus/en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esperanto.html)).
- The numbers **change between retellings**. One French-language variant gives 1,500 h German, 1,000 h English, 700 h Italian, 150 h Esperanto, dated "1987" (seen only in a search-engine summary [unverified]). The Italian advocacy appendix says "after about 176 h of Esperanto, the pupil reached the result obtained after about 1,500 h of English", citing Selten & Frank 2005 and Frank 2014 ([Madella](https://old.usr.sicilia.it/attachments/article/8070/Appendice_30_ore_d'oro.pdf)).
- Likely origin: Frank's own "relative learning ease" papers in his own journal. Frank, H. 1977, "Die Messmöglichkeit der relativen Schwierigkeit von Fremdsprachen", in Bink & Lobin (eds.), *Kybernetik und Bildung III*, pp. 14-25. Frank, H. 1979, "Zur relativen Lernleichtigkeit einiger Sprachen", *GrKG* 20(4):120-124 (both listed in Madella's bibliography). **I found no peer-reviewed independent publication of the hour table.** Its population (French-speaking lycée students) doesn't even match the Paderborn experiment (German primary pupils learning English).
- **Assessment:** an untraced, self-published figure from an author with a conflict of interest, quoted with drifting numbers. Treat it as advocacy, not evidence. It is entirely plausible that a regular language with ~16k roots shared with Romance and English is learned faster *to a beginner level*. The Essex Study 1 gain (45% vs 14%) points the same way, but it doesn't measure hours to a fixed standard.

### 1c. Other planned languages
- **Toki Pona.**
  - Coluzzi, P. 2022, ["How learning Toki Pona may help improving communication strategies…"](https://doi.org/10.1075/lplp.00086.col), *LPLP* 46(1):78-98. Six volunteer students at the University of Malaya, four Toki Pona classes. Students were marginally better at circumlocution afterwards. Pilot, no control.
  - Kitano, M. L. 2024, ["Teaching Toki Pona in Japan"](https://doi.org/10.37546/JALTPCP2023-27), JALT2023 proceedings. A university club: 7 students in spring, 2 continued into the fall. Data are a teaching log and a questionnaire. Students reported more awareness of parts of speech. No control group. Kitano repeats "said to take only 30 hours to learn", citing a popular source (Yuzbay 2023).
  - Mubin et al. 2009, CHI Extended Abstracts, ["Using language tests and emotional expressions to determine the learnability of artificial languages"](http://www.bartneck.de/publications/2009/learnabilityArtificialLanguages/index.html). Children played a game in Toki Pona vs Klingon. It's a methods paper for the ROILA robot-language project.
  - **No controlled learnability study of Toki Pona exists that I could find.** The "30 hours" figure is folklore.
- **Interlingua.** Claims that educated Romance speakers understand 80-90% of it on sight in IALA's 1940s tests appear only in AI or secondary summaries (e.g. Grokipedia) **[unverified]**. I found no primary report. Plausible for written Romance-heavy text, but unmeasured in anything I could read.
- **Basic English (Ogden).** I found **no controlled learnability study**. The evidence is advocacy and teaching practice, e.g. I. A. Richards's work in China ([Wikipedia: Basic English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_English), as a pointer).
- **Ido, Volapük, Lojban, Novial etc.**: I found no controlled learnability studies.

### 1d. Artificial-language-learning (ALL) lab evidence on regularity (high-quality psycholinguistics, but small-N lab miniature languages)
- **Hudson Kam & Newport 2005.** "Regularizing unpredictable variation: the roles of adult and child learners in language formation and change", *Language Learning and Development* 1:151-195 ([listing](https://blogs.ubc.ca/languageandlearninglab/publications/)). Adults tend to reproduce inconsistent input; children aged 5-6 regularize it.
- **Hudson Kam & Newport 2009.** ["Getting it right by getting it wrong: when learners change languages"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2703698/), *Cognitive Psychology* 59:30-66.
  - Exp 1 (N=60 adults): adults reproduce variation at low scatter. With 16 noise determiners they produce the main form ~90% of the time when the input had it only 60%.
  - Exp 2 (N=11): rare but *consistent* forms are learned veridically, ~95%.
  - Exp 3 (30 children aged 5-7, 16 adults): children "almost always" regularize.
  - → Lexically conditioned irregularity is learnable. *Unpredictable* variation gets flattened, especially by children.
- **Kirby, Cornish & Smith 2008.** ["Cumulative cultural evolution in the laboratory"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2504810/), *PNAS* 105(31):10681-6. Two experiments, each with 4 chains × 10 participants.
  - Languages became easier to learn over the generations.
  - Without pressure to stay expressive, they collapsed: 27 words → 2-5 words by generation 10, through underspecification.
  - When homonyms were filtered out, compositional structure emerged.
  - → Learnability alone drives languages toward ambiguity. Compositionality needs learnability *plus* expressivity.
- **Culbertson, Smolensky & Legendre 2012.** ["Learning biases predict a word order universal"](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2011.10.017), *Cognition* 122(3):306-329. Adult learners exposed to mixed Adj/Num/Noun orders regularized in ways that mirror Greenberg's Universal 18 (harmonic orders favoured; the typologically rare pattern disfavoured).
- **Fedzechkina, Jaeger & Newport 2012.** ["Language learners restructure their input to facilitate efficient communication"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3497763/), *PNAS* 109(44):17897-902.
  - Input: optional case marking at 60% with flexible SOV/OSV order (2 experiments, N=20 each analysed).
  - Learners used more case marking on atypical (animate) objects and in OSV sentences. Over training they came to mark inanimate (atypical) subjects more.
  - → Learners spontaneously invent differential marking where ambiguity is likely. This is a typologically common pattern.
- **Caveats.** These are mostly English-speaking undergraduates learning toy languages for minutes to days. They show the *direction* of learner biases, not the hours to fluency. Still, they are the best-controlled evidence relevant to "regular = learnable" and to *which* regularities are natural.
