# Review: the senary measurement system, and what this language should take from it

Written 2026-10-08. Sources: `~/work/repos/senary` (README, primer, specification, rationale, notes §6A–§6I, §11.5, §17–§18, both reviews, `research/*.py`, `src/verify.py`). I ran B's three research scripts and its verify suite, and ran a quick juncture check on this repo's word lists (script kept in `/tmp/junc/`, not committed). **[inferred]** marks conclusions I drew rather than read.

## 1. What B is

A coherent base-six measurement system, "built as a worldbuilding artefact". In the lore it is a metrology designed from scratch by people with **no legacy system to migrate from**, so "upheaval cost, backward compatibility and 'practitioners are used to it' are void as arguments". The binding constraint: a physicist must be able to work in it rigorously, and a layman must find it easy.

Scope: six dimensions (time, length, mass, charge, temperature, angle). Five SI defining constants are set to exact powers of six; the only arbitrary anchor is the day = 6⁷ Kena. It covers 20 named units, a prefix ladder stepping by 6⁴, spoken numerals, a 7-digit decimal-fraction-of-the-day clock, zero-based day-number dates, a six-day week, and coordinates as fractions of a turn.

Audiences: technical readers (the Specification, 37 KB), laymen (the Primer, 12 KB), future designers (the Rationale, 76 KB); the 124 KB of notes are written for an LLM.

Key decisions: base six ("ten multiplication facts"; more fractions terminate; numbers run 1.29× longer than decimal). Constants first. Strict coherence. Human-scale units (Nira 1.53 m, Woba 1.51 kg). Every number is generated by one script. Every coined word is provisional and unscreened.

## 2. How B solved its word and naming problems

**Evidence base.** Miller & Nicely 1955, the same paper this repo's `confusion.py` uses. B took one rule from it: *no two load-bearing consonants may differ only in place.* B turned the paper's qualitative findings into hand weights (voicing 3, nasality 3, affrication 2, duration 2, place 1). Its vowel distances are log-formant estimates. B says itself that these "rank candidates; they measure nothing". /l/ was never tested by Miller & Nicely, so digit 0 got /l/ because zero is the rarest digit position.

**Spoken numerals: three vowels with fixed jobs.** **u** = a digit, **a** = times a power of six, **i** = divided by one. The consonant is always a digit: l d s m t z = 0–5.
- Digits: *lu du su mu tu zu*. Place words: *dap sap map* (6, 36, 216), *dip sip mip* (1/6, 1/36, 1/216). Point: *fu*. Minus: *vi* (always leads).
- **Two registers, both standard.** In the quantity reading, 244₆ is *su sap tu dap tu*, zeros are skipped and "one" is dropped before a place word (*dap*, not *du dap*). In the string reading, digits are said one by one, and it is mandatory for clocks, dates, IDs and everything after the point. Reasoning: the quantity reading averages 5.3 syllables over 1–1295 against 3.8 for strings, so speakers will mix the two anyway; "codifying only one would guarantee drift toward the other".
- Evidence cited: real base-six languages coin power words (Kómnzo, Ndom); short digit words improve digit span (Ellis & Hennelly 1980); Norway's 1951 numeral reform, whose invented "toti" was rejected: "never codify what nobody would say".

**Prefixes.** Steps of 6⁴, spelled from the step number's digits with **a** for up and **i** for down: *da sa ma* … *dala* (step 6 = 10₆). An unseen prefix can be decoded. (Steps 1–5 are one syllable, so one misheard vowel flips the direction; the first review made B correct its claim otherwise.) A ladder word standing alone is a number word: *mu da* = 3 × 6⁴, and daNi literally reads "6⁴ Nira".

**Unit names.** Arbitrary but constrained: uniform CVCa shape; onsets k b n f v w (the largest set with no place-only pair); no minimal pairs; and the tightest sounds go to the least-said units (/v/ to charge, a judgment call). A machine check caught four drafts that eyeballing would have shipped (Koba/Woba, Bema/Vema, Nema/Vema, Kuna/Kena).

**Junctures, B's most transferable finding.** The place words were first agreed as *dan san man*. A plain read-through then found that "dan Nira" (6 Nira) sounds like "da Nira" (1296 Nira): the coda merges with the unit's initial n. Pairwise confusability scoring can't see this.

**What the three scripts test, and what they found.** I reran all three.

| script | what it tests | what it found |
|---|---|---|
| `numwords.py` | o/u digit vowels with e for place words | rejected: *do*~*da* 1.24 and *de*~*di* 1.46, below the ladder's weakest pair (2.0). Quantity reading 5.34 syllables, string 3.80; quantity reading unambiguous over 1–1295. |
| `numwords2.py` | a/i/u design; a redundant -n on digits 3–5 | the coda buys nothing: weakest pair *lu*/*du* (2.0) either way, which turns on the untested /l/. |
| `juncture.py` | every grammatical word pair run together, doubled consonants collapsed | -n coda: 24 collisions (each place word before Nira, Noza, Nawa, Neta), each a ×216 error. -p coda: none. Its remaining "1 collision" is the old minus *vu* (*vu ta* = Vuta); with *vi* it is 0. `verify.py` asserts both outcomes. |

**Honest limits, from B's own text.** No coined word has been spoken by anyone, and none has been screened against the world's languages. B admits that "universality and noise robustness pull in opposite directions", and robustness won: the digit consonants rely on t/d and s/z voicing, and on /z/, which Spanish lacks.

Run it whenever words are added (A2, A4, B1) | **High** | Of all checks, only this one looks across word boundaries. Back-translation tests are written text, so they can never hear a juncture error. See the results below. |
| 2 | **Scale words double as unit prefixes** (B: "a ladder word standing alone is a number word") | B2 units; A2 | **Medium** | Here the noun comes before the count, so 5 km is already **MEta SA MEwu** "metre 5-thousand" **[inferred: no metre word exists yet]**. **What the quick juncture check found here** (exhaustive over 1- and 2-word sequences of all 375 forms; stress-aware; doubled consonants collapsed as in B; **not filtered for grammatical adjacency**, so it is an upper bound):

- **15 cases where one word sounds like two.** A stressed short digit followed by an unstressed grammar word has exactly a content word's stress pattern, so stress doesn't separate them. The worst is **PU mu** "1, NOT" = **PUmu** "1": a lost negation, which `build.py` weights at 10×. Five of the ten long digits are short digit + a grammar word (**PU mu**, **TI we**, **KO se**, **NO bi**, **YA lu**). Others land on content words: **SA ba** = **SAba** "sit", **DA bu** = **DAbu** "mouth", **YA ga** = **YAga** "put". This is a side effect of "short = first syllable of long" **[inferred]**.
- **10 grammar-word pairs differ only by a final -n** (so/son, de/den, ga/gan, fa/fan, pi/pin, do/don, ta/tan, fu/fun, gu/gun, to/ton). Each merges before any of the 23 n-initial words: **de NImo** = **den NImo**. Most pairs sit in different slots, so context saves them. de/den, do/don and ta/tan look like real ambiguities **[inferred, not checked against a parser]**. It is the drift table's "final n melting away", happening across a word boundary.
- Hearing a doubled n as one is B's conservative assumption; whether a long [nː] survives is a listening-test question (C2).

**Ideas that would conflict with this language's evidence:** B's digit consonants rest on t/d and s/z voicing, but this repo deleted /z/ and its drift model predicts s/z merging and middle consonants voicing. B puts the value before the unit, against heads first (principle 2). Zero-based dates, one world time and coordinates in turns all redesign what modern Earth already shares. B's "u = digit" vowel scheme would move number words, which are now fixed.

## 4. Conflicts and mismatches

- **Base 6 vs base 10.** B's case is real: 10 multiplication facts instead of 36, and more fractions terminate. But B gets there by declaring legacy arguments void. This language's base 10 rests on exactly the legacy B voids: finger counting, and a decimal world of prices, phone numbers and SI that a native speaker on modern Earth must talk about. These are opposite premises, not a disagreement to resolve.
- **Worldbuilding vs modern Earth.** B's names are explicitly lore placeholders; this language's words are meant for use.
- **Phonology.** B uses CV syllables (plus a -p coda on place words), /v/ and /z/, and no stress rule **[inferred from absence]**; this language uses (C)V(n), no v or z, and initial pitch stress. 6 of B's 20 unit names contain v or z. **buda** and **koda** already mean "dark" and "sell" here. B's *mu* = 3 is NOT here, *lu* = 0 is OR, *fu* (point) is LIKE, and *da*, *sa*, *ti* are short digits here with other values.
- **Process and tooling.** B generates its documents from one source of truth and runs 93 assertions (92 ran here; one needs an optional package), 19 mutants and two adversarial reviews. This repo has an optimizer, a confusion model and back-translation tests. B's outputs are numbers that must be exact; this language is judged by transmission, so the tooling should differ.

## 5. Options

**(a) Merge the projects.**
- Pros: shared Miller & Nicely basis, a shared concern with drift and robust number words, one home for numeral design.
- Cons: opposite premises, a different base, incompatible phonologies; B is finished; a merge imports about 320 KB of process text.

**(b) Adopt B's system as the measurement vocabulary.**
- Pros: complete, coherent, already named and checked; base six really is easier to multiply in.
- Cons: nobody on Earth measures in Nira. It needs senary numerals beside decimal ones, which contradicts the base-10 decision. Its names break this phonology and collide with existing words. It would turn this language into a worldbuilding artefact, which is a change of goal.

**(c) Borrow specific ideas only.**
- Pros: cheap. Idea 1 is a ~30-line script that already found concrete defects. Idea 2 *deletes* a vocabulary layer.
- Cons: every borrowing is a new part. Unfiltered, the juncture check is noisy (379 raw hits), so it is only useful with a slot-adjacency filter, which `build.py`'s grammar groups half-provide.

**(d) Take nothing.**
- Pros: the tooling here already covers most of what B did, often better. Nothing new to maintain. The core lesson fits in one line of the drift notes.
- Cons: the **PU mu** = **PUmu** class and the -n pairs stay undetected. Text-based back-translation structurally can't find them; only a listening test (C2) would.

**Recommendation: (c), narrowly.**

| part | source | add-back / drop trigger |
|---|---|---|
| Juncture check in `build.py`, filtered by grammatical adjacency, run when words are added; first decision: what to do about **PU mu** and de/den | B's documented defect plus my run here. *Claude-added, so scrutinise it* | Drop it if a listening test shows that stress plus a long [nː] keeps these apart |
| Scale words double as unit prefixes, as an input to B2 | B §2/§8 | Add SI prefix words back if back-translating unit-heavy text loses meaning |
| Error-size weighting for scale and unit words, as an input to B2 | B Rationale ("detectability rises with error size") | none needed: it's a weighting choice, not a part |

Everything else in B: take nothing. Add-back trigger: B2 work hits a numeral or unit problem that B already solved.
