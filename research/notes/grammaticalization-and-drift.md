# Grammaticalization and drift: how native use will reshape the grammar

Compiled 2026-10-08. Status tags: **[S]** read in the source text or abstract, **[2nd]** secondary source or search digest only, **[I]** my inference, **UNVERIFIED** couldn't check. The World Lexicon of Grammaticalization (WLG) itself was not read; WLG claims come via a review.

## 1. Grammaticalization pathways

- WLG (Heine & Kuteva 2002) lists about 400 source→target paths from about 500 languages. Sources with the most targets: LOCATIVE (11), COMITATIVE (10), GET and ONE (9 each), DEMONSTRATIVE and SAY (8 each). Targets with the most sources: CONTINUOUS (13), FUTURE (12), CAUSE and PASSIVE (9 each). The sample paths include SAY > EVIDENTIAL, COME TO > FUTURE, SIT > CONTINUOUS, BELLY > IN, PERFECT > PAST and REFLEXIVE > PASSIVE. The reviewer's caveats: unidirectionality is asserted, not tested, and coverage is "a fraction" of real cases [2nd, LINGUIST List review].
- The eight WLG targets of SAY are cause, complementizer, conditional, evidential, purpose, quotative, simile and subordinator [2nd]. A reported evidential often comes from a speech verb, e.g. Qiang's hearsay marker from "say" [2nd, Aikhenvald].
- In Bybee, Perkins & Pagliuca 1994, verbs of motion toward a goal often become futures, and "finish" becomes a completive or perfect (Bantu) [2nd].
- Demonstratives are a common source of definite articles, third-person pronouns, relative markers, non-verbal copulas, complementizers, focus markers and connectives. Over 50% of free relative markers derive from demonstratives [2nd, Diessel 1999 and later work].
- Real examples of the "finish" and "go/come" paths: Mandarin perfective *le* < *liǎo* "finish", common by the Tang dynasty and phonologically reduced [2nd]. Late Latin *cantare habeo* "I have to sing" > "I will sing" > French *chanterai*, with the first fused form (*daras*) in 7th-century Fredegar [2nd].
- Standard textbook paths I didn't check in WLG: BODY/HEAD > reflexive, BACK/FACE > "behind/in front", GIVE > benefactive, ONE > indefinite article (English *a(n)* < Old English *ān*) **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Categories that appear without anyone designing them** [I, from the above]: definite articles (from "this/that"), quotatives and hearsay evidentials (from "say"), futures (from go/come/want), progressives (from sit/stay/"be at"), perfect > past, and epistemic parentheticals. English *I think* lost its *that* and became a movable stance marker (Thompson & Mulac 1991) [2nd]. Definite articles also come and go quickly (Greenhill et al. 2017) [S].

## 2. The morphological cycle and how fast it runs

- **Fast case, creole.** Tok Pisin *baimbai* > *bai* > [ba] moved next to the verb, lost stress and became close to an obligatory future within roughly two generations. Child native speakers were in the lead (Sankoff & Laberge 1973) [2nd].
- Haitian Creole is about 300 years old. Its pronouns cliticize to *m, w, l, n, y*, and preverbal TAM markers fuse (*pral* < *pour aller*) [2nd, APiCS].
- **Slow case, inflection.** Fusional inflection is a "mature phenomenon" that needs "a non-trivial prehistory" of many generations (Dahl 2004) [2nd]. Romance took several centuries from periphrasis to suffix (exact span **UNVERIFIED**), and then renewed the future analytically (*je vais chanter*) [I from §1].
- **Isolating languages can stay isolating.** Mandarin *le* is still a separate syllable after about 1,000 years [2nd]. My guess is that rigid syllables slow fusion [I] **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Unstressed words next to their hosts.** Words that occur together often fuse into one unit, and reduction is greatest in the most frequent combinations: *don't* reduces most after *I* and before *know* (Bybee & Scheibman 1999) [S, abstract].
- **What this means here** [I]: the tightest chunks will be pronoun + aspect + verb (*e fa* …), NOT + verb, *mon* + digit, and noun + determiner. In this design those are proclitics before stressed heads and enclitics after nouns.

## 3. Erosion and renewal

- **Zipf's law of abbreviation.**
  - In a miniature language, users shorten frequent forms when pressured for both accuracy and effort (Kanwal et al. 2017) [2nd].
  - Speakers pick *math* over *mathematics* in predictive contexts (Mahowald et al. 2013) [S, abstract].
  - Prediction for this design: frequent compounds clip to one element [I].
- **Jespersen's cycle.** Jespersen (1917): "the original negative adverb is first weakened, then found insufficient and therefore strengthened, generally through some additional word…" [2nd, quoted]. Examples: French *ne* > *ne … pas* ("step") > *pas*, and Welsh *ny(t)* > *ddim*, with the cycle taking centuries [2nd].
- **Intensifier renewal.**
  - In York, *very* marks a speaker over 35 and *really* marks younger speakers (Ito & Tagliamonte 2003) [2nd].
  - In Toronto 2002–06, speakers over 60 used almost only *very*, and *so* was rising, led by women (Tagliamonte 2008) [2nd].
  - Change can be fast: quotative *be like* went from 13% to 58% of quotatives in seven years (1995–2002, Toronto) [2nd].
- **Euphemism treadmill.** Pinker 1994: "concepts, not words, are in charge: give a concept a new name, and the name becomes colored by the concept" [2nd].
- **Politeness inflation.**
  - European T/V use moved from the power semantic to the solidarity semantic (Brown & Gilman 1960) [2nd].
  - Honorific titles wore down into plain pronouns: *vuestra merced* > *usted*, and *vossa mercê* > *você* > *cê* [2nd].
  - Japanese *kisama* ("honoured sir") became an insult, and *omae* became plain "you" [2nd, non-scholarly sources].
- **Certainty and hyperbole inflation.**
  - *No doubt* weakened from certainty to probability (Simon-Vandenbergen & Aijmer 2007) [2nd].
  - Hyperbolic *literally* dates from at least 1769, and Merriam-Webster's 1909 dictionary already noted it as "often used hyperbolically" [2nd].
  - "100%" as an agreement or emphasis token: I found no corpus study. **UNVERIFIED** as a documented change, though it is easy to observe.

## 4. Regularization

- **Children regularize unpredictable variation.** Children made probabilistic determiners categorical, while adults reproduced the input probabilities (Hudson Kam & Newport 2005) [S, abstract]. Adults also regularize when the variation is complex (2009) [2nd].
- **Iterated learning.** Chains of adult learners, though not isolated learners, cumulatively removed unpredictable plural marking by lexicalizing it (Smith & Wonnacott 2010) [S, abstract].
- **Frequency protects irregularity.** Of 177 Old English irregular verbs, 79 have regularized. An irregular verb's half-life scales with the square root of its frequency (Lieberman et al. 2007) [2nd].
- **Do regular systems stay regular?** Analogy keeps rare items regular, but sound change makes the most frequent items irregular (point 2). So irregularity collects in the top few hundred chunks [I].
- **Caveat.** Kempe, Blasi & Raviv (2026, BBS) argue that young children are *not* agents of change: most of their innovations are treated as acquisition errors and don't spread [S, abstract]. Social diffusion among adolescents and adults drives change. For this design, social pressures (politeness, emphasis, identity) are the bigger force, more than child regularization.

## 5. Stability

- **Greenhill et al. 2017** (81 Austronesian languages) [S]:
  - Grammar changed faster than basic vocabulary: a median of 7.93e-05 vs 1.48e-05 changes per item per year, or 13.7 vs 2.5 changes over the trees.
  - Order features "tend to fall in the fast rate category".
  - Slow features are abstract ones, such as "is there an inclusive/exclusive distinction?", which the authors call "less available to speaker reflection".
  - Clusivity and gender are highly stable. Definite articles and numeral classifiers are unstable.
- **Dediu & Cysouw 2013** (7 methods, WALS) [S]: across methods, word-order features are the *most* stable, especially adjective–noun and genitive–noun order. This is stability of *which* order a language has, not of fixed vs free order; don't cite it for "fixed order is stable" *(added 2026-10-12 after the fact-check, C14)*.
- **The conflict between them** is probably sampling: one family versus world-wide aggregates [S, Greenhill].
- **Takeaway** [I]: basic content words are the slow core. Particles, overt categories and anything speakers can consciously notice change fastest. Head-modifier order is probably safe.

## 6. Specific risks

- **(a) Numeric confidence turning into emphasis.**
  - This follows the *literally* / *no doubt* / *surely* pattern [2nd].
  - A digit is maximally overt and open to reflection, so it is the kind of feature that gets recruited socially [I, Greenhill].
  - Calibration holds up only with outcome feedback, not habit (see [numeric-confidence.md](numeric-confidence.md)).
- **(b) Politeness taking over hedges.**
  - Listeners read "possible" about a severe outcome as tact (Bonnefon & Villejoubert 2006) [S, via numeric-confidence.md].
  - Conventional indirectness turns questions into requests [2nd, stance_pragmatics.md].
  - Expected result: low digits used as softeners, and *to* (advise) used as a polite *gon* (instruct) [I].
- **(c) Reference-tracking categories.**
  - Algonquian obviation reconstructs to Proto-Algonquian (obviative *-ri*). It survives family-wide but has been worn down, losing obviative number in Cree, Menominee and others (Oxford) [S]. There it is morphology governed by syntax, not a free pronoun.
  - Logophoricity is areal (the Macro-Sudan belt). It was innovated, inherited and also borrowed (Güldemann 2003/2008) [2nd]. I found no data on how fast it is lost. **UNVERIFIED**.
  - Switch-reference markers grow out of conjunctions, demonstratives, case markers and nonfinite forms [2nd]. Loss rates **UNVERIFIED**.
  - Evidentials spread readily through contact and are lost when speakers shift to a language without them (Aikhenvald) [2nd].
  - Inclusive/exclusive is among the most stable features (Greenhill) [S].
- **(d) Echo answers.**
  - 62 of Holmberg's 130 languages use verb-echo answers [2nd].
  - Latin used echoes, and Romance built yes-words from *sic* "thus" and *hoc (ille)* "this" [2nd].
  - The Irish echo survived the shift to English: "Will you come?" → "I will" [2nd].
  - Colloquial Finnish borrowed *joo* from Swedish *jo* and uses it alongside echoes [2nd].
  - Echo systems are stable but leak: yes-words come from "thus/this" or from contact.

## Implications for the design

Each prediction is ranked by likelihood × impact, with an "add-back trigger" where I propose doing nothing. Overall: the content lexicon is the slow core, and almost all the faithful-transmission machinery (speech act, NOT, confidence, source, reference) sits in the fastest-changing layer. My unranked meta-prediction is that a single language for all of Earth will split into regional dialects as Latin did. That can't be designed away [I].

1. **The confidence top end inflates into emphasis and agreement** (high × high). Expect *mon newen* to become "really" or "absolutely", the scale to compress toward 5/8/10, and doubling to become the new marker of actual certainty.
   - **Pre-empt:** give emphasis cheap, socially rewarded outlets of its own. *dun*, *we* and doubling already exist; teach them as *the* way to emphasize. Keep *mon* optional so that saying it carries information.
   - Accept that renewal will happen, and make sure the renewed forms stay inside the system: the two-digit form *mon ENge ENge* already gives a precise top end once *newen* bleaches.
   - No grammar can keep the numbers calibrated; only scored feedback in practice can.
2. **Politeness takes over the low digits, questions and *to*** (high × high). Confidence becomes unreadable in exactly the face-threatening contexts where it matters.
   - **Pre-empt (part I'm adding):** a dedicated politeness particle with no truth-conditional meaning, so the treadmill runs on it and not on *mon*.
   - Expect that particle to inflate too (the *usted* / *kisama* pattern) and plan for it to renew. This conflicts with numeric-confidence.md's "let hedges grow on their own"; the owner should decide.
3. **Unstressed vowels collapse grammar-word contrasts** (medium × high). Unstressed CV and CVn words placed before stressed heads will cliticize (Tok Pisin moved fast). If unstressed vowels centralize, which happens in many languages but not all [I], these same-slot pairs merge:
   - *bin* "only" / *ban* "rather"
   - *wan* "unspecified" / *wun* "at least"
   - *ti* "if" / *to* "advise", both sentence-initial
   - *mon* / *men*

   Losing a final -n creates no collisions; I checked the full list. **Pre-empt:** make grammar words that share a slot differ by consonant, not only by vowel.
   - Fusion itself is harmless if each particle keeps a fixed slot and a distinct consonant: it yields agglutinative prefixes, still one form per meaning.
   - Fusion is harmful when two particles merge into one form (a portmanteau). Pronoun + aspect, like Haitian *m ap*, is the likeliest case.
4. **Obviation drifts** (medium × high). *tan* is chosen by "importance", which learners can't predict, so they will regularize it to something they can: tan = non-subject or later-mentioned, or a pejorative "that other one". **Pre-empt:** state the default as the rule children would arrive at anyway (e.g. the first-introduced subject stays *lu* until it is explicitly switched). Algonquian shows obviation can last millennia when the syntax governs it.
5. **Grammaticalization fills slots that weren't designed** (high × medium).
   - "say" becomes a quotative or hearsay marker competing with *sun*.
   - "go/come" becomes a future.
   - *fa* drifts from perfect to past; narrative *fa* is already halfway there.
   - *va* or *nun* bleaches into a definite article.

   **Proposed deletion:** don't pre-empt articles or a future. They're cheap and harmless. **Add-back trigger:** an emergent article starts dropping the deictic contrast this/that.
6. **Jespersen's cycle on *na*** (medium × medium, over centuries). **Pre-empt:** sanction *na na* (doubling) as the reinforcer, so the strengthening stays inside the system.
7. **Echo answers acquire a yes-word** from *mm*, *la* or "like this" (medium × low-medium). The cost is ambiguous answers to negative questions. **Proposed deletion:** no pre-emption. **Add-back trigger:** a yes-word appears with polarity confusion.
8. **The euphemism treadmill and compound clipping** are certain but low-impact. The compound system renews words cheaply. Words keep their form while their meanings move; that is fine.
9. **Lower-risk features:** inclusive/exclusive (*nin*/*so*) and head-modifier order are among the most stable features. *gan* has no loss-rate data. My guess is that it merges with *ku* or with plain *lu* [I] **UNVERIFIED**.
