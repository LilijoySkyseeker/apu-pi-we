# Numeral bases in natural languages: typology, origins, drift

Research notes, 2026-10-08. Labels: **[S]** sourced (read in this session), **[I]** my inference, **UNVERIFIED** = seen only in secondary/tertiary sources or recalled. Bracketed numbers point to the source list at the end.

## 1. How common is each base?

**WALS 131A (Comrie), 196 languages** [1] (counts re-checked against the WALS data table, v2020.6): decimal 125 (63.8%), hybrid vigesimal-decimal 22 (11.2%), pure vigesimal 20 (10.2%), restricted (no real numerals above about 20) 20 (10.2%), other base 5 (2.6%), extended body-part 4 (2.0%). The five "other base" languages are Ekari (base 60), Ngiti (32), Supyire and Tommo So (both West African, with 20/80/400-type bases), and Embera Chami. Body-part systems: Kobon, Haruai, Eipo, Una (all New Guinea). **No base-6, base-8 or base-12 language appears in the 196-language sample.** [S]

**Larger sample (Numeralbank, as reported in Koile & Blasi 2025)** [2]: of 2,577 languages with full base annotation, 1,712 (66%) base-10, 465 (18%) base-5, 326 (13%) base-20, 74 (3%) other. Vigesimal clusters in Mesoamerica and the Caucasus. [S]

**Hammarström (2010), surveying over 4,000 sources (about 2,500 languages)** [3, abstract only; the full chapter was paywalled]. He defines "rare" as "present in few geographical areas" rather than as rare in language counts. Bases 5, 10 and 20 are "omnipresent". Base 4 is attested on four continents. There are "three true base-6 systems" on Frederik Hendrik Island (Drabbe 1949: Kimaghama, Ndom, Riantana) and "many attestations of bona-fide base-12 systems in the Plateau Area of Nigeria", with Dhivehi (Maldives) a possible further case. Bases 15, 24 and 36 are virtually unique. [S]

**Rare bases in detail:**
- **Senary (base 6):** Kanum and the Morehead-Maro (Yam) family of southern New Guinea, with single-word powers up to 6⁶ (Evans 2009) [4]. Nen's speaker remembered a term at 6⁷, and Keraakie has one too. Agöb, a neighbouring language, "recently borrowed the senary power numerals". For Ndom, the numerals *mer* 6 and *nif* 36 come from Wikipedia only and are UNVERIFIED (the primary source is Drabbe 1949). [S except Ndom forms]
- **Octal (base 8):** Northern Pame (Mexico), e.g. *karnuʔ tenhiuɲ rnuʔ* "three eight three" = 27 [5, citing Avelino 2006]. Comrie lists "8: spaces between fingers (attested for some California languages)" [5]. That Yuki counted the spaces between fingers with twigs, and that Pame counts knuckles, comes from Wikipedia only: UNVERIFIED.
- **Duodecimal (base 12):** Plateau Nigeria, e.g. Berom *ba-kuru ba-ba* "twelves two" + 2 = 26 [5]; Janji, Gure, Kahugu and Piti [6]. **Chepang (Nepal) is weaker than Wikipedia implies.** Caughley [7] reports that no living speaker knows Chepang numerals above five, that speakers use Nepali decimal numerals above three, and that the base-12 evidence is a tally word *hale* "dozen" used in game-counting plus a reconstructed "spirit" number list. He calls it "tantalisingly incomplete". Treat Chepang as a trace, not a living system. [S]
- **Sexagesimal (base 60):** Sumerian and Ekari (Comrie) [1]. Babylonian written numerals used sub-base 10 inside each base-60 place [8]. **Vigesimal (base 20):** prototypically Classical Maya. In the Maya calendar *bak* meant 360 instead of 400 [5]. [S]

**Where bases come from.** Comrie's hypothesis [5] is that bases have "either a somatic or a commercial (transactional) origin". On the somatic side: 10 = fingers; 20 = fingers and toes; 8 = spaces between fingers; 12 = finger phalanges without the thumb. On the commercial side: English *score*, and Balinese numerals rebuilt around packages of Chinese coins (25 = "one thread", 200 = "one bundle"). The Old Norse and medieval English "long hundred" = 120 shows decimal and duodecimal mixing [9]. [S]

## 2. How senary and duodecimal systems actually work

**Komnzo (Döhler 2018)** [10] is the best primary description. [S]
- **Two systems side by side.** One is a restricted everyday set that goes to about 4–5: *näbi* 1, *eda* 2, *etha* 3 (which also means "a few"), *asar* 4. The other is an unrestricted senary set **used only in ritual yam counting**: *nibo* 6, *fta* 36, *taruba* 216, *damno* 1,296, *wärämäkä* 7,776, *wi* 46,656.
- **English is a third system.** English numerals are borrowed "when trading goods, charging one's mobile phone credit, or counting the eleven members of a soccer team".
- **The ritual forms are older.** Ritual *yda*/*ytho* keep an initial *y* that the everyday words lost in a regular sound change. The ritual register protected the old forms.
- **Length.** 72 = *eda fta* "2 × 36"; 50 = *näbi fta a eda nibo a eda* (seven words). Döhler: senary "differs from a decimal system only in the location of simple and complex points in the number space, but not in its overall complexity". 46,656 is one word, *wi*.
- **Origin in yam counting.** Two men each pick up three yams, so each move deposits six. The teller calls "one one one …" for each six. Each 36 is tallied with a counter yam set aside, and the counters are then counted the same way. One *damno* is roughly a family's yearly store. Nobody in Rouku remembered anyone reaching *wi*.

**Evans 2009** [4] adds: the system is "intimately associated with the counting of yams" in every language of the family. Yei, the one family member without senary powers, grows sago, not yams. The yams are laid out radially at 60° angles (a six-petal arrangement). Nen counts the five fingers and then the wrist as 6. Hammarström's counterpoint, quoted by Evans, is that "there is nothing we know about yams that predicts 6". Evans's own caution: if six-part hands were naturally salient to humans, "we would then face the problem of explaining why senary systems are so rare". [S]

**Duodecimal in speech.** Hammarström lists "unanalyzable forms for numerals up to 12" in Jere and Janji as a rarity [3]: base-12 languages carry 12 atomic words. Origins: phalanx counting (Comrie [5]). Chepang counts the thumb over finger interstices, 3 × 4 = 12 per hand [7]. [S]

## 3. Diachrony: what changes, how fast, and why

**Low numerals are among the most stable words.** Pagel & Meade 2018 [11]: "one" to "five" have "exceptionally slow rates of lexical replacement" in Indo-European, Bantu and Austronesian, stable for "10 000 to over 100 000 years". They add that "the 'teens' … and the names of the numbers that are powers of 10 can be more variable". A 2025 correction changed table values but not the conclusions [12]. [S]

**Borrowing goes from the top down.** Matras's scale (cited in [13]) runs: 1000, 100 > above 20 > above 10 > above 5 > below 5. Greenberg's near-universal: if an atomic numeral is borrowed, all higher atomic numerals are too. Gardani & Zanini [13] sampled 25 languages that had borrowed at least one numeral from 1–10. Borrowing rates were 18–27% for 1–3, 37.5% for 4, and 48–70% for 5–10. Their explanation is subitizing; the sociolinguistic explanation is trade, schooling and formal registers (Matras). [S]

**Bases change easily, under contact.**
- **Mesoamerica.** Comrie: "Pre-Conquest Mesoamerica was largely vigesimal". Spanish *ciento* brought decimal hundreds, so **the "hybrid vigesimal-decimal" WALS type is partly a contact product**. In many languages, "Spanish numeral expressions are used for all but the lowest numbers" [1]. [S]
- **Nigeria (Matsushita 1998)** [6]. This is a conference paper reproduced on the Dozenal Society's site and not peer-reviewed. Plateau base-12 languages were "in the process of transition to decimal" already in Meek's 1931 records. The switch was "rather painless": the 12-word was reinterpreted in place as 10, and the words for 10 and 11 dropped out. The Nimbia dialect of Gwandara went the other way, decimal to duodecimal, through contact with Plateau languages, by adding a superstructure on top of its decimal words. It is now reverting toward Hausa-style decimal. Matsushita concludes that changing systems "does not seem to be a 'big deal'"; numerals behave "like unit of measure, colonial law or some fancy goods". [S, single source]
- **Decimal to vigesimal also happens.** Hindu Kush Indo-Aryan languages "shifted to a vigesimal system" as an areal feature (Cathcart 2025) [14]. [S]
- **Wholesale replacement.** Comrie: numeral systems "are even more endangered than the languages", and some inherited systems were replaced entirely [9]. Japanese and Thai being largely replaced by Chinese numerals appeared only in a search snippet attributing it to Comrie 2005: UNVERIFIED. Chepang, Komnzo and Mesoamerica above are verified cases. [S]

**Erosion and irregularization.**
- **Hindi/Urdu.** Sanskrit's "relatively (albeit not entirely) transparent" decimal system became one of "extreme irregularity" in Hindi/Urdu, where arguably all of 1–100 must be memorized: 50 *pacās* versus 51 *ikyāvan*; 29 *untīs* is subtractive [5, 14]. The causes are regular sound change plus analogical contamination (Berger 1992, cited in [14]). [S]
- **Irregularity follows frequency.** Complexity "decays as cardinality increases (and frequency decreases)", and frequency predicts it better than cardinality [14]. That matches English, where *eleven* and *twelve* ("one left", "two left") and *fif-teen* are irregular but *ninety-nine* is regular. [S; the eleven/twelve etymology is recalled and consistent with secondary sources]
- **What the drift looks like.** Fused tens (Russian *sorok* 40), overcounting (Danish *halvtreds* = "half-third × 20", now clipped), overrunning (French *soixante-dix-sept*), and meaning shifts in high powers (*billion*; Chinese 億 has meant 10⁵ or 10⁸ at different times) [5]. [S]
- **French.** That France chose *soixante-dix* over *septante* while Belgium and Switzerland kept *septante*, plus Académie involvement: popular/forum sources only, UNVERIFIED. Hammarström (on the LINGUIST List) notes that the Celtic-substrate explanation lacks Gaulish evidence.

**Designing numerals to be learnable.** Across 30 languages, attested systems sit near the optimal trade-off between complexity and informativeness [15]. Canonical decimal (Mandarin-type) is among the simplest recursive systems; English-style teens and base-20 or 10/20 hybrids are more complex [15]. Hammarström's rare-means-few-areas framing implies that rare bases are rare because of history, not because they cannot be learned [I].

## 4. Mixed-radix everyday systems

- **Time.** French decimal time became mandatory for public records on 22 Sep 1794, and its mandatory use was suspended on 7 Apr 1795. The 1897 commission (Poincaré secretary) proposed decimal minutes and was abandoned in 1900 [16]. The objections reported were cost, habit, confusion and watchmakers' losses, all switching-cost arguments. [S, Wikipedia citing primary sources]
- **Currency.** £sd had 12 pence to the shilling and 20 shillings to the pound, 240 pence in all. It was in use "for centuries" and was decimalised on 15 Feb 1971 [17]. **A highly divisible system was abandoned once a state coordinated the switch.** Matsushita notes the irony that Nigerian base-12 languages decimalised under a colonial currency built on 12 [6]. [S]
- **Sexagesimal origin.** The theories (Theon's divisibility argument, Neugebauer's thirds in weights and measures, astronomy, finger counting 5 × 12, a merger of peoples) are all contested. MacTutor treats the designed-for-divisibility accounts sceptically [8]. [S]
- **No study ties persistence to divisibility.** I searched and found only advocacy and popular pages. The documented evidence fits **coordination and switching costs** (clocks, global time standards) at least as well as divisibility. Divisible systems died when switching was coordinated (£sd, Plateau base 12); a less divisible system (decimal time) failed when switching was too costly. [I]
- 360° and dozens/grosses: persistence is observed, but I found no causal study. Recalled, not checked.

## 5. Spoken length (brief)

- Bilingual Welsh speakers have shorter digit span in Welsh, whose digits are syllabically longer, than in English (Ellis & Hennelly 1980, as reported in [18]). Murray & Jones (2002) attribute the Welsh deficit to **between-word coarticulation**, not word duration [18]. [S, secondary]
- Mattys, Baddeley & Trenkic 2017 [18] confirm Mandarin speakers' higher span. **Rehearsal speed "did not provide an adequate account"**, and the advantage extends to non-digit words. Word length matters, but it is not the whole story. [S]
- Arithmetic [I]: for the same quantity, base 6 needs about 1.285× as many digits as base 10, and base 12 about 0.927×. 999,999 takes 8 senary digits versus 6 decimal or duodecimal. Komnzo's 7-word "50" is the spoken-form version of this cost [10].

## Implications for choosing a base and for its drift

1. **Natural selection of bases has not favoured 6 or 12.** About 64–66% of languages are decimal [1, 2]. Base 6 and base 12 survive only as small regional systems tied to a specific counting practice: yams, phalanges, game tallies. None of the 196 WALS languages uses them. Rarity reflects body-based and trade-based origins plus diffusion, not proof of unlearnability: Komnzo children do learn senary. But **no large society has used a base-6 or base-12 spoken system for general purposes**, so a designed one would be untested at scale. [I]
2. **The base word and the higher powers are the fragile parts, not the digits.** Words for 1–4 will barely move. The word for b, b², and the round multiples are what get borrowed, reinterpreted (Plateau "12 → 10") or fused. Design effort belongs there. [I from §3]
3. **The main documented driver of base change is contact** with a prestige language that uses another base: Spanish in Mesoamerica, Hausa and English in Nigeria, Nepali for Chepang, English for Komnzo. In the thought experiment everyone is native and no rival base exists, so the biggest pressure is removed. The residual risk is the *writing and computing* layer. If machines, legacy science or units stay decimal, they act as a permanent contact language, which is exactly how base-12 Plateau and Mesoamerican vigesimal were eroded. [I]
4. **Expect irregularization where frequency is highest.** That means the zone just above the base (b+1 … 2b) and the round multiples (Hindi, English teens, Danish tens). The base choice matters here:
   - **Base 6:** compounds start at 7, so very frequent numbers (7–11, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36) are compounds. Usage predicts they will fuse fastest. The plausible failure mode is that they lexicalize into atoms and the system drifts toward a de-facto larger base. [I; prediction, untested]
   - **Base 12:** twelve atoms keep 1–12 monomorphemic, so compounds begin at 13. The frequency-weighted surface exposed to fusion is smaller. The cost is 12 short, distinct atoms, each needing phonetic space; Pagel & Meade's "full phonetic space" hypothesis suggests crowded numeral sets resist change [11].
   - **Base 10:** the most-tested baseline, and it matches the existing notation of the world.
5. **Guard against fusion by phonological design.** Hindi shows that a transparent system can become fully opaque within about two to three millennia through ordinary sound change [14]. Keep morpheme boundaries robust: no vowel-vowel junctures at the digit/base joint, distinct onsets, and monosyllabic atoms. These also help digit span, given the Welsh and coarticulation findings [18]. Accept that some irregularity in the most frequent forms will arise and is, on the efficiency evidence, arguably fine [14, 15]. [I]
6. **Divisibility is not a proven survival advantage.** Divisible systems (£sd, Plateau base 12) died quickly when switching was coordinated. The case for 12 has to rest on arithmetic merit (covered in `base-mathematics.md`), not on claims that it persists naturally. [I]
7. **Plan for registers.** Komnzo keeps senary in a ritual register and uses restricted numerals in everyday speech, and the ritual register preserved older forms [10]. A designed system may split the same way, with precise counting and quick talk diverging. Specify a fast colloquial form so the population does not invent one. [I]
