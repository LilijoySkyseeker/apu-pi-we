# Word formation: which derivations to provide, and how to keep them decodable

Compiled 2026-10-08 for roadmap item A2. Status tags: **[S]** read in the source text or abstract, **[2nd]** secondary source, search digest or unsourced wiki, **[I]** my inference, **[R]** recalled from memory, **UNVERIFIED** couldn't check. Štekauer, Valera & Körtvélyessy 2012 (55 languages) was **not readable** (paywalled; Cambridge pages and reviews 403'd), so its per-category frequencies are missing. The best category counts I could read come from a 40-language **European** sample (Körtvélyessy, Bagasheva & Štekauer, eds., 2020), which is areally biased.

## 1. Which derivational categories are widespread

- **Processes.** In Štekauer et al. 2012, 50 of 55 languages have compounds (91%) [2nd]. For diminutives the processes were suffixation, then reduplication, then compounding; prefixation was very rare (Štekauer et al. 2012: 269, 325, as cited by Schneider) [S]. Schneider's hierarchy for diminutives puts synthetic before analytic and affixation before compounding. He also notes that English prefers the analytic type (*little house*) [S].
- **Category counts, European sample** (*Derivational Networks Across Languages*, De Gruyter 2020, ch. 49, 40 languages) [S]. A count is the number of languages where most of the 10 sample bases take the category as a first derivation.
  - Verb bases: ACTION 24, AGENT 21, RESULTATIVE 15, ABILITY 11, DIMINUTIVE 7.
  - Noun bases: DIMINUTIVE 25, QUALITY 21, PRIVATIVE 14 (*toothless*), RELATIONAL 11.
  - Adjective bases: STATIVE 23, MANNER 21, DIMINUTIVE 17, AUGMENTATIVE 15.
- **Evaluatives** (Körtvélyessy 2015, 71 European languages, quoted in the same chapter) [S]: 60 have evaluative morphology, 39 have both diminutives and augmentatives, 21 have only diminutives, and none has augmentatives without diminutives. So augmentative implies diminutive.
- **World samples.**
  - Bauer 2002 (more than 40 languages) found deverbal abstract nouns and agent nouns the most frequent nominal derivations [2nd].
  - **Location** (Bauer, 50 languages chosen *because* they have locational affixes) [S]:
    - 40/50 derive "place where you do X" from verbs; 21/50 derive "place where X is found" from nouns; 3/50 derive locations from adjectives.
    - Meanings that location affixes share: instrument 14, abstract noun 10, patient 8, result 8, time 7.
  - **Agent and instrument** share a marker in a large cross-linguistic tendency (Luschützky & Rainer 2011, 100+ languages). Meaning spreads along AGENT > INSTRUMENT > LOCATION [2nd]. Bauer says the agent–instrument merger is rarer than Western European experience suggests [S].
  - **Causative**: 278 of 310 languages (90%) have a morphological causative (WALS ch. 111, Song) [S].
- **Patient nouns** are a common gap. English has *-ee* only for human patients, so speakers press other affixes into service (*washables*, *barterables*). Lieber concludes "any word is better than no word" [S, via Bauer].
- **Gaps:** I found no frequency ranking for reversative, inchoative, collective or a stand-alone instrument category. WALS has no chapters on derivational categories [R].

## 2. How analytic languages build vocabulary

- **Mandarin.**
  - 75–80% of words are compounds of two or more characters (Packard 2000, via Liu et al. 2022) [S]. Almost 95% of the 709 two-syllable neologisms in a 2002 dictionary are compounds (Ceccagno & Basciano 2007) [2nd].
  - **There is no formal line between a compound head and a suffix.** Arcodia's criterion is meaning: a bound item in a fixed position whose meaning is more general than its free use counts as a suffix, even with no sound change [S, Basciano & Bareato 2020].
  - Examples [S, same source]:
    - **Suffixes by that criterion:** 学 *xué* "study" > "field of study" (语言学 linguistics), and 性 *xìng* "nature" > "-ity/-ness" (毒性 toxicity).
    - **Still just compound heads:** 人 *rén* (上海人 Shanghaiese) and 店 *diàn* (书店 bookstore). They are productive but keep their full meaning, and they also occur on the left (店员 shop assistant).
  - A computational model found "no clear criteria" for affix status in Mandarin (Tseng et al. 2020) [S].
- **Person suffixes keep turning over in Mandarin.** In the *People's Daily*, 310 new X-族 *zú* words appeared in 1995–2006 [S]. X-党 *dǎng* then overtook it online, and 客 *kè* got a boost from English *hacker*.
  - Both 族 and 党 shifted from "group" to "an individual of the group". They overlap ("people with common characteristics or behaviour"), and 党 keeps a negative tinge [S].
  - The authors ask "why so many different affixes are needed to create words referring to persons". Their answer is that many coinages serve fashion and social signalling, not naming needs [S].
- **Thai** builds words head-first, the same order as this language. It has nominalizers *khwaam-* (quality: *khwaam-sǔay* "beauty"), *kaan-* (action), *nák-* (agent or profession) and *phûu-* (person who) [S for *khwaam*, *nák*; 2nd for the others].
  - Instrument compounds such as *khrʉ̂aŋ-wát* "instrument-measure" = gauge have a noun head plus a verb modifier [2nd].
  - *nák-* is a Khmer loan. Shan uses *mɔ̌* "expert" instead, which in Thai is still a plain word [S].
- **Vietnamese**: "most words are created by either compounding or reduplicative derivation. Affixation is a relatively minor derivational process." It has Sino-Vietnamese suffixes *-viên*/*-giả* (agent), *-gia* (profession), *-học* (field), *-hóa* (causative "-ize") and prefixes *khả-* (ability) and *phi-* (not) [2nd, unsourced Wikipedia].
- **Yoruba**, usually called isolating, has productive nominalizing prefixes [S]: *a-* (agent: *afúrúgbìn* "sower"), *ì-*/*à-* (action, agent), *oní-* "owner/seller/doer of" (= *o-* + *ní* "have"), and negative *àì-* (Eleshin 2021; Awobuluyi via AJOL [2nd]).
- **Pattern** [I]: every analytic language here has a small set of near-grammatical heads for **person-who-does, thing-for-doing, place, action, quality, and field**. All of them came from ordinary nouns or verbs (人, 学, *mɔ̌*, *oní* < "have").

## 3. Transparency helps processing and learning

- **Head transparency matters most.** In English lexical decision, compounds with a transparent head (*bedroom*, *strawberry*) were faster than those with an opaque head (*jailbird*, *hogwash*). Head transparency also shaped decomposition and repetition effects (Libben et al. 2003) [S].
- In derived words, recognition gets faster as transparency rises (Wurm 1997; Baayen et al. 2007; Balling & Baayen 2008). Morphological priming varies with semantic transparency [S, Plag & Balling review].
- **Family size.** A root that occurs in many derived words and compounds is recognized faster. The effect counts word types, not tokens (de Jong, Schreuder & Baayen 2000) [S]. It is semantic: dropping opaque family members from the count *improves* the fit (Schreuder & Baayen 1997) [S].
  - So a productive, transparent marker builds a large family for every root it attaches to [I].
- **Mandarin results are mixed.** Some studies find transparent compounds faster and others find no difference (Myers et al. 2004a vs 2004b; Mok 2009 facilitatory) [2nd].
  - Compound-structure awareness explains vocabulary growth in Chinese children beyond phonological awareness [2nd].
- **Children (Clark).** Clark's principles for acquiring word formation are **transparency**, **simplicity** (least change of form), **productivity** and **conventionality** (Clark & Berman 1984) [S].
  - English children aged 3–6 all understood novel *-er* nouns. In production, the youngest "relied on simple compounds, especially for agents" before using *-er* for agents and then for instruments (Clark & Hecht 1982) [2nd, abstract via search].
  - Children learning Hebrew or English coined agents before instruments. Hebrew children preferred suffixes after age 4 and compounded less, because compounding is less useful in Hebrew (Berman & Clark, ERIC ED222047) [S].
  - [I] Children use what is formally simplest and transparent first. In this language a free marker word is as simple as a compound, because no affix changes form, so the usual simplicity/transparency trade-off disappears.

## 4. Fixed-relation marker vs generic-noun head

- **Noun–noun compounds leave the relation open.**
  - CARIN (Gagné & Shoben 1997) uses 16 relation types (LOCATED, FOR, MADE OF, ABOUT, DURING…). The modifier's usual relation predicts how fast a compound is understood: *mountain* + LOCATED was .82 in their data [S, via Maguire et al. 2007].
  - A British National Corpus (BNC) reanalysis (Maguire et al. 2007) [S]:
    - *mountain* is LOCATED in only 51% of corpus compounds (*mountain peak*, *mountain chain*), and *book* is ABOUT in only 22%.
    - Two raters assigning relations agreed only **68%** (κ = .65), and many compounds fit several relations.
    - 4–8% fit none of the 16 relations (*chocolate eater*, *water supply*).
- **An expressed action is what makes a coinage predictable.**
  - Štekauer 2005 splits naming units by which parts are expressed. Type 1 expresses all three parts (*language teach-er*). Type 2 expresses the action and the base (*teach-er*). Type 3 leaves out the action (*honey bee*, *dog spade*) [2nd, Mason 2011].
  - He calls types 1, 2 and 4 "predictability boosting" and type 3 a "serious obstacle". In one experiment, type 3 units averaged a Predictability Rate of 0.322 [2nd]. Mason's replication found type 3 predictable when other expressed parts filled the gap.
  - [I] This explains the blind test exactly. **ewo wungu** "person lead" is type 2: a verb is present, so person = doer. **ewo yaye** "person sky" is type 3: no action, and the relation could be LOCATED, FROM, ABOUT or FOR.
- **Generic heads still decode when the head word is clear** (Mandarin 人, 店) [S]. What fails is an unknown relation, not a generic head [I].
- **Markers drift into polysemy anyway.**
  - English *-er* covers agent (*killer*), instrument (*washer*), location (*diner*), patient (*loaner*) and inhabitant (*New Yorker*). *Sleeper* and *smoker* each have 5–8 senses.
  - Bauer: "affixes with multiple distinguishable meanings are more common" than one-meaning affixes, and he suggests that one-meaning affixes become polysemous over time [S].
- **Gap:** I found **no experiment that compares a dedicated affix with a generic-noun head on the same novel bases**. The project's own blind test is the closest evidence.

## 5. Lexicalization and drift

- **Relative frequency drives drift.** Derived words more frequent than their base (*government* vs *govern*) are harder to decompose and more likely to drift. Absolute frequency does not predict drift (Hay 2001) [2nd].
- Across six corpora, four languages and two centuries, the rate of semantic change falls with word frequency by an inverse power law ("conformity"). Words with more senses change faster ("innovation") (Hamilton, Leskovec & Jurafsky 2016) [S].
- **Anecdotes.**
  - *computer* meant "one who computes" from 1613. Its machine sense came with 1940s electronic computing [2nd].
  - *hardware* has meant metal goods since the mid-15th century, and computer equipment by 1952 [2nd].
  - [I] Both stayed compositional ("thing that computes"). What changed was the referent, and *-er*'s agent/instrument polysemy made the change cheap. Transparency survived because the pattern stayed productive.
- **Compound heads become affixes.** Derivational affixes arise from compound members by "constructionalization" (Hüning & Booij 2014) [2nd]. Mandarin 学 and 性 did so with no change of sound [S]. English *-hood*, *-dom* and *-ly* come from Old English nouns *hād*, *dōm* and *līc* [R].
- **Rate:** I found no general figure for how fast derived words lose transparency. The cases above took centuries, while Mandarin person suffixes turned over within about a decade [S/I].

## Implications for the design

These are proposals, not decisions [I].

**1. The problem is the relation, not compounding.** Head + verb (**ewo wungu**) already decodes; head + noun with no action (**ewo yaye**) doesn't. So fix the relation in as few places as possible.

**2. Provide a small set of one-syllable, unstressed relation markers that head a verb base.** Ranked by evidence and usefulness:

| # | Category | Evidence | Source of the requirement |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | AGENT (one who does V) | top-two category everywhere; children's first | typology + Clark |
| 2 | PLACE (where V happens) | 40/50 location sample; deverbal type most common | Bauer |
| 3 | INSTRUMENT (thing for V-ing) | agent/instrument/location polysemy hotspot; children's second | Rainer, Clark |
| 4 | PATIENT/RESULT (what is V-ed or made) | RESULTATIVE 15/40; patient a common gap | networks book, Lieber |
| 5 | FIELD/PRACTICE (study or craft of X) | Mandarin 学, Vietnamese *-học* | **added by me**; weakest evidence |
| 6 | GROUP (set of X) | Mandarin 族/党; collective | **added by me** |

The markers fix the relation, and that matters more than the head being a generic noun. One syllable each beats **Ewo** (two syllables), and as a head it comes first, matching Thai *nák-*/*khrʉ̂aŋ-*. Keep AGENT, INSTRUMENT and PLACE separate even though natural languages merge them: *sleeper* and *smoker* show the cost of merging. Expect speakers to stretch them into each other anyway (Bauer). Make the three markers sound maximally different, under the existing rule for slot-mates.

**3. Proposed deletions.** These need no derivation because syntax already covers them:

| Category | Already covered by | Add-back trigger |
|---|---|---|
| Action noun, quality noun | conversion by position (**SEpi**) | blind readers confuse "the running" with "runner" or "run" |
| Causative | **MUge** | causative phrases get clipped or misread in tests |
| NOT, privative | **mu** / a "without" word | a "without" word keeps getting dropped |
| Diminutive, augmentative | "small"/"big" modifiers (English is analytic too) | an affectionate sense separate from size keeps being needed |
| Gender | already a modifier | — |
| Ability (*-able*), inchoative, reversative | verb + verb (**WAlo** "can/try") | the 100-word coining test needs them often |

Item 6 (GROUP) also goes until a coining test needs it.

**4. Count: 4 core markers, at most 6.** Thai manages with about 5–8 such heads [2nd/R]. Mandarin's open-ended person suffixes show what happens without a fixed set: fashion-driven churn.

**5. Noun + noun coinages.** Bare N+N gets one default reading, "N1 characterized by N2". Any other relation is spelled out with an existing relation word ("from", "for", "made of", "like"). That turns type 3 coinages into type 1. The cost is one unstressed syllable.

**6. Transparency.** Keep markers productive, since family size helps and opaque members don't count. Never give a marker a second job, because polysemy speeds drift (Hamilton). Accept that frequent coinages will lexicalize (Hay). Transparency is maintained by keeping the *pattern* alive, not by freezing individual words.

**7. Test before building.** The roadmap's 100-word blind test should compare marker vs **Ewo**-head vs bare N+N on the same bases. That is the experiment the literature lacks.
