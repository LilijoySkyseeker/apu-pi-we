# Politeness: mitigation, typology, drift, indirect requests, routines

Tags: **[read]** = primary text read; **[abs]** = abstract or publisher summary; **[2nd]** = secondary source; **UNVERIFIED** = not confirmed in any source I opened. Related notes: [high_stakes_domains.md](high_stakes_domains.md) (Avianca, SMCP markers), [grammaticalization-and-drift.md](grammaticalization-and-drift.md) §3, [stance_pragmatics.md](stance_pragmatics.md) (CCSARP).

## 1. Mitigated speech and fidelity

- **Linde 1988** (Language in Society 17:375–399) studied 8 accident CVR transcripts and 14 simulator sessions using a 4-point mitigation scale. Five hypotheses held:
  - requests to superiors are more mitigated (marginal on χ²);
  - mitigation drops in crew-recognised emergencies and problems, but "it never disappears entirely, no matter how serious the situation";
  - topics raised with more mitigation are more often dropped;
  - suggestions the captain ignored were more mitigated.

  [read, [ERIC ED267656](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED267656.pdf); [journal abstract](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ380203)]
- **Fischer & Orasanu** (NASA NCC 2-933 final report, 1999 symposium paper "Say it again, Sam!") [read, [NTRS](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19990047594/downloads/19990047594.pdf)]:
  - *Study 1*: 157 US pilots answered written scenarios. Captains mostly used commands and first officers mostly used **hints** ("That return at 25 miles looks mean"). When risk rose, first officers used more crew-obligation statements, but "hints remained their predominant strategy, even in high risk situations."
  - *Study 2*: 376 European pilots showed the same status pattern, with smaller status differences.
  - *Study 4*: 63 pilots rated effectiveness. **Commands were rated *less* effective than crew-obligation statements ("I think we need to deviate right about now")**, even at high risk. Adding a problem or goal statement raised effectiveness without changing perceived directness.
  - Caveat: these are ratings of written vignettes, not observed outcomes.
- **NTSB SS-94/01** reviewed 37 accidents from 1978–90 [read, [rec. letter A-94-1–5](https://www.ntsb.gov/safety/safety-recs/recletters/A94_1_5.pdf)]:
  - Monitoring/challenging failures were 70 of 302 errors (23%).
  - 28 of 51 tactical-decision errors went unchallenged.
  - The captain was flying in >80% of the accidents, against about 50% of normal flights.
  - A first officer "may be concerned that a challenge … may be perceived as a direct challenge to the captain's authority."
- **Air Florida 90 (1982).** On the takeoff roll the first officer said: "That don't seem right, does it? Uh, that's not right … Naw, I don't think that's right. Ah, maybe it is." [[CVR transcript, AVweb](https://avweb.com/features_old/cvr-transcript-for-the-crash-of-air-florida-flight-90/)] Avianca 52 ("priority", never "emergency") is covered in high_stakes_domains.md.
- **Medicine.**
  - *TeamSTEPPS tools*: **CUS** ("I am Concerned / Uncomfortable / this is a Safety issue") and the **two-challenge rule** (voice a concern at least twice; if ignored, escalate) [[AHRQ CUS tool](https://www.ahrq.gov/hai/tools/ambulatory-surgery/sections/implementation/training-tools/cus-tool.html)].
  - *Pian-Smith 2009*: in simulation, two-challenge and advocacy-inquiry debriefing raised residents' challenges to superior physicians [abs, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19444045/)].
  - *Raemer 2016*: an RCT with 71 practising anaesthesiologists found **no significant effect** of a 50-minute speaking-up workshop [abs, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26703413/)].
  - *Lemke et al. 2021*: in observed real anaesthesia inductions, 37% of speaking-up instances were "oblique hints". Inquiry was what got issues discussed [abs, [BJA](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0007091221005225)].
  - *O'Connor et al. 2008* (meta-analysis of 16 studies): CRM training improves attitudes and behaviour, but there is no evidence on accident rates [abs, [Galway](https://research.universityofgalway.ie/en/publications/crew-resource-management-training-effectiveness-a-meta-analysis-a-3/)].
- **What fraction of failures involve mitigation?** **No source gives one.** The nearest figures are NTSB's 23% of errors as unchallenged errors, and Lemke's 37% of routine speak-ups as hints. Linde's data are failed flights only. Popular claims (Gladwell, *Outliers*) are **UNVERIFIED** here.

## 2. Politeness typology

- **Brown & Levinson 1987**: face is universal, and requests are face threats. The choice of strategy (bald → positive → negative → off-record) scales with distance, power and imposition. Matsumoto (1988) and Ide (1989) object that Japanese honorifics are obligatory social indexing ("discernment"), not a strategy the speaker chooses [2nd, [summary](https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/SALT/article/download/1650/694/4071)].
- **WALS ch. 45 (Helmbrecht)** [read, [WALS](https://wals.info/chapter/45)]. Second-person politeness distinctions in 207 languages:

  | Type | Languages |
  |---|---|
  | None | **136** |
  | Binary | 49 |
  | Multiple | 15 |
  | Pronoun avoidance | 7 |

  - None: the Americas, New Guinea, Australia, most of Africa.
  - Binary: a "hotbed in Europe".
  - Multiple: South Asia.
  - Avoidance: Southeast Asia (Burmese, Indonesian, Japanese, Khmer, Korean, Thai, Vietnamese, per [Yoon 2021](http://isli.khu.ac.kr/journal/content/data/38_3/3.pdf)).
  - Polite pronouns come from plurals, demonstratives, reflexives or status nouns. All of these start out as indirect reference.
- **Speech levels** are areal and rare.
  - Korean has 6–7 verb-ending levels. *Hage* and *hao* are merging into *haeyo* [2nd, [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_speech_levels)].
  - Javanese has ngoko / madya / krama (+ krama inggil). Many young speakers command only ngoko [2nd, [Atlantis](https://www.atlantis-press.com/article/125982946.pdf)].
- **Verdict.** Some politeness system is plausibly universal. *Grammaticalised* pronoun politeness is a minority pattern (34% of the WALS sample), and speech levels are rarer still and receding. That address terms (names, kin terms, titles) exist in every language is likely but **UNVERIFIED** as a typological count.

## 3. Drift

- **Polite pronouns wear down** [2nd unless marked]:
  - **English**: *ye/you* became the polite singular in the 13th century; *thou* was largely gone by c.1650–1700 [[Yoon 2021](http://isli.khu.ac.kr/journal/content/data/38_3/3.pdf), read].
  - **Spanish**: *vuestra merced* spread in the 16th century and had become *usted* by the 17th–18th [[Illinois thesis](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/44787)].
  - **Portuguese**: *vossa mercê* > *vosmecê* > *você* > *cê* [[Coelho](https://ufsj.edu.br/portal-repositorio/File/vertentes/Vertentes_32/socorro_coelho.pdf)].
  - **German**: *Ihr* > *Er/Sie* > *Sie*, each a new layer [2nd].
  - **Korean**: *tangsin* became an honorific 2nd-person pronoun in the late 18th century and "now has no higher status than *ne*" [read, Yoon 2021].
  - **Japanese**: *kisama* and *omae* were once respectful and are now rude [2nd, non-scholarly; dates **UNVERIFIED**].
  - **Rate**: irregular; no source was found for a fixed cycle, and some systems held 300–400 years (*usted*, German *Sie*, English *you*). *(Corrected 2026-10-12 after the fact-check, C32: was "about 150–300 years per cycle", which had no source.)*
  - **Brown & Gilman 1960**: European T/V shifted from a power semantic to a solidarity semantic [2nd].
- **Mechanism (Yoon 2021, read).** Frequent polite forms suffer "attrition of value". Open-class address terms can simply be replaced, but pronouns are a closed class, so the result is a *paradigm gap* (pronoun avoidance). Also: "Lowering is easy; raising is hard". First-person *humble* forms (Korean *ce*) stay stable.
- **A dedicated politeness morpheme does bleach.**
  - English *please* (< *if it please you*) is first attested as a stand-alone marker in 1771.
  - British *please* is now a "bleached", routine form.
  - Both varieties have developed *prod* and *dismissal* uses ("Oh, please"), which are more frequent in American web English [abs, [Murphy 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/english-language-and-linguistics/article/separated-by-a-common-impoliteness-marker-please-in-american-and-british-webbased-english/3EDE29FABD5DB11786565545E0DC1664)].
  - So **ku will inflate**. The open question is where the wear lands.

## 4. Indirect requests

- **CCSARP** (Blum-Kulka & House 1989): conventional indirectness ("Could you…") is preferred.
  - Australian English: 82.4% conventionally indirect, <10% direct, 7.8% hints.
  - German: 20.5% direct, 76.7% conventionally indirect, 2.8% hints.
  
  [2nd, [GWU paper](https://gbl.digital.library.gwu.edu/articles/volume_24/10_4079_gbl_v24_2.pdf)]. Direct-request rates of Hebrew 33%, Canadian French 24% and Argentine Spanish 49% are **UNVERIFIED** (search summary only).
- **Blum-Kulka 1987**: English and Hebrew speakers rated conventional indirectness most polite. **Hints were rated most indirect but *less* polite**, because "pragmatic clarity … is an essential part of politeness" [2nd].
- **Imperatives are not universally rude.**
  - Polish and Russian imperatives make up 20% and 35% of requests [abs, [Ogiermann 2009](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/JPLR.2009.011/html)].
  - Wierzbicka (1985) calls English imperative-avoidance culture-specific [abs, [ANU](https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/different-cultures-different-languages-different-speech-acts-poli/)].
  - Lee-Wong (1994): Chinese imperatives with *qing* are not impolite [title only; claim **UNVERIFIED**].
- **Misreading.** Clark 1979 phoned merchants with conventional "Could you tell me the time you close?": 0 of 30 answered only the literal question. Non-conventional forms ("Do you close before seven?") drew literal answers more often [read, [Clark 1979](https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1970s/Clark.responding.79.pdf)]. Between native speakers, conventional indirect requests are rarely misread. The documented losses are **hints** (§1) and cross-cultural transfer.

## 5. Routines

- **Thanks**: Floyd et al. 2018 studied 8 languages and 928 complied-with requests [read, [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5990755/)].
  - Gratitude was expressed in **5.5%** of them.
  - English 14.5% (highest), Cha'palaa 0%. Cha'palaa has no conventional "thank you" at all.
  - The norm is tacit acknowledgement.
- **Greetings**: Duranti (1997) gives six cross-cultural criteria [2nd, [course summary](https://korean.sitehost.iu.edu/E305/greeting_criteria.html)]:
  1. occurs near a boundary;
  2. establishes a shared perceptual field;
  3. is an adjacency pair;
  4. is predictable;
  5. opens a unit of interaction;
  6. recognises the interlocutor.

  Greetings are widely attested, but universality is assumed rather than counted (**UNVERIFIED**).
- **Apologies and farewells**: I found no typological survey; universality is **UNVERIFIED**.

## Implications for the design

The parts, with their sources (⊕ = added by me):

1. **No T/V, no speech levels** (source: WALS, Yoon, Korean/Javanese decline). Polite pronouns inflate (irregularly; the "150–300-year cycle" once here had no source, cut 2026-10-12, C32), and because pronouns are closed-class they can't be renewed. Let deference live in **ku** and in open-class address (names, titles), where turnover is cheap. *Add-back trigger:* back-translations show speakers coining honorific pronoun substitutes or avoiding **me** ("you") toward superiors.
2. **Expect ku to inflate, and design for containment, not prevention** (source: *please*). Predicted path: bare **tin** comes to sound curt, **so ku** becomes neutral, **ku ku** becomes polite. Keep **ku** freely iterable so renewal happens by stacking inside its own slot. The failure to watch for is renewal by *recruiting* hedges, questions, **fon** or low confidence digits.
3. **The real leak is speech-act choice, not the words added.** Subordinates downgrade the *act* (hint, statement, or **fon** instead of **tin**/**yo**), and urgency doesn't remove this (Fischer: hints persist at high risk; Linde: mitigation "never disappears"). **ku** alone doesn't stop it. Remedies:
   - (a) State as a norm that **yo ku ku** / **tin ku ku** are fully polite. Deference lives in ku, so no one needs to downgrade the act. Blum-Kulka's finding supports this: clarity is part of politeness.
   - (b) ⊕ Teach the effective pattern from Fischer Study 4 as usage: a problem statement + an explicit act + a joint framing ("we need…"), e.g. **yo**: fuel low. **tin ku**: **pen** (we) land now. No new particle. *Add-back trigger:* tests show joint-obligation requests getting coded as **fon**.
   - (c) ⊕ Consider one conventional, rank-neutral escalation routine, like CUS or the two-challenge rule: a repeated **yo** means "I'm escalating". Evidence for training these is weak (Raemer null result), so make it a *convention of the language*, not a training programme.
4. ⊕ **Urgent register: no ku expected.** Mark it with an urgency token, so that dropping ku in an emergency reads as urgent, not rude. *Source:* SMCP/aviation phraseology, by analogy. Untested. *Deletion trigger:* drop it if ku proves not to become obligatory.
5. **Minimal routines.** Provide one word each for greeting, farewell, thanks and sorry. Mandate none of them: thanks is rare even where it exists (5.5%). ⊕ A greeting can double as a channel-open check (Duranti's "shared perceptual field"). *Deletion candidate:* a "you're welcome" reply. *Add-back trigger:* tests show a thanks with no reply being read as rude.

Doghouse flag (once): items 3c and 4 are process I added, not something the evidence demands. Build them only if back-translation shows urgency being lost.
