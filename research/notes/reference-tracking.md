# Reference tracking: can humans use numbered third-person "slots" in speech?

Devil's-advocate evidence for the conlang slot-pronoun proposal (slots 1–3 by order of introduction; slot pronoun only when referents compete; loci in the signed form). Compiled 2026-10-08. Status labels: **sourced** = read in the cited abstract or text; **secondary** = reported by another paper I read, original not read; **UNVERIFIED** = from memory or not found.

## 1. Capacity

- **Cowan 2001** (BBS 24:87–114): there is "a single, central capacity limit averaging about four chunks", which he proposes as the capacity of the focus of attention. Sourced: [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11515286/).
- **Pylyshyn & Storm 1988** (Spatial Vision 3:179–97): subjects tracked "up to 5 objects in a field of 10" with 87% accuracy, where serial scanning would predict about 40%. The paper proposes FINSTs, a small set of parallel indexes. Sourced: [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3153671/).
- **Arnold & Griffin 2007** (JML 56:521–536): even when a pronoun was unambiguous (Minnie with Donald), the mere presence of a second character in the discourse cut pronoun use and slowed reference. They attribute this to "competition for attentional resources in the speaker's representation of the discourse." Sourced: [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18449327/). So the discourse model feels pressure from just two referents.
- **Gernsbacher & Hargreaves 1988** (JML 27:699–717): first-mentioned participants are more accessible. Secondary: [Springer citing it](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03196293). This bears on the proposal: slot 1 already gets the first-mention advantage.

## 2. Why sign loci work

- **Spatial indexing.** Richardson & Spivey 2000 (Cognition 76:269–95): when asked about facts that had been presented in screen regions, participants made more saccades to the now-empty region during recall. This is the "looking at nothing" effect. Sourced: [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10913578/). Location is encoded even when it's irrelevant, so loci attach referents to a memory system people already use for free.
- **How many loci in practice.** Kuhn writes: "psychological constraints prevent signers from using more than a small number (about four) in a given discourse… loci must be recycled" ([Kuhn, *ASL Loci: Variables or Features?*](https://semanticsarchive.net/Archive/jMzZGJhY/kuhn-ASL-loci.pdf), §4.1). This is an assertion with **no cited count**, and I found no corpus measurement. Treat "about four" as UNVERIFIED expert opinion. The signer's own body is often one more referent, via role shift.
- **Acquisition is late.** Source: Chen Pichler's acquisition chapter in *Sign Language: An International Handbook* (De Gruyter 2012) ([PDF](https://lingdept.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/ch-28-acquisition-chen-pichler.pdf)).
  - ASL pronouns with *present* referents are mastered by about 27 months, the same timing as English pronouns. Iconicity doesn't speed this up (Petitto 1987).
  - Agreement with *non-present* referents was "not consistently correct until 4;09" (Loew 1984). Children made "stacking" errors (one locus for two referents) at 3;6–3;11, and errors continued to 5;0 (Bellugi et al. 1988).
  - Three-year-olds could map 2–3 referents to loci, but did better with two than three, i.e. memory-limited (Lillo-Martin et al. 1985).
  - Referential shift: the markers appear at 3–4 but are "inconsistent in their timing and scope until the age of 6 or 7" (Reilly 2006). In BSL, 4–6-year-olds overused full NPs, and only 11–13-year-olds integrated simultaneous events (Morgan 2006).
  - All of these primary studies are secondary for me, via the chapter. **Verdict: verified.** Discourse-level locus use is a late (5+) skill, even for native signers.

## 3. Spoken co-speech gesture already does loci

- **Abstract deixis** (McNeill, Cassell & Levy 1993, *Semiotica* 95): speakers point at empty space when introducing a character ("and there was [this guy]"). Secondary: [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/270480455_Abstract_Deixis).
- **Gullberg 2006** (Language Learning 56:155–196): studied Dutch learners of French retelling stories. Anaphoric gestures anchor referents in gesture space. Their *presence* is not interactionally motivated, but their *spatial articulation* is: it changed with addressee visibility. Sourced: [ERIC abstract](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ733038).
- **So, Kita & Goldin-Meadow 2009** (Cognitive Science 33:115–125): speakers used gesture location to identify referents mostly when speech *already* specified the referent uniquely. Gesture went "hand-in-hand" with lexical specificity rather than compensating for pronouns. Secondary: [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1551-6709.2008.01006.x) (403 for me), and the Gunter et al. abstract below, which says it "typically provides redundant information."
- **Reliability is partial.** Gunter, Weinbrenner & Holle 2015 summarize the literature this way:
  - Gullberg: gesture space was used consistently "only in 42% of the time."
  - So et al.: "approximately 35% of the time."
  - Alamillo et al. 2010: "68% of their adult participants showed consistent usage."
  - Sourced: [Frontiers in Psychology 6:80, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4321330/).
- **Listeners do use it.**
  - Gunter et al. found a gesture–speech locus mismatch produced N400/P600 effects even though gesture was task-irrelevant (sourced, same link).
  - Goodrich & Hudson Kam 2012: a cohesive gesture toward the second-mentioned character pushed ambiguous-pronoun interpretation against the first-mention bias. Secondary, via Sekine & Kita.
- **Development.** Sekine & Kita 2015 (LCN 30:1245–58):
  - Comprehension: 5-year-olds were at chance on comprehending cohesive gesture space, 6-year-olds above chance, 10-year-olds adult-like.
  - Production: children "rarely" produce cohesive gestures before about 9, and use them frequently from 10–11.
  - Sourced: [WRAP accepted manuscript](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/67617/9/WRAP_Cohesive_LCP_4_for_postprint_server.pdf).

## 4. Ordinal / arbitrary labels in speech

- **"The former / the latter."** I found **no experimental or corpus study** on their spoken frequency or processing cost. Searches returned only general anaphora work. The claim that they are rare in speech and costly is UNVERIFIED (my recall/intuition).
- **Numeric or arbitrary labels for discourse referents.** I found **no experiments**, and know of no natural language that grammaticalizes ordinal reference to discourse participants. UNVERIFIED: absence of evidence from a limited search.
- What the theories predict (inference):
  - Ariel's accessibility theory ([Routledge](https://www.routledge.com/Accessing-Noun-Phrase-Antecedents/Ariel/p/book/9781138965737)) and Gundel, Hedberg & Zacharski 1993's givenness hierarchy ([Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeanette_Gundel); [Gundel paper](http://www.zacharski.org/papers/ghz/GundelStuttgart.pdf)) both tie referring forms to the referent's *current salience*, not its *history*.
  - A slot number encodes introduction order. That is a fact the listener must have stored separately, and it drifts away from salience as a story proceeds.

## 5. Natural spoken systems

**Obviation (Algonquian)**
- In a clause with several animate third persons, "one, normally the most topical, is designated as proximate… any others must be obviative." It is **obligatory** in transitive clauses with two third-person arguments and on possessed nouns with a third-person possessor. Source: Oxford, *Algonquian* ([PDF](https://home.cc.umanitoba.ca/~oxfordwr/papers/Oxford_Algonquian_Routledge.pdf)).
- It is a **two-way** system: proximate vs. everyone else. It does not distinguish obviatives from one another.
- Assignment is by narrative salience: "the hero of the discourse" (Goddard), or the point-of-view character (Dahlstrom). Narrators can deliberately shift it. Source: Volchok 2019 MA thesis ([PDF](https://mspace.lib.umanitoba.ca/bitstream/1993/33852/1/Volchok_Irina.pdf)).
- Acquisition data are thin. In Northern East Cree, one child acquired the obviative *possessor* suffix last of the possessor suffixes, at 4;07. The input was infrequent, and Henke suggests obviation is semantically harder. Source: Henke 2024, J Child Lang ([Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-child-language/article/frequency-perceptual-salience-and-semantic-complexity-the-acquisition-of-possessor-inflection-in-northern-east-cree/5BF44BA8D79FE866FEE6FE02FDC7FC9B)); n=1, one construction. Other CCLAS obviation work exists only as conference talks ([list](https://www.mun.ca/cclas/project-output/presentations/academic-presentations/)).

**Logophors (West Africa)**
- In Igbo, *Ò sìrì nà Ò byàrà* means "He_i said that he_j came," and *Ò sìrì nà yá byàrà* means "He_i said that he_i came."
- The ordinary/logophoric opposition is "usually obligatory": you can't use the logophor for non-coreference or the plain pronoun for coreference (Culy 1994, 1997, as reported by Comrie). This contrasts with "optional" long-distance reflexives (e.g., Icelandic *sig*).
- Weak spot: in "X heard from Y that…", some languages (Ewe) let either X or Y trigger the logophor, which leaves the sentence ambiguous.
- The feature is areal: West Africa only.
- Source: Comrie 2004, *West African Logophorics and the Typology of Reference-Tracking*, JWAL 30.2 ([PDF](https://journalofwestafricanlanguages.org/downloads?catid=101&id=422&m=0&task=download.send)).
- This maps directly onto "Mara told Dilo that he won."

**Switch-reference**
- Each chained clause marks whether the next subject is the same or different (Sarvasy & Choi 2020, Frontiers editorial, [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7661487/)).
- Acquisition: children in clause-chaining languages (Ku Waru, Nungon, Pitjantjatjara, etc.) chain clauses by about 2;6, with "early accuracy in switch-reference marking."
- I did not verify typological frequency figures (UNVERIFIED).

**Do these systems reduce ambiguity?** I found no experiment that directly measures ambiguity reduction for obviation or logophors versus a control. The disambiguating function is argued from grammar: obviation and logophors force a reading, as the Oji-Cree and Igbo examples show. One processing study ([Hammerly, Ojibwe](https://christopherhammerly.com/publication/ojibwe-rcs/ojibwe-RCs.pdf)) reports that proximate nouns trigger agent-first commitments during parsing; that is secondary, from a search snippet, and I didn't read the full paper.

## 6. Speakers don't notice ambiguity

- **Ferreira, Slevc & Rogers 2005** (Cognition 96:263–84): speakers "almost always" avoided *nonlinguistic* ambiguity (two flying bats) but only "sometimes" avoided *linguistic* ambiguity (animal bat vs. baseball bat). They avoided the latter consistently only after already producing it. Sourced: [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15996561/).
- **Keysar & Henly 2002** (Psych Sci 13:207–12): speakers "underestimate the ambiguity of their own utterances and overestimate" how well their cues work. Informed overhearers don't make this mistake. Sourced: [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12009039/).
- **Keysar et al. 2000** (Psych Sci 11:32–38): listeners use an egocentric heuristic first and correct afterwards. Secondary: [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/1467-9280.00211).
- **Arnold & Griffin 2007** (above): referring-form choice is driven partly by the *speaker's* attention, not by the listener's need.
- **Repeated-name penalty** (Gordon, Grosz & Gilliom 1993, Cognitive Science 17): sentences that re-mention a focused subject by name are read *slower* than with a pronoun. Over-explicit reference to the topic has a comprehension cost. Secondary: [Wiley record](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1207/s15516709cog1703_1), [Springer review citing it](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10936-017-9529-4).

## Implications for the design

**Does numbered-slot reference work for speech?** Partly, and the slot *count* is not the problem.
- Three slots plus a plain pronoun fits the roughly 4-item attention limit and the (unverified) roughly 4 loci signers reportedly use. *Inferred.*
- The weak link is the *index*. Spoken slots key on introduction order, but every natural system keys on something the listener already has in mind:
  - current salience (obviation, accessibility),
  - a structural role (logophor = the reporter; switch-reference = previous subject),
  - or space, which comes with free indexing (loci, gesture, "looking at nothing").
- An arbitrary number has none of these. The listener must store "Dilo = 2" as an extra fact, and that fact stops tracking salience as the story moves on.
- I found no natural language or experiment that supports ordinal reference. That is not proof it fails, but nature offers no precedent.

**What would work better (natural precedents):**
1. **Proximate/obviative** for the main competition: the topic vs. the others. It is salience-based and obligatory within the clause. Limit: it's two-way, so three-way competition still needs something else.
2. **Logophoric pronoun** for "Mara told Dilo that he won." It is obligatory and attested. Specify which argument (source or subject) triggers it to avoid Ewe-style ambiguity.
3. **Make spoken slots spatial, not numeric.** Spoken slot words could be deictics ("this-side one / that-side one") paired with abstract-pointing gesture. Speakers already gesture this way, but only 35–68% of the time and redundantly, and listeners are sensitive to it. Then speech and sign really share one grammar: the locus *is* the slot, and the word names the locus.
4. If numbers stay, reassign them by salience (slot 1 = current topic) rather than by introduction order, and allow explicit re-slotting, as signers recycle loci.

**Optional or obligatory?** Make it obligatory and structurally triggered.
- An "only when referents compete" rule requires the speaker to detect linguistic ambiguity. That is exactly what Ferreira et al. and Keysar & Henly show speakers are bad at, so optional marking will be under-used where it matters most.
- The attested systems that reliably disambiguate (logophors, obviation within a clause, switch-reference) are obligatory in their domain, and switch-reference is acquired early and accurately.
- A workable trigger is "a second third-person referent is active." Arnold & Griffin show speakers already respond to that, ambiguous or not. A trigger that depends on whether ambiguity exists won't work as well.
- Caveat: don't force explicit slot marking on the sole topic. The repeated-name penalty suggests over-marking the salient referent slows comprehension.

**Remaining risks**
- **Acquisition.** In every modality, discourse-level reference tracking matures late: loci with absent referents by about 5, referential shift consistent by 6–7, cohesive gesture production by 10 or later. Native child speakers will likely misuse slots until school age. Clause-local systems (switch-reference, logophors) are safer.
- **More than 3–4 referents.** Recycling and reassignment rules are needed; signers recycle loci.
- **Drift.** Introduction order goes stale over a long narrative.
- **Number homophony.** Slot words that resemble numerals will clash with real counting.
- **Evidence gaps.** There is no corpus count of loci, no former/latter processing data, and no experiment on arbitrary-label reference. The ambiguity-reduction value of obviation and logophors is argued from grammar, not measured.
