## 1. The languages, one by one

Each entry follows the same order where sources allow: aim; designer and method; sounds and writing; grammar; vocabulary; precision; learnability evidence; what users changed; governance; adoption; tooling; and what it means for Apupiwe. Main histories used throughout:

### 1.1 Schematic languages: regular systems with European roots

#### Volapük (Johann Martin Schleyer, 1879-80)

- **Aim.** "First to produce a language capable of expressing thought with the greatest clearness and accuracy; second, to make its acquisition as easy as possible"; for grammar, "the first requisite is regularity, and the second is simplicity" ([Sprague 1888, *Hand-book of Volapük*, Introduction](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Hand-book_of_Volap%C3%BCk/Introduction)). Garvía calls it "a solution in search of a problem": Schleyer first designed a world phonetic alphabet, then a language to carry it, conceived in one night in 1879 (Garvía 2015, pp. 21-23). The movement never agreed what it was *for*: Schleyer wanted a written "new Latin" for the educated; Kerckhoffs's French wing wanted a commercial code (Garvía 2015, pp. 26, 45, 158).
- **Sounds and writing.** Eight vowels including ä ö ü; stress always final; roots avoided "difficult and unusual sounds", and *h* ([Sprague 1888, Sounds](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Hand-book_of_Volap%C3%BCk/Sounds_and_Letters)); *r* was used sparingly (restored in de Jong's 1931 revision [secondary]).
- **Grammar.** Agglutinative and heavily inflected: four cases by suffix, tense by vowel prefix, person by suffix, plus passive, moods and more ([Sprague 1888, Cases; Tenses](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Hand-book_of_Volap%C3%BCk/Tenses)). A stenographer counted "505,440 ways of using the language's verbal modes", and some Volapükists took this as proof of power (Garvía 2015, p. 28).
- **Vocabulary.** About 40% of roots from English, the rest mostly German and Romance (Sprague 1888). Roots had to begin and end in a consonant so that the vowel affixes were always separable, i.e. a *parsing-transparency rule*; together with brevity and collision avoidance this made coinage "a combinatorial challenge" (*pük* from "speak" because *pik* was taken; *vol* from "world") (Garvía 2015, p. 23). Result: the American Philosophical Society's 1888 committee found it "too inflectional and its vocabulary unrecognizable" (Garvía 2015, p. 42). Root count: not found **[unverified]**.
- **Precision.** Schleyer wanted room "for the smallest nuances and subtleties of human cognition"; Kerckhoffs argued the complexity "could ruin the language and the movement" and cut forms (Garvía 2015, pp. 44-45).
- **Learnability evidence.** None beyond assertion.
- **Governance and collapse.** Bylaws made Schleyer "supreme leader"; a majority counted only if he voted with it (Garvía 2015, p. 25). In 1888 he declared: "Any resolution of the Academy that has not been accepted by the Inventor is null" (p. 47). When the 1889 congress let the Academy override him, he claimed the language as his property and founded a rival academy (pp. 47-48). He kept changing the language himself, "which obliged his loyal supporters to purchase new dictionaries and unlearn old words" (p. 50). Peak 1889: 253 clubs, 14 journals, almost 900 certified teachers (p. 47); 9 clubs by 1905, the last dissolved 1909 (p. 50). The popular "one million speakers" is a movement claim **[unverified, likely inflated]**.
- **Design or governance?** Garvía: governance first; the reformers spent years designing a language that did not yet exist, "there was no point in propagating, learning, and teaching a language that did not yet exist", while Esperanto was available (pp. 156-157). But design was the *content* of the quarrel (inflection, unrecognisable roots) (p. 45). Zamenhof first blamed its sounds and vocabulary (1889), later said it died because its users "did not let it evolve" (p. 155).
- **For Apupiwe.** Volapük is the nearest ancestor of Apupiwe's form engineering: roots reshaped to satisfy a formal constraint, at the cost of recognisability. For native children (C2) unrecognisability doesn't matter; for the author learning it and the people it is shared with, it does. Its second lesson, a sole author who keeps changing the language after others learn it, is exactly Apupiwe's present shape (one author, 194 commits in six days, freeze lifted).

#### Esperanto (L. L. Zamenhof, 1887)

- **Aim.** A neutral second language for everyone. Zamenhof's "crucial insight", in Fettes's words, "that it must develop through collective use; accordingly, he restricted his initial proposal to a minimalist grammatical sketch, a vocabulary of some 900 words, some samples of poetry and prose, and a persuasive introductory essay" ([Fettes, "Esperanto and language awareness"](https://www.esperantic.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/Fettes_Esperanto-and-Language-Awareness.pdf)). Privately he went further, in a line that anticipates Apupiwe's native framing: "An international language will be consolidated for ever only in case there will be some group of people who accept it as their familial and inherited language. A hundred of such people are enormously more important for the idea of a neutral language than millions of other people" (Zamenhof, letter of 1901, translated in [Lindstedt 2010, "Esperanto as a family language", in Dervin (ed.), *Lingua francas*, L'Harmattan](http://esperantic.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/Lindstedt_2010_Family.pdf)).
- **Designer and method.** One person, publishing as "Doktoro Esperanto", who declared on page two: "The international language, as any other language, should be a common property, for which its author resigns forever to all personal rights to it" (Garvía 2015, p. 66). He called himself the "initiator" rather than the creator (p. 69).
- **Sounds and writing.** 28 letters, five vowels, phonemic spelling, diacritics on ĉ ĝ ĥ ĵ ŝ ŭ (Garvía 2015, p. 66); fixed penultimate stress (Fundamenta Gramatiko rule 10, [Akademio de Esperanto](https://www.akademio-de-esperanto.org/fundamento/gramatiko_angla.html)).
- **Grammar.** Agglutinative: part-of-speech endings (-o noun, -a adjective, -e adverb, -i infinitive), an obligatory accusative *-n* (rule 2), adjective agreement, a closed set of affixes; compounds are head-*last* ("the principal word standing last", rule 11). Sixteen rules on six pages (Garvía 2015, p. 66; [UEA facila, "La Unua Libro"](https://uea.facila.org/artikoloj/lingvo/la-unua-libro/)). The rules never fixed word order; usage did (Lindstedt 2006, below).
- **Vocabulary.** Roots chosen by frequency among Indo-European languages, hence mostly Latin-Romance (Garvía 2015, p. 66). Size: **917 roots** in the first book's word list ([UEA facila](https://uea.facila.org/artikoloj/lingvo/la-unua-libro/)); the *Universala Vortaro* of 1894, later part of the *Fundamento*, has **2,636 entries** (roots plus affixes and endings; my count of the Akademio's online edition, [universala_vortaro.html](https://www.akademio-de-esperanto.org/fundamento/universala_vortaro.html)). So Apupiwe's design-log figures (about 900, then about 2,600) check out, with the caveat that the 2,636 includes grammatical morphemes. Later dictionaries run to tens of thousands of words **[exact counts unverified]**.
- **Word classes in use.** Wim Jansen's corpus-based study finds that Esperanto's roots started out largely category-neutral (one root gives noun, verb, adjective and adverb with predictable meanings by ending), but that an "increasing number of verbally specialized lexemes" has emerged in use; he reads this as natural diachrony in which "verbal specialization precedes all other forms of syntactic specialization" ([Jansen 2013, "Radikoj kaj vortoj en esperanto", *Esperantologio / Esperanto Studies* 6:9-43, doi:10.59718/ees29613](https://doi.org/10.59718/ees29613)). This bears directly on Apupiwe's decision to have no word classes.
- **Learnability evidence.** The best controlled study (Essex, 2013-17): English 8-9-year-olds learning Esperanto gained more in their target language in a year than those learning French (45% vs 14%, different tests), but there was **no** later head start for French and no gain in language awareness; "learning Esperanto could not compensate for low language learning aptitude" ([Roehr-Brackin & Tellier 2018, *LPLP* 42(1)](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/24243/)). The famous "150 hours vs 1,500" table traces to the Esperantist Helmar Frank's own journal, with numbers that change between retellings; treat it as advocacy (details in `auxlangs-parts/6-cross-cutting.md` §1b). IALA's Thorndike studies (1933) found about a fifth of the learning time of French, but "the experimental conditions were not adequate to allow strong conclusions" (Fettes, citing Fantini & Reagan 1992).
- **Native speakers (denaskuloj).** About 1,000 by Lindstedt's estimate, "perhaps one per cent of all the speakers"; "All first-language speakers of Esperanto are at least bilingual ... and practically all of them use another language more often than Esperanto in their adult lives"; "there are no compact native speech communities"; Esperanto "has been nativised ... but it has not been creolised" (Lindstedt 2010). Bergen's study of eight children found no compound tenses or aspect affixes, reduction of unstressed grammatical syllables ("a good deal" in Bergen's words; Apupiwe's fact-check, C17, reads his data as about 5% of cases), and the accusative in about half its contexts ([Bergen 2001, *J. Child Lang.* 28](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~bkbergen/papers/NEJCL.pdf)). Lindstedt's rebuttal: no change yet found that isn't transfer from the children's other native language, a spoken-versus-written register difference, or incomplete acquisition; natives may change little because "Esperanto has already been adjusted to the requirements of language universals ... in the process of being used by non-native speakers for more than 120 years" (Lindstedt 2010; [Lindstedt 2006](https://journal.fi/finjol/article/view/153130/98645)). Full notes: `designed-languages-going-native.md` §1. Two further details matter for Apupiwe: for natives Esperanto is "primarily a spoken language", and "not all of them acquire good literacy in it"; and Esperantists coined *krokodili* ("speak another language when Esperanto should be spoken"), a sign of how hard keeping the language in use is for a scattered community (Lindstedt 2010).
- **Governance.** Three pieces. (1) The 1894 subscriber referendum on reform: 157 against, 107 for, 257 abstaining (Garvía 2015, p. 75, Table 1). The debate itself was costly: *La Esperantisto* fell from close to 900 subscribers to half, German subscribers from 98 to 10 (p. 75). (2) The 1905 Boulogne congress made the *Fundamento* "netuŝebla" (untouchable) as a baseline, and set up a Lingva Komitato that, unlike the Volapük Academy, "did not have any power to enforce reforms. It could only make suggestions" (p. 83). (3) Change then came from use: "Several grammatical and lexical changes ... have not been due to official or unofficial language planning ... but have been initiated and spread by anonymous speakers, being codified only afterwards (or not at all)" (Lindstedt 2010). The standard modern grammar (Wennergren's PMEG) is mainly descriptive (ibid.).
- **Adoption.** Garvía's explanation: a "bottom-up" strategy, an agreed baseline, then diversification into many special-interest groups; an "inner idea" (peace, fraternity) that held a core through crises (Garvía 2015, pp. 157-158). External limits: France blocked the League of Nations proposal of 1920-22; the French education minister banned Esperanto teaching in 1922 (pp. 161-163); persecution in Germany and the USSR and the post-1945 rise of English (Fettes). Speaker numbers are unknown: published estimates run from 120,000 to 15 million; Lindstedt guesses about 100,000 active users and 10,000 fluent, each within half an order of magnitude (Lindstedt 2010). Wandel's Facebook-based 2 million ([Wandel 2015, *INDECS* 13(2)](https://doi.org/10.7906/indecs.13.2.9)) is undermined by his own check: Klingon scored 250,000 "speakers" by the same method. The Esperanto Wikipedia had 368 active editors on 2026-10-13 against a few dozen for every other auxlang (`auxlangs-parts/4-modern-auxlangs.md`, snapshot table).
- **Tooling.** Dictionaries in many languages from the start (*Universala Vortaro* in five), a large literature, Google Translate support since 2012 (Wandel 2015).
- **For Apupiwe.** Esperanto is the only case where a designed language got native speakers at all, and it shows what those natives are like: bilingual, scattered, more spoken than literate, shaped mainly by their other language. Apupiwe's insides are designed for monolingual-style native acquisition (C2); the only real precedent says its natives would be bilingual children in families, and the design should expect transfer as the main force. Zamenhof's governance (give it away, freeze a small core, let usage lead) is the one that worked, and Apupiwe's B9 and CC0 licence match it. The Jansen finding is a direct warning that a class-free root system may grow a verb class in use.

#### Ido (Delegation for the Adoption of an International Auxiliary Language, 1907)

- **Origin.** The Delegation's committee (linguists Baudouin de Courtenay and Jespersen, chemist Ostwald, with Couturat and Leau as secretaries) examined a dozen schemes in 1907; at a late meeting Couturat presented an anonymous project, "Ido", a radically reformed Esperanto; the committee accepted Esperanto "in principle ... on condition that it is changed ... in the direction indicated by ... the project called Ido" (Garvía 2015, pp. 133-134). The author turned out to be de Beaufront, the Esperantists' own delegate, working with Couturat (pp. 136-137).
- **Design changes.** No diacritics, no obligatory accusative, no adjective agreement, and Couturat's derivation rules (Garvía 2015, p. 134). The central principle was **reversibility**: "Every derivative must be reversible; that is to say, if one passes from one word to another of the same family in virtue of a certain rule, one must be able to pass inversely from the second to the first in virtue of a rule which is exactly the inverse" (Couturat, quoted in Jacob 1947, ch. VIII.2, [PALviii](https://archive.org/download/interlanguages/PALviii.html)). Roots were classed as verbal or nominal; "to crown" needs an explicit affix (*kron-iz-ar*), "to hammer" another (*martel-ag-ar*) (ibid.).
- **What users did with it.** The precise affixes *-if-*, *-ig-*, *-iz-* "were said to be difficult to master by the average student", and the Interim Academy of 1937 received proposals to let a single vague *-i-* stand in for them "for ordinary speech", keeping the precise ones for scientific terms (Jacob 1947, ch. VIII.3). This is the clearest historical case of a precise derivation system being pushed, in use, toward one underspecified marker.
- **Governance.** An Academy of scientists and linguists whose decisions bound the speakers; Garvía calls this "exactly the same mistake that the Volapükists had made and the Esperantists had avoided" (Garvía 2015, p. 138). Couturat held that "there is only one ideal international language" and that reform should continue so the language would not get "crystallize[d] by general usage" (pp. 137, 140). The pace: the 1914 English reprint of the 1910 handbook dropped its Ido specimens because "the difference between 1910 Ido and that of 1914 onwards is great enough that it may cause confusion" (p. 138). A stabilisation period (1911, extended in 1914 for ten years) produced two official Idos, "klasika" and the changing one (p. 140). Idist leaders even discouraged original literature as building an irrational "love to the language" that would block progress (p. 140). Jespersen, Ostwald, Baudouin and others left (pp. 138-139).
- **Adoption.** About a quarter of the Esperanto *leadership* moved to Ido; most rank-and-file Esperantists did not (Garvía 2015, p. 136). By 1925 the Ido Uniono had 500 members; in 1926 it split again into reformist and conservative camps (p. 142). The Ido Wikipedia today: 64,832 articles, 63 active editors (snapshot, part 4).
- **For Apupiwe.** Ido is the warning about *improving* a language after people have learned it, and about the belief that a technically better design wins by being better. It also tests Apupiwe's derivation idea: four explicit markers (**no ge du pu**) are closer to Ido's logic than to Esperanto's looseness. Ido's users wanted fewer, vaguer markers; Apupiwe's own blind A/B test kept markers because they were shorter (rationale §5.1). Watch whether speakers collapse them.

### 1.2 Naturalistic languages: extract the vocabulary Europe already shares

The naturalistic school held that an international vocabulary already exists in the European languages and only needs extracting. Its trade, stated openly by its founders, is recognisability for some people at the price of neutrality for everyone.

#### Latino sine flexione (Giuseppe Peano, 1903)

- **Aim and method.** The whole programme in one line: "vocabulario internationale, orthographia latino, sine grammatica"; words common to English, French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, German and Russian, in Latin stem form; plural *-s* used only when needed ([Peano, *Interlingua*, 1927, pp. 1, 4](https://archive.org/details/PeanoInterlingua)). Claimed to be "intelligibile ad primo visu aut quasi" to educated readers (p. 3).
- **Governance.** From 1910 the Academia pro Interlingua's voted rules were advice "obligatorio pro nullo", binding on no one (p. 4): a deliberate absence of a standard.
- **What critics found.** Jespersen could not parse its sentences reliably (*more* = "custom" or "died"?), and advice to "take the ablative" is useless to someone without Latin; an auxiliary language "must necessarily be autonomous and have its own grammar and dictionary" ([Jespersen, *An International Language*, 1928, ch. "Latino sine flexione"](https://archive.org/details/AILjespersen)).
- **Adoption.** Mainly Peano's mathematical circle (*Formulario mathematico*); faded after his death in 1932 **[trajectory secondary]**.
- **For Apupiwe.** "No grammar" turned out to mean "the grammar of whatever language you already know". It is the extreme of leaving everything to context; Apupiwe's B3 (a precise form always exists) is the right correction.

#### Occidental / Interlingue (Edgar de Wahl, 1922)

- **Aim.** Openly European: English as world language would mean "die geistige Herrschaft des Angelsachsentums über Europa ... Europa muß seine eigene Sprache haben"; in introducing a language, "die Gewohnheiten der Europäer" count for more than adapting to "Orientalen, die an den Formen nicht mäkeln werden" ([de Wahl 1930, "Wege und Irrwege zur Weltsprache"](http://dicta.bplaced.net/bibliotheca_occidental/odw/13_wege.html)). Economy is measured not in letters, words or rules "sondern im Kraftaufwand beim Erlernen und Gebrauch" (ibid.). He admits that regularity, phonetic spelling, logical derivation, internationality and naturalness cannot all be had; balancing them is a matter of "künstlerischen Empfindens" (ibid.).
- **Designer.** One person, thirty years' gestation, published 1922 when the League of Nations took up the question, then eight years of trial in use; about 15 named helpers (de Wahl 1930; [*Radicarium directiv*, 1925](http://dicta.bplaced.net/bibliotheca_occidental/radicarium_directiv.html)).
- **Writing.** Historical, not phonetic, spelling (*c* = ts before e/i), to keep derivational families visible (*critic*, *criticism*); de Wahl tried another value for *c* and abandoned it "nach längeren praktischen Versuchen", a design changed by use ([de Wahl 1930, "Begründung"](http://dicta.bplaced.net/bibliotheca_occidental/odw/65_begruendung.html)).
- **The engineering idea: de Wahl's rule.** Most international words come from about 100 irregular Latin verbs. A short algorithm forms the "perfect stem" from the infinitive (drop *-r/-er*; add *-t* after a vowel; *d*/*r* become *s*: *vid-er* → *vision*, *curr-er* → *curs*), and nouns of action add *-ion* to it ([Pigal, "Li essentie de Occidental"](http://dicta.bplaced.net/bibliotheca_occidental/essentie.html)). Regularity of *derivation*, not of surface. Its limits are admitted: the static/dynamic verb split behind *-ie* vs *-ion* "ne es absolut strict" (ibid.).
- **Precision.** Strict one-word-one-meaning is "un reva ínrealisabil"; polysemy is natural and context resolves it (Pigal). Many partly overlapping affixes, on the view that the unfit ones "will die by themselves" in use (ibid.).
- **Vocabulary.** Romance/Latinate with common Germanic words; the 1925 dictionary was deliberately "directiv": it shows the direction in which users should extend it, as living languages do ([*Radicarium directiv*](http://dicta.bplaced.net/bibliotheca_occidental/radicarium_directiv.html)). Launch root count not found **[unverified]**.
- **Learnability evidence.** None. "Sofortige absolute Verständlichkeit für jeden Westeuropäer" is asserted, not tested (de Wahl 1930). Jespersen's reply: "when Occidental is praised as very easy, it is chiefly by people who are already familiar with two or three of the great European languages" ([Jespersen 1928, ch. "Occidental"](https://archive.org/details/AILjespersen)).
- **Governance and fate.** An Occidental-Union from 1928; renamed Interlingue in 1949; when IALA's Interlingua appeared in 1951, leading figures moved to it **[secondary: Wikipedia, History of Interlingue]**. A small online revival; the Interlingue Wikipedia has 13,804 articles and 29 active editors (snapshot, part 4).
- **For Apupiwe.** De Wahl's rule is the best historical example of what Apupiwe does with derivation markers: a small algorithm that yields predictable derived words. His candour about the trade-offs ("artistic judgement") is worth copying; his Eurocentrism is the thing Apupiwe's B5 and N3 exist to avoid.

#### Novial (Otto Jespersen, 1928)

- **Aim.** "The second language to everybody"; the maxim: "that international language is best which in every point offers the greatest facility to the greatest number", including facility for speaker and writer, not only the reader (Jespersen 1928, Introduction; Jacob 1947, ch. IV.1, [NovHist](https://archive.org/download/interlanguages/NovHist.html)).
- **Designer.** One person, "the first interlanguage ever framed by a professional philologist" (Jespersen 1928), after presiding over the Ido Academy 1907-10.
- **Vocabulary method.** Internationality counted by *speakers*, not by languages; even so, the result is "very much more Romanic in appearance than might be anticipated", because English's Romance layer joins French, Italian and Spanish (Jespersen 1928, ch. "Vocabulary"). He rejected both over-precision (three Ido verbs for "to tame") and Esperanto-style root economy (*malsato* "hunger" must be learnt anyway) (ibid.).
- **Grammar.** Analytic: invariable verb plus auxiliaries (*me protekte, me ha protekte, me sal protekte, me vud protekte*), passive with *bli* chosen over German-looking *vird* to avoid a false friend; common-gender pronoun *le*; one "democratic" *vu* for all addressees (Jespersen 1928, chs. "Verbal System", "Pronouns"). When critics showed *me ha lekte e studia* is ambiguous, he answered that context and optional words resolve it, "language is only meant for such [natural sentences]" (ibid.).
- **What use changed.** In 1934-37 Jespersen admitted alternatives "saying that experience and experiment should show which are the better", and created an "Orthographic Novial" that brought back *c* and *z* beside the original phonetic spelling, so there were now two standards (Jacob 1947, ch. IV.6, [NovChanges](https://archive.org/download/interlanguages/NovChanges.html)).
- **Fate.** No academy; Jespersen was the authority; the movement went quiet after his death in 1943 **[secondary; no careful history of 1943-1990 found]**. The community came from Ido and had IALA's project to move to.
- **For Apupiwe.** The cleanest case of a language whose authority was one living person, with unresolved alternatives at the moment the person stopped. Apupiwe has the same structure (one author decides; Claude drafts). Esperanto's answer was to freeze a small core and hand over before the founder left.

#### Interlingua (IALA, Alexander Gode, 1951)

- **Institution and method.** The International Auxiliary Language Association, founded 1924 with money from Alice and Dave Morris, ran research for 27 years: Thorndike's learning experiments at Columbia (1933), Sapir's "Foundations of Language" studies, word-collection methods tested at Liverpool 1936-39, then André Martinet and Alexander Gode as research directors ([Gode, *Interlingua-English Dictionary*, 1951, Foreword](https://www.interlingua.com/ied/intro/)). IALA first meant to choose among existing languages; in 1945-46 it presented four models from very naturalistic to moderately schematic and polled interested people; the naturalistic ones won (P 26.6%, M 37.5%, C 20%, K 15%) **[secondary: Wikipedia, Interlingua; the 1945 General Report not read]**.
- **The vocabulary rule.** A word is international if attested "in corresponding forms and with corresponding meanings -- in at least three of the language units, Italian, Spanish and/or Portuguese, French and English, with German and Russian as possible substitutes"; it enters in its "prototype" form, the nearest common ancestor of the variants, "with the proviso that the resultant form must never be conditioned by a trait restricted to one single contributing variant"; words that are identical in form but differ in meaning across the sources are excluded (Gode 1951, Introduction). Gode is frank about the base: English "is so strongly romanic that ... it is a Romance language"; the Anglo-Romance group has "close to half a billion speakers" (ibid.). About 27,000 entries (ibid.).
- **Design by procedure.** "The international vocabulary is as much subject to further growth as are the vocabularies of all national languages, but its future problems must not be left to depend on subjective decisions. Their solution must result from the application of a specific technique" (Gode 1951). This is the same stance as Apupiwe's ladders and tools.
- **Grammar.** Minimal: grammatical features absent from any of the primary control languages were dropped **[secondary: Wikipedia, Interlingua grammar]**.
- **Learnability evidence.** None measured. The claims that Romance speakers understand it at sight on first reading (often "80-90%") appear only in secondary or AI-written summaries **[unverified]**.
- **Adoption.** The first printing was 5,000 copies; twenty years later Gode reports use "in such varied fields as molecular spectroscopy and demography" (Gode 1951, preface to the 1971 edition). Interlingua Wikipedia: 30,312 articles, 56 active editors (snapshot, part 4).
- **For Apupiwe.** Interlingua is the most rigorous algorithmic vocabulary before Lojban and the closest precedent for "a rule, not a person, decides each word". It also shows the limit: the algorithm is only as neutral as its input languages, and the input was chosen for the designers' world. Apupiwe's input (CLICS lineages, Wordbank, modern frequency) is broader, but its test texts and readers are English (rationale §11).

#### Lingua Franca Nova / Elefen (C. George Boeree, from 1965; online from 1998)

- **Aim and sources.** Vocabulary from Catalan, Spanish, French, Italian and Portuguese; grammar "un simpli de la gramatica comun" of those languages, resembling "la gramatica de la creoles romanica, como aitian, capoverdean, papiamento e xavacano" ([Elefen grammar](https://elefen.org/salon/gramatica/xef.html)). Boeree's reason for not adding other families: "by adding words and other things from many other unrelated languages, I add little to Elefen's learnability for the people of China, etc., while reducing greatly the learnability for those familiar with the Romance languages" ([Elefen FAQ](https://elefen.org/salon/lingua/faq.html)).
- **Designer and governance.** One person, then an online group; a second group (Europijin) for those wanting more creole-like changes; "Bjorn and I and others of the two groups agreed that a number of these suggestions would indeed improve Elefen, and we eventually adopted them" (FAQ). Boeree died in January 2021 (FAQ).
- **What use changed.** "Elefen has become slightly less 'creole-like' over time. For example, the affixes have become far more general than originally intended. Originally, -or was for tradespeople, but now is used for almost any actor ...; -eria has gone far beyond its original use as a place of business ...; and -ador is no longer only for tools. In reverse, the use of broadly defined prepositions has lessened." Boeree attributes this to speakers' native languages and to Esperanto, "a second language to most of our crew" (FAQ). A suffix *-os* was changed to *-osa* "to avoid confusion with plurals" (FAQ).
- **Size and status.** "Nearly 10,000 Elefen entries" (FAQ); ISO 639-3 code `lfn` (FAQ); Wikipedia 4,686 articles, 21 active editors (snapshot, part 4).
- **For Apupiwe.** Elefen's affix drift is a direct prediction for Apupiwe's **no** (doer), **du** (tool) and **pu** (place): narrow derivation markers widen in use. And its users replaced broad prepositions with narrower ones, the opposite of the minimalist bet. Both are worth putting on Apupiwe's watch list (`docs/drift.md`).

### 1.3 Reduced vocabularies: a few hundred words and composition

#### Basic English (C. K. Ogden, 1930)

- **Design.** A subset of English: 850 words, with only 18 verbs (Ogden's "operators") that combine with particles and nouns to replace thousands of verbs ("put together" for *assemble*) **[secondary: Wikipedia, Basic English; Ogden's 1930 *Basic English: A General Introduction* not accessible to me; the 850-word list is at [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/ogdens-basic-english-words-list-alphabetic)]**.
- **Critique.** Rhiemeier, quoting Rosenfelder: "Ogden and Richards cheated": the phrases that replace the missing words "must be learned individually like the words they replace", so a closed vocabulary only "displaces the problem rather than solving it" ([Rhiemeier 2007](https://www.joerg-rhiemeier.de/Conlang/auxlang-design.html)). Roosevelt's joke to Churchill, that Basic could offer only "blood, work, eye water and face water", is the popular version of the same point ([International Churchill Society, *Finest Hour* 135](https://winstonchurchill.org/publications/finest-hour/finest-hour-135/riddles-mysteries-enigmas-basic-english/)).
- **Politics.** Churchill promoted it in his 1943 Harvard speech (ibid.); a subset of one national language reads as that nation's project.
- **Learnability evidence.** No controlled study found (part 6 §1c).
- **What survived.** Not the closed list but the looser idea of a controlled core plus whatever domain terms a text needs, as in VOA's Special English and in Simplified Technical English (1.8).
- **For Apupiwe.** Apupiwe's ladder ("say it with words you have → a new root only when earned") is Basic English's bet made with an exit: roots can be added where phrases fail a test. Basic English shows the cost if the exit is used too little: every phrase standing for one concept is a new item to learn (Mühlhäusler's Tok Pisin finding below says the same).

#### Glosa (Ronald Clark and Wendy Ashby, 1972-92, from Lancelot Hogben's Interglossa, 1943)

- **Design.** Fully isolating: "Words remain always in their original form ... A Glosa word represents an idea, but no part of speech. The same word can function as a verb, noun, adjective or preposition within reason"; grammar is "operator words and the word order"; roots from Latin and Greek; a core "Glosa 1000" for everyday use and "Glosa 6000" for science and art; Latin alphabet without special characters ([glosa.org, English introduction](http://www.glosa.org/en/)).
- **Evidence and adoption.** None measured; a small correspondence community **[size unverified]**.
- **For Apupiwe.** Glosa is the closest earlier design to Apupiwe's grammar (isolating, no word classes, operator particles, order carries role, a 1,000-word core plus extension). It drew its roots from the learned vocabulary of one civilisation; Apupiwe keeps the grammar and replaces the roots. That nobody adopted Glosa says nothing against its grammar; it shows that grammar was never what decided adoption.

#### Toki Pona (Sonja Lang, 2001; book *pu* 2014; dictionary *ku* 2021)

- **Aim.** Minimalism as a philosophy: a small language for simple thought, not an international auxiliary language in the classical sense.
- **Design.** About 120 words in *pu*; 14 phonemes; syllables (C)V(n), the same template Apupiwe reached independently (earlier report). Isolating; words are class-free; modifiers follow heads; the particle *li* separates subject and predicate.
- **Precision.** Deliberately vague. One study mapped the lexicon to 4,027 WordNet senses, about 30 per word ([Fabbri et al., arXiv:1712.09359](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.09359)). Vecderg's examples: "I ate" is *tenpo pini la mi moku* ("during past time, I eat") against Spanish *comí*; there is no word for anger, only *pilin utala* ("feeling of struggle") ([vecderg, "Why there can't be a perfect auxlang", 2024](https://www.vecderg.com/articles/auxlangs2)).
- **What use changed.** Words *pu* had dropped (*leko, monsuta, kipisi*) stayed in use and returned in *ku* (2021), which was compiled from community polls; the 2024 Linku survey counts 118 core words used by at least 90% of speakers plus a tail of common, uncommon and obscure words (`designed-languages-going-native.md` §6; fact-check C48). Minimalism held at the core; the edges grew.
- **Learnability evidence.** No controlled study; the "30 hours" figure is folklore (part 6 §1c).
- **Adoption.** The 2024 census had 1,997 responses; 53% reported at least conversational level; 49% use it at least weekly; Discord dominates; 52% started with jan Misali's free video course ([Toki Pona census 2024](https://tokiponacensus.github.io/results2024/)). ISO 639-3 `tok`, adopted 2022-01-20 ([SIL](https://iso639-3.sil.org/code/tok)). A 2025 corpus study finds "sociolinguistic factors influence Toki Pona in the same way as natural languages" ([Huang & Joo, arXiv:2508.10246](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.10246)).
- **For Apupiwe.** The one modern success grew by being small, free, fun and taught well, not by precision. Its users accepted vagueness and extended the lexicon from below; its author ratified usage rather than fighting it. Apupiwe aims the other way on precision (B3) and size (about 1,500 roots), which is consistent with its goal, but it should take the governance lesson: describe usage, don't legislate it (already B9 and N4).

### 1.4 Logical languages: unambiguous by construction

#### Loglan (James Cooke Brown, 1955-)

- **Aim.** A test of the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis: "a reasonably culture-free second language with known formal properties" for a "laboratory-like setting" ([Brown, *Loglan 1*, 4th ed. 1989, ch. 1](http://www.loglan.org/Loglan1/chap1.html)). In 1989, 34 years in, "the crucial experiments have not yet been run" (ibid.).
- **Vocabulary algorithm.** Primitives blended from the eight most-spoken languages of 1955, weighted by speakers, scored by a computed "recognizability": "The average recognizability score of a Loglan composite word over this immense source population is 45%" ([*Loglan 1*, ch. 6](https://www.loglan.org/Loglan1/chap6.html)). This is a computed overlap of sounds, not a measured recall rate; the learning evidence offered is anecdotal ("30 days of an hour a day of vocabulary drill ... seems to have been sufficient") (ch. 1).
- **Governance and split.** When volunteers built a flashcard program in 1986, Brown demanded they "sign a copyright acknowledgement and agree to pay royalties, claiming copyright on the individual words"; the community relexified the whole language rather than accept ([LeChevalier, "The Loglan-Lojban Dispute"](https://mw.lojban.org/papri/the_Loglan-Lojban_Dispute); a partisan source). The courts later held "the Institute's mark, LOGLAN, is generic" ([*Loglan Institute v. Logical Language Group*, 962 F.2d 1038, Fed. Cir. 1992](https://www.leagle.com/decision/19922000962f2d103811839)).
- **For Apupiwe.** Ownership kills. Apupiwe's CC0 dedication already covers text, data and code (README; design log 2026-10-11), so the word list is free in a way Loglan's never was.

#### Lojban (Logical Language Group, 1987-)

- **Aim and claims.** "Based on the principles of predicate logic"; "an unambiguous yet flexible grammar"; "unambiguously resolves its sounds into words"; "neutral between cultures"; "1300 root words can be easily combined to form a vocabulary of millions of words"; "regular; the rules of the language are without exceptions" ([CLL 1.1](https://lojban.github.io/cll/1/1/)). Not invented by one person: "Often, grammatical features were introduced into the language long before their usage was fully understood" ([CLL 1.2](https://lojban.github.io/cll/1/2/)).
- **Sounds and words.** Latin letters, one sound each; word classes identifiable from shape so the speech stream segments unambiguously; about 1,350 five-letter roots (*gismu*), CVCCV or CCVCV, stressed on the first syllable ([CLL 4.1, 4.4](https://lojban.github.io/cll/4/4/)).
- **The gismu algorithm (the closest precedent to Apupiwe's optimiser).** For each concept, words from Chinese, English, Hindi, Spanish, Russian and Arabic were respelled in Lojban sounds; every possible root form was scored by shared letters in order, weighted by speakers (Chinese 0.36, English 0.21, Hindi 0.16, Spanish 0.11, Russian 0.09, Arabic 0.07); candidates were removed if they differed from an existing root only in the final vowel or by one consonant from a "too similar" set (b~p,v; c~j,s; l~r; m~n; ...); the top scorer usually won ([CLL 4.14](https://lojban.github.io/cll/4/14/)). Two things matter here. First, the **stressed vowel was exempt** from the similarity filter: *bradi, bredi, bridi, brodi* are all roots (ibid.). Second, the concept list was never designed: "The gismu do not represent any sort of systematic partitioning of semantic space ... the gismu list was being collected for almost 35 years and was only weeded out once" ([CLL 4.4](https://lojban.github.io/cll/4/4/)). About 70 "cultural gismu" had to be hand-made outside the algorithm ([CLL 4.15](https://lojban.github.io/cll/4/15/)).
- **Did the roots work?** The official claim is 8-12 weeks at an hour a day for all 1,350 roots ([LLG brochure](https://www.lojban.org/publications/level0/brochure/lojbanmo.html)), with no study behind it. Users disagree: "The vocabulary is unmemorable" ([Johansen, "Reasons not to like Lojban"](http://arj.nvg.org/lojban/anti-lojban.html)); Robin Lee Powell, author of the main parser and former chair of the language committee: "The language is *physically* hard to speak", citing "self-similarity of existing words, and the vast array of accidental tongue twisters" ([lojban list, 26 Oct 2014](https://groups.google.com/g/lojban/c/e7cg5vy2EfA/m/aJaC09ERWasJ)). A mechanical distinctness filter did not prevent words from feeling alike to the people saying them.
- **Precision.** Syntax: a formal (YACC) grammar, though in 1997 it parsed only part of the language and the word-segmentation algorithm "has not yet been fully debugged" ([CLL 21](https://lojban.github.io/cll/21/1/); [CLL 4.1](https://lojban.github.io/cll/4/1/)). Meaning: "All tanru are ambiguous semantically" ([CLL 5.2](https://lojban.github.io/cll/5/2/)); compounding is "a poetic or creative act, not a science" ([CLL 4.5](https://lojban.github.io/cll/4/5/)). So Lojban is unambiguous where a machine checks it and vague where speakers make meaning, which is where the meaning is.
- **What users changed.** The article system: CLL's *lo* meant "things which really are cats" with a default quantifier; the 2004 "xorlo" reform made *lo* generic and removed default quantifiers ("There are no default quantifiers. At all") ([xorlo](https://mw.lojban.org/papri/xorlo)). The logic-derived default was too heavy for use. Experimental particles and rival article systems followed; one proposal comes with a word for "I am now speaking dialect X" ([zipcpi, gadganzu](https://mw.lojban.org/papri/zipcpi:_Yet_another_gadri_article)). The ilmentufa repository now ships several PEG grammars, standard and experimental ([ilmentufa](https://github.com/lojban/ilmentufa)): the formal grammar became the medium in which dialects are defined.
- **Freeze.** The design was "baselined and frozen" in January 1997 for at least five years ([1997 Baseline](https://mw.lojban.org/papri/1997_Baseline_Announcement)). In 2002 the group admitted "people have been unable or unwilling to work on completing the documentation of a baseline lexicon under the freeze conditions"; "our statement, that the language design was 'complete', was therefore premature" ([Baseline Statement 2002](https://mw.lojban.org/papri/Official_Baseline_Statement_(2002/2003))). No printed dictionary followed; the online jbovlaste became the de facto lexicon.
- **Adoption.** 2002: about 375 copies of the reference grammar sold, about 250 on the mailing list, "a couple dozen" conversational speakers ([Lojban FAQ](https://lojban.org/static/resources/faq.html)). Lojban Wikipedia: 1,364 articles, 19 active editors (snapshot, part 4).
- **For Apupiwe.** Lojban is Apupiwe's nearest relative in method: an algorithm chooses word forms, a filter rejects near neighbours, a formal grammar parses the reference. Its record says: (1) a filter on letters is not a test on ears, and the people who spoke it found it hard to say; (2) a formal grammar proves the reference, not the language people speak; (3) the logic-heavy defaults were the first thing users replaced; (4) freezing before the dictionary was done froze the wrong thing. Apupiwe has a better filter (a confusion model, with vowels included, and drift keys) but the same blind spot: no listener has tested it (rationale §11).

### 1.5 A priori languages: meaning in the sound, or no source at all

#### Philosophical languages (Wilkins 1668; Solresol 1817-66; Ro 1904; aUI 1950s)

- **Design.** Wilkins built words from a taxonomy: genus syllable + difference consonant + species vowel, so *Tida* is redness and *Tide* greenness ([Wilkins 1668](https://archive.org/details/AnEssayTowardsARealCharacterAndAPhilosophicalLanguage)). Ro (1904) did the same with category prefixes (colours in *bofo-*); Solresol built 11,732 words from seven solfège syllables, with some opposites as reversals (*fala* "good", *lafa* "bad"); aUI gave every phoneme a meaning **[Ro, Solresol, aUI: secondary, Wikipedia; see the earlier report]**.
- **What went wrong.** One misheard sound gives a plausible neighbouring meaning that context cannot catch; categories go stale (Wilkins filed comets under fire); taxonomies run out of slots and need escape hacks (Rhiemeier 2007; earlier report). Wilkins's enthusiasts "were numerous ... practitioners less so" ([*Notes Rec. R. Soc.*, PMC5906426](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5906426/)).
- **For Apupiwe.** Apupiwe's rationale §3.1 already draws the right lesson (arbitrary roots; similar meanings get dissimilar forms). Rhiemeier states it as a principle: "The arbitrariness of the relation between sound and meaning in human languages is not a bug, it is a feature" ([Rhiemeier 2007](https://www.joerg-rhiemeier.de/Conlang/auxlang-design.html)).

#### Ithkuil (John Quijada, 2004-2023)

- Maximal explicitness: thousands of roots, hundreds of affixes, dozens of cases in its latest version; its author is reported not to speak it fluently **[secondary: Wikipedia and the earlier report]**. The extreme case of obligatory precision: every utterance pays for distinctions most utterances don't need. For Apupiwe this is the argument for B3's wording ("a precise way exists, even when speakers don't use it") over obligatory marking.

#### Kotava (Staren Fetcey, 1978-)

- **Aim.** "Absolute cultural and philosophical neutrality ... by not drawing its lexicon from any existing language ... but inventing it in its entirety"; postulates include "Upgradability" ("the language needs to be living and evolve with its speakers") ([*Kotava Official Complete Grammar* v4.03, 2016/2018, pp. 4-5](https://www.kotava.org/en/Kotava_Grammar_v4.03_englava.pdf)). It claims "Simplicity is absolute rather than relative since no known language is borrowed from" (p. 4), which confuses *equal* unfamiliarity with ease.
- **Design.** 24 letters, five vowels, predictable stress; mostly agglutinative with fusional and isolating streaks; no noun case or gender; prepositions mark every nominal complement; mainly SOV or OSV; a rich verb (moods, aspects, voices, inclusive/exclusive "we") (grammar pp. 4-6).
- **Vocabulary.** Entirely invented; dictionary claims range from about 17,000 entries (2005) to over 30,000 radicals (French Wikipedia, secondary). **How roots were generated is not documented** in any primary source found.
- **Governance.** A linguistic committee meeting yearly "validates or rejects certain proposed evolutions"; the grammar is "simply a description of the language at some point in time" (p. 5). Reforms 1983, 1993, 2008 (plural), 2011 (letter *h* removed), with the admission that old habits and texts mean the change "will only gradually disappear" (p. 5).
- **Adoption.** ISO 639-3 `avk` (2008) ([SIL](https://iso639-3.sil.org/code/avk)); users in the tens by every estimate; mostly French-speaking; the Wikipedia has 29,905 articles but about 92 words each and 17 active editors (snapshot, part 4).
- **For Apupiwe.** The only long-lived a priori lexicon. It shows that invented roots are *sustainable* for a committed group for half a century, and that neutrality alone did not attract a world audience: its users came through French materials. Kotava never published how it made its roots; Apupiwe's documented optimiser is a real advance on that.

### 1.6 Worldlangs: vocabulary from many families

#### Globasa (Hector Ortega, 2019) (brief; full study in `globasa.md`)

- **Aim.** A thought experiment: 1,000 random people from around the world settle an island: "What would the village language look like within three generations?" ([globasa.net](https://www.globasa.net/eng/)). Principles include "Optimal simplicity with the goal of maximum learnability and ease of use for both speaker and listener", and a warning that simplicity "taken too far" brings "reduced functionality, impracticality or even greater difficulty" ([Design Principles](https://www.globasa.net/eng/max/diseno-principi)).
- **Vocabulary method.** First try derivation or compounding; otherwise check 16-17 source languages and "Select the source with the most language families represented", with tie-breakers favouring East Asian, then Arabic, Hindi, European, Persian-Turkish clusters; form constraints override source (no word-final minimal pairs, avoid minimal pairs generally, prefer 2-3 syllables) ([Methodology](https://www.globasa.net/eng/max/metode-fe-lexiseleti)). Outcome: the languages most often among a root's sources are English (60.6%), Spanish, French, Turkish, German; Mandarin is last (22.3%) ([Menalari etymology stats](https://menalari.globasa.net/eng/estatisti-fe-lexiasel), 2026-10-13). Counting families rewards words that colonial and scientific borrowing already spread, which are mostly European.
- **Size and churn.** 1,000 roots at launch; about 3,220 roots in the 2026 word list (my count of [word-list.csv](https://cdn.globasa.net/api2/word-list.csv)). Six recorded phases of change, 175 changes in the first, many of them fixes for ambiguities found in use (an obligatory infinitive particle because *Mi suki oko* meant both "I like the eye" and "I like to see") ([Changes and Adjustments](https://github.com/Globasa-Team/main-pages-grav/blob/HEAD/10.max/04.alogi-ji-lilalogi/default.eng.md)).
- **Governance and tooling.** A development committee since 2022; anyone may propose roots, checked with tools for homonyms and minimal pairs ([Word Proposal Process](https://www.globasa.net/eng/max/proceso-fe-lexijeni)); everything CC0 with a machine-readable dictionary API ([globasa-docs](https://github.com/Globasa-Team/globasa-docs)). The best-engineered process of the group.
- **Evidence.** No learner study. About 494 Discord members (2026-10-13).
- **For Apupiwe.** The closest living project in spirit (a thought experiment, designed for the listener, built with tools, CC0). Two warnings: a "fair" source algorithm still yields a mostly European vocabulary; and paper designs meet their ambiguities only in use.

#### Pandunia (Risto Kupsala, 2007-) and its siblings

- **Aim.** "Evenly global": fair, words from all regions, rejecting Standard Average European as a guide ([Idea page](https://pandunia.info/pandunia/I-idea.html)).
- **Outcome of the sourcing.** Of 1,206 words with etymologies, English is a source for 919 (76%) ([lexi_nume.md](https://github.com/barumau/pandunia/blob/HEAD/pandunia/lexi_nume.md), 2024). The same pattern as Globasa.
- **Churn.** Phonology rolled back in 2017; an Esperanto-like branch split off as Panlingue (2019-21); version 2.0 made derivation isolating, then agglutinative; an English-based sibling, Panglo (2024), whose site says "It's better to speak good Panglo than bad English"; the site banner in 2026 says a new version is being made; in 2025 the factual history page was replaced by a fiction ([history at commit 2c93a18](https://github.com/barumau/pandunia/blob/2c93a184f2b95f28823f848810248a365a66f494/eng/I-historia.md); [Panglo](https://pandunia.info/panglo/index.html)). "Pandunia was Risto's personal language project. It was like play dough to him that could be shaped and reshaped again" (history).
- **Adoption.** The site is honest: "spoken by a small number of people currently" ([site](https://pandunia.info/pandunia/index.html)); about 118 Discord members.
- **For Apupiwe.** Pandunia is what a one-person language looks like after twenty years without a freeze: good tooling, an unstable target, nobody able to learn it. It is the clearest warning for Apupiwe's present pace of change.

#### Lingwa de Planeta / Lidepla (Dmitri Ivanov and a St Petersburg group, 2006-10)

- **Aim.** Based on "the most widely spoken languages of the world, including English, German, French, Spanish, Portuguese, Chinese, Russian, Hindi, Arabic, and Persian"; the motive is psychological: "elements of one's own native language in a constructed language evoke positive emotions and increase greatly one's motivation to learn it"; the belief that a global language "will necessarily arise" and Lidepla is "a prototype" of it ([lingwadeplaneta.info](http://www.lingwadeplaneta.info/en/)).
- **Size.** A 1,500-root dictionary in 2009 (site news); Basic Edition 2010 (ibid.).
- **Rights.** The site is marked "© Dmitry Ivanov 2006-2014 - All rights reserved" (ibid.).
- **Adoption.** Translations of literature (Bulgakov chapters, a magazine *JIVA* to 2018). An ISO 639-3 request was rejected in 2018 because the language did not appear "to be used in a variety of domains nor for communication within a community that includes all ages" ([SIL change request 2017-033](https://iso639-3.sil.org/request/2017-033), quoted by Wikipedia). Sambahsa's request was rejected the same year ([2017-036](https://iso639-3.sil.org/request/2017-036)).
- **For Apupiwe.** Lidepla names a motive Apupiwe's a priori roots give up on purpose: learners like seeing their own language in the new one. Apupiwe is built for natives (C2), for whom this doesn't apply, but its author and the people it is shared with are adults.

#### Sambahsa-Mundialect (Olivier Simon, 2007)

- **Aim.** Simplicity is "only one objective; other objectives are naturalness (implying internationality), brevity, and precision"; core vocabulary from reconstructed Proto-Indo-European, opened to Perso-Arabic and Sinitic words ([Simon, *The Grammar of Sambahsa-Mundialect in English*, 2023, pp. 3-5](http://sambahsa.pbworks.com/w/page/10183084/FrontPage)).
- **Brevity argument.** "'Short' will always look easier and handier than a compound like 'non-lengthy'"; English "looks more practical as a world language than today's most famous auxlangs" because of its short words (p. 4). Four cases kept, with optional endings, inspired by Modern Standard Arabic's optional case endings; one-syllable declined pronouns and articles (pp. 4-5).
- **Size and use.** About 20,000 entries; "the number of speakers hasn't significantly increased" (p. 5). ISO request rejected in 2018 (above).
- **For Apupiwe.** Sambahsa's point about short words is the same as Apupiwe's B8 and its frequency-weighted form lengths. Its cost is the opposite of Apupiwe's one letter, one sound: "Sambahsa's orthographic and accentual system is quite elaborate", to keep loanwords looking as they do in their sources; learners need a separate phonetic transcription, and the project's own pronunciation tool is "reliable over 90%" (pp. 6-7).

#### Neo (Arturo Alfandari, 1937; 1961)

- A schematic language with mostly French roots, published 1937; it drew attention in 1961 with 1,304 pages of courses, grammars and dictionaries (some 75,000 words). "As Alfandari's health worsened, to avoid disappearance of his language, he founded ... the Academy of Neo", which "was not very successful"; after his death in 1969 "the language was mostly forgotten" **[secondary: Wikipedia, Neo language; no primary read]**. An academy founded late, by a failing author, did not carry the language past him: the hand-over has to happen while the author is active.

### 1.7 Zonal languages: one family at a time

#### Interslavic / Medžuslovjansky (Jan van Steenbergen, Vojtěch Merunka and others, 2006-2017)

- **Aim.** Not simplicity but familiarity: in zonal languages "the main focus lies on familiarity and immediate passive understanding"; Interslavic is built from what all Slavic languages share, "or whenever there is no such solution available, in a majority of them" ([Merunka, van Steenbergen, Yordanova & Kocór 2019, "The Interslavic language as a tool for supporting e-democracy in Central and Eastern Europe", *Int. J. Electronic Governance* 11(3/4):260-288](https://interslavic-language.org/doc/ijeg-2019-interslavic.pdf), pp. 269-270).
- **Design.** Inflecting like its sources: three genders, seven cases, adjective agreement, verbal aspect, "very few irregularities". Two criteria: the six main Slavic sub-branches are weighed equally, and words are never borrowed directly from one language but derived regularly from reconstructed proto-forms, "to ensure etymological coherence" (p. 270). Latin and Cyrillic spellings, with automatic transliteration (ibid.).
- **Designers and governance.** Started 2006 as Slovianski with several competing models; in 2009 only the most naturalistic was kept; merged in 2011-2017 with Merunka's Neoslavonic, "both approaches gave almost identical results" (pp. 268-269). "Not the work of a single author ... the ultimate shape of this language is also determined by input from its user community" (p. 269).
- **Evidence: the only real comprehension test in this survey.** An online cloze test (seven words missing from a text) run in all Slavic countries, Nov 2015 to June 2018: 1,822 valid responses; population-weighted mean 84% of missing words recovered; 18% scored below five of seven; self-assessed understanding (70%) was lower than measured (84%); people with higher education scored 88%, others 72-73% (pp. 271-272). Limits: self-selected through social media, five men to every woman, run by the designers.
- **Adoption.** ISO 639-3 `isv`, adopted 2024-04-03 ([SIL](https://iso639-3.sil.org/code/isv)).
- **For Apupiwe.** Interslavic shows what a measured claim looks like: state a hypothesis ("a slightly advanced speaker's passive understanding"), test it on people outside the project, publish the method and the failures. Apupiwe can copy the *method* for B1 even though its vocabulary rules out the zonal shortcut.

#### Folkspraak (online collaboration, 1995-)

- A pan-Germanic project begun on the internet in 1995 that "has not advanced much"; rifts produced rival variants (Middelsprake 2004, Sprak 2006, Frenkisch 2008) **[secondary: Wikipedia, Pan-Germanic language]**. Design by open committee without a decider produced several languages and none finished. The opposite failure to a sole author.

#### Afrihili (K. A. Kumi Attobrah, Ghana, 1970)

- Meant as a lingua franca for all Africa, with phonology, vocabulary and grammar from African languages, chiefly Swahili and Akan **[secondary: Wikipedia, Afrihili]**. A reader of the original 40-page *Ni Afrihili Oluga* reports a verb system that is "an interesting mix of English and Swahili semantics", with a tense-aspect series that mirrors English categories (future perfect, past perfect continuous) ([Afrihili notes, lachi-lochu.conlang.org](https://lachi-lochu.conlang.org/conlang/afrihili/)). It has an ISO 639-2 code `afh` ([SIL](https://iso639-3.sil.org/code/afh)) and no known community.
- **For Apupiwe.** A design from outside Europe still imported the categories of the colonial language its author was educated in. Apupiwe's English-centred instruments (rationale §11) carry the same risk in subtler form: the categories a designer notices are the ones their own language marks.

### 1.8 Controls: what happens to small regular languages, and to engineered sub-languages

#### Tok Pisin (a contact language that children took over)

- **Why a control.** The nearest natural experiment to "give children a small, regular, mostly isolating language": invariant words, preverbal tense-aspect particles (*bin* past, *bai* future, *pinis* completive), inclusive/exclusive "we" (*yumi*/*mipela*), compounding and circumlocution for missing words ([Smith & Siegel 2013, "Tok Pisin", *APiCS Online*](https://apics-online.info/surveys/22)). English is the main lexifier, with Portuguese, Malay, German and Tolai words (ibid.).
- **What heavy users and native speakers did.** *baimbai* > *bai* > *ba*: a sentence adverb became a reduced, obligatory-looking future marker next to the verb (Sankoff & Laberge 1973, summarised in Smith & Siegel 2013). Native speakers "have little patience with ... a language with few grammatical categories, limited lexicon, virtually no morphophonemic reduction rules, insufficient redundancy, and little opportunity for stylistic manoeuvre" (Sankoff & Laberge, quoted in [Samarin 1993](https://utoronto.scholaris.ca/server/api/core/bitstreams/3bbd3c9c-c094-4147-9ffb-9b25f6d18d40/content)). But Samarin shows some of these changes came *before* native speakers, from people using it as their primary language (ibid.). Fast native speech reduces *-pela*, the pronouns and transitive *-im*, creating new homophones (Smith 2002, *Growing up with Tok Pisin* [secondary: reviews]); plural *-s* is "by now common in the speech of first language speakers" (Smith 2002, quoted in [Zimmermann 2010, p. 184](https://epub.uni-regensburg.de/19705/4/Diss_JZ_20-02-2011.pdf)), i.e. natives *added* a category the pidgin left optional.
- **Circumlocution versus borrowing.** "Circumlocutions thus are best regarded as a temporary stage in lexical expansion ... Circumlocutions involving five or more existing words are not felt to stand for single concepts and thus act as distractors" ([Mühlhäusler 1985, *Handbook of Tok Pisin*, pp. 603-4](https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/b72400ac-f17c-41f5-80db-710196c7b1a2/content)). Purist native coinages (*wok bilong kamapim diwai* "forestry") "fail to provide the degree of exactitude required"; fixing them as words would make the surface "drastically" more complex (p. 658). In the *Wantok* newspaper corpus (1972-2004), English loans displaced transparent phrases (*haus mani* "bank" gone, replaced by *bank*), circumlocutions became few, and loans created homonyms (*hat* = hard, hot, heart) (Zimmermann 2010, pp. 182-186). "English is borrowed simply because English has more prestige" (Romaine 1990, quoted ibid.).
- **Standardisation.** No academy; a dictionary (Mihalic 1971), a newspaper and the Bible did the standardising (Smith & Siegel 2013; Zimmermann 2010, p. 186).
- **For Apupiwe.** Three predictions. (1) Optional particles that are used constantly will reduce and become obligatory-looking; that is fine if they stay distinct (Apupiwe's drift rules aim at this). (2) Natives may add categories the design left optional (Tok Pisin plural). (3) Long phrases for frequent concepts will be replaced, and if a prestige language is at hand, by loans. Apupiwe's decision to give frequent modern concepts their own roots rather than phrases (design log, 2026-10-13, "No fusing; seed, then release") is the right response; its ban on loans except world-shared forms (L4) will be tested by exactly this pressure (roadmap: "Speakers' own borrowings").

#### ICAO aviation English (radiotelephony phraseology)

- **Design.** "ICAO standardized phraseology shall be used in all situations for which it has been specified. Only when standardized phraseology cannot serve an intended transmission, plain language shall be used" ([ICAO Annex 10, Vol. II, 7th ed. 2016, 5.1.1.1](https://www.bazl.admin.ch/dam/en/sd-web/S16bWUG7eTRQ/icao_annex_10_aeronauticaltelecommunicationsvolumeii-communicati.pdf)). Numbers digit by digit (5.2.1.4.1.1), read-back of numbers on request (5.2.1.4.2.1), fixed pronunciations (*TREE*, *FIFE*, *NIN-er*) (5.2.1.4.3.1).
- **Why phraseology isn't enough.** "Sometimes the unexpected happens ... In these cases, where phraseology provides no ready-made form for communication, pilots and controllers must resort to plain language", defined as "the spontaneous, creative and non-coded use of a given natural language" ([ICAO Doc 9835, 2nd ed. 2010, 3.3.13-3.3.14](https://skybrary.aero/sites/default/files/bookshelf/2497.pdf)). The proficiency requirements followed accidents in which "insufficient English language proficiency on the part of the flight crew or a controller had played a contributing role" (Doc 9835, 1.2.1). Doc 9835 calls phraseology "a true sub-language" (3.2.7).
- **How the spelling alphabet was made.** The 1952 ICAO alphabet came from explicit criteria: each word "a live word in each of the three working languages" (English, French, Spanish), "easily pronounced and recognized by airmen of all languages", good radio characteristics, similar spelling, and "free from any association with objectionable meanings"; it was then tested with speakers and trained listeners from six NATO nations at five signal-to-noise ratios, which led to the 1956 revision ([*The Evolution and Rationale of the ICAO Word-Spelling Alphabet*, USAF/DTIC AD227630, July 1959](https://www.governmentattic.org/4docs/ICAO-WordSpellingAlphabet_1959.pdf), pp. 10-12). The report argues that longer words survive noise: a noise "sufficient to drown out all of the word PIN" would leave enough of PROPAGANDA ("GANDA would be sufficient to identify the letter"), and that mixing one-, two- and three-syllable words lowers confusability (around p. 22 of the report).
- **Note on Apupiwe's fact-check C44.** The fact-check said no ICAO text supports "a two-syllable word survives a noise burst". This 1959 report on the ICAO alphabet makes exactly that argument for spelling words (not for digits). It is a USAF laboratory report, not ICAO's own rationale, but it partly rehabilitates the reasoning behind Apupiwe's two-syllable digits 6-9.
- **For Apupiwe.** Two lessons. First, the method: candidate words were filtered by criteria across languages *and then tested with listeners of several native languages in noise*. This is the experiment Apupiwe has deferred (design log 2026-10-13). Second, the scope: a designed sub-language works inside a closed domain and needs a full language around it for the unexpected. Apupiwe's speech-act particles, acknowledgement ladder and **yo yo** emergency marker are phraseology-like layers inside a general language, which is the right arrangement.

#### Simplified Technical English (ASD-STE100)

- **Design.** A controlled language "developed in the early Eighties (as AECMA Simplified English) to help the users of English-language maintenance documentation understand what they read"; a required standard in commercial aviation documentation since 1986; writing rules plus a dictionary of approved general words; "In general, there is only one word for one meaning, and one part of speech for one word"; users add their own "technical names and technical verbs" ([ASD-STE100, About](https://www.asd-ste100.org/about.html)). Issue 9, 15 January 2025; an international standard from 2025 ([ASD-STE100 home](https://www.asd-ste100.org/)). Word count of the approved list: about 900 **[unverified; not on the pages read]**.
- **Evidence.** A controlled study found better comprehension and task performance with STE than with ordinary English for complex maintenance procedures, for native and non-native readers (Shubert, Spyridakis, Holmback & Coney 1995, "The comprehensibility of Simplified English in procedures", *J. Technical Writing and Communication* 25(4):347-369 **[secondary: abstract summaries]**).
- **For Apupiwe.** STE's "one part of speech for one word" is the opposite of Apupiwe's class-free roots, adopted for readers who are not native; Apupiwe chose for natives (C2), which is defensible, but STE is evidence that class marking helps non-native readers. STE's core-plus-technical-terms structure is the surviving form of Basic English and matches Apupiwe's root base plus world-shared technical loans (L3, L4).

#### Modern Hebrew (a revived language with native speakers)

- Children acquired it first and taught their parents; by 1916-18, over 75% of the young population in the new settlements used Hebrew as their main language ([Izre'el 2003](https://www.tau.ac.il/~izreel/publications/Emergence_Hary2003(corr).pdf)). The founders' first languages set the phonology and much of the syntax; the Semitic morphology survived ([Zuckermann 2006](http://www.zuckermann.org/pdf/new-vision.pdf)); Ben-Yehuda's prescribed alveolar *r* lost to a uvular one **[secondary]** (all from `designed-languages-going-native.md` §2).
- **For Apupiwe.** The one case of a whole community going native in a planned standard: it took a territory, schools, a national project, and families with no other shared language. The sounds followed the founders. Apupiwe's drift-proofing protects distinctness rather than a fixed accent, which is the right target.

#### Indonesian (a chosen, not designed, national language)

- Chosen in 1928 as the national language from trade Malay rather than the majority language Javanese; today known by most Indonesians but the daily home language of a minority, about a fifth in the 2010 census ([BPS 2012, *Nationality, Ethnicity, Religion, and Daily Language of Indonesian Population*](https://www.bps.go.id/en/publication/2012/05/23/55eca38b7fe0830834605b35/nationality--ethnicity--religion--and-daily--language-of-indonesian-population.html) **[figure via search summary; table not read]**).
- **For Apupiwe.** A neutral, simple-grammar language succeeded as a second language when a state needed it; native acquisition followed slowly, in cities and mixed families. Design made it possible; politics made it happen.

## 2. Two essays close to Apupiwe's direction

### 2.1 Jörg Rhiemeier, "Designing an international auxiliary language" (2007)

Source: [joerg-rhiemeier.de/Conlang/auxlang-design.html](https://www.joerg-rhiemeier.de/Conlang/auxlang-design.html), last updated 2007-11-17. Rhiemeier is an artlanger who says he would not design an auxlang himself ("currently, it seems more likely that English will win out than even Esperanto").

**Where it agrees with Apupiwe.** Arbitrary sound-meaning links ("not a bug, it is a feature"; philosophical languages put horse, donkey and zebra one letter apart, "just asking for confusion"); against logical languages ("Language is language and logic is logic"); against closed vocabularies (Basic English "displaces the problem"); five vowels, a small consonant set, mostly CV syllables with only nasal or liquid codas; one letter per sound, no diacritics; no case, no agreement, no person marking; an isolating type; SVO with heads first throughout. On sounds, syllables, morphology and word order, Apupiwe is very close to the design Rhiemeier sketches.

**Where it disagrees.** Rhiemeier comes down for *a posteriori* vocabulary: scientific terminology, cultural words (*spaghetti*, *kimono*) and proper names should be taken as they are, and cultural neutrality "should not take the form of a language that is equally difficult for everyone". He would have no tense and no aspect system, and debates having a plural at all. Apupiwe's a priori roots are exactly the "equally difficult for everyone" option for adult learners; its defence is the native framing (C2, N2), which Rhiemeier doesn't consider.

**His ideas, checked against Apupiwe.**

| Idea in the essay | Has Apupiwe considered it? | Finding |

### 2.2 Vecderg, "Why there can't be a perfect auxlang" (5 March 2024), with its predecessor "The search for the perfect auxlang"

Source: [vecderg.com/articles/auxlangs2](https://www.vecderg.com/articles/auxlangs2); predecessor [vecderg.com/articles/auxlangs](https://www.vecderg.com/articles/auxlangs) (page dated 11 October 2025, probably a revision date). The author designs his own auxlang (Dasopya) and ranks Globasa best overall. The site is a Google Sites page; I read it through a fetch tool that summarises, so the quotations below are those the tool returned verbatim.

**Its argument.** Every design choice trades one good for another, so no auxlang can be perfect. Three vocabulary strategies: a priori (Solresol: "equally hard for everyone"), one family (Elefen: "me ama tu" is clear to Romance speakers only), and a world spread (Globasa: "Hin lala sen meli", hard for most). Simplified sounds make loans unrecognisable (Toki Pona *tawa* from "towards"). A simpler grammar makes sentences longer (Spanish *comí* against *tenpo pini la mi moku*); a tiny lexicon makes concepts vague (anger = *pilin utala*). "Around 1,000 words or more is the bare minimum for a functional auxlang." Auxlangs "aren't supposed to replace any current language"; "it's better for an auxlang to be an extra, simplified language that anyone can learn". The earlier article estimates how much vocabulary a learner must memorise: about 10% for a Romance-based language, about 50% for a worldlang, 100% for an a priori language. These are his estimates, not measurements.

## 3. Synthesis

### 3.1 Recurring design patterns, and what each cost or gained

| Pattern | Who | What it gained | What it cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Schematic regularity** (every word built by rule from a fixed set of endings and affixes) | Volapük, Esperanto, Ido, Novial | Predictable forms; Esperanto's regular endings survived a century of use and native acquisition with little change (Lindstedt 2010). | Volapük's roots were bent to fit the rules until nobody recognised them (Garvía 2015, p. 42). Ido's *logically* regular derivation (reversibility) was too hard for ordinary use, and users asked for one vague affix instead of three precise ones (Jacob 1947, VIII.3). |
| **Naturalism** (extract the vocabulary Europe shares) | Latino sine flexione, Occidental, Interlingua, Elefen | Instant reading for educated Western Europeans; real engineering in derivation (de Wahl's rule). | Neutrality, admitted openly (de Wahl, Gode, Boeree); the ease favours "people who are already familiar with two or three of the great European languages" (Jespersen 1928). Never measured. |
| **Algorithmic sourcing** (score candidate forms against weighted source languages) | Loglan, Lojban, Interlingua's prototypes, Globasa, Pandunia | Reproducible, documented, defensible choices. | Blends that are partly recognisable to many and fully to nobody (Loglan's computed 45%); "fair" weights still produced European-heavy outcomes (Globasa: English in 60.6% of etymologies, Mandarin last; Pandunia: English in 76%); hand-made exceptions were always needed (Lojban's cultural gismu). |
| **A priori roots** | Wilkins, Solresol, Ro, aUI, Kotava; Apupiwe | Freedom to optimise form; nobody privileged. | Every adult learns 100% of the roots. When the sounds encode meaning (Wilkins, Ro, Solresol), errors turn into plausible wrong meanings. Kotava shows arbitrary roots are sustainable for a small group for decades, but neutrality alone attracted no world audience. |
| **Isolating, class-free roots with particles** | Glosa, Toki Pona, Elefen, worldlangs, Apupiwe | No inflection to learn; the creole-like type. | Sapir's "masked complexity": which sense a bare root has in each role must be learnt word by word (Sapir 1925). Users rebuild distinctions: Esperanto grew verb-specialised roots (Jansen 2013); Globasa added two word classes and an infinitive particle after ambiguities in use; STE requires "one part of speech for one word" for non-native readers. |
| **Small closed vocabulary + composition** | Basic English, Toki Pona, early Tok Pisin | A small core to learn. | Phrases are vocabulary too ("Ogden and Richards cheated"); long circumlocutions for frequent concepts get replaced, usually by prestige loans (Mühlhäusler 1985; Zimmermann 2010). Toki Pona's users accepted vagueness instead. |
| **Formal unambiguity** | Lojban; partly Ido and Interlingua (homonyms excluded) | Unambiguous where a machine checks: segmentation, syntax. | Meaning stayed vague where speakers make it ("All tanru are ambiguous semantically"); logic-derived defaults were the first thing users replaced (xorlo); formal grammars split into dialect grammars. |
| **Obligatory precision** | Volapük's moods, Esperanto's accusative, Ithkuil | Expressive power on paper. | Users dropped or simplified it (Kerckhoffs cut Volapük's forms; Bergen's native children used the accusative in half its contexts, mostly by transfer); Ithkuil reportedly has no fluent speaker [secondary]. |
| **Optional precision** | Novial, Apupiwe | Cheap common case, precise form available. | Optional forms may go unused (Bergen: no aspect affixes) or get pressed into other jobs; no record yet of an optional precision layer surviving in use. |
| **Freeze and baseline** | Esperanto (1905), Lojban (1997) | Esperanto: a stable target that let the community diversify (Garvía 2015, pp. 157-158). | Esperanto locked in early flaws. Lojban froze before its dictionary existed and the freeze stopped the work it needed (2002 statement). |
| **Continuous reform** | Volapük under Schleyer, Ido, Pandunia, early Globasa | Fixes real flaws. | Learners must unlearn; movements split; "no point in ... teaching a language that did not yet exist" (Garvía 2015, p. 157). |

### 3.2 What decided success or failure

**What the record shows.**

1. **No language succeeded because its design was better, and none failed only because its design was worse.** Ido was the technically "improved" Esperanto and lost (Garvía 2015, pp. 136-142). Occidental lost its niche to a near-twin (Interlingua) and to the war. Toki Pona, the least precise, has the liveliest modern community. Garvía rejects ranking auxlangs by learnability: the "proof of the pudding" is whether a community keeps using them (p. 154).
2. **Governance decided the internal fate.** Owner control killed or split Volapük and Loglan. Expert academies with binding power (Volapük 1887-89, Ido) produced endless reform. A founder as sole authority ended with the founder (Novial, Neo, arguably Pandunia's instability). Giving the language away, freezing a small core and letting usage lead (Esperanto; Toki Pona's *ku*) is the only pattern that held.
3. **Community and meaning decided survival through crises.** Esperanto's "inner idea" held its core through the 1894 collapse; Volapük and Ido, deliberately ideology-free, had nothing to hold people (Garvía 2015, pp. 158-159). Toki Pona's community grew around a free course and Discord.
4. **Politics set the ceiling.** France blocked Esperanto at the League of Nations; Hitler and Stalin persecuted its speakers; English rose after 1945 (Garvía 2015, pp. 161-163; Fettes). The planned or chosen languages that went national (Hebrew, Indonesian) had a state or proto-state, schools and a population that needed a common language.

**What design can and can't affect.**

- **Design can affect:** how fast beginners progress (Esperanto beat French in a year for English-speaking children; Roehr-Brackin & Tellier 2018); how much natives need to change (Esperanto "already adjusted" by use; Lindstedt 2010); how much reform pressure builds, which is what governance then has to absorb (Volapük's quarrel was *about* its design); whether a sentence is ambiguous (Globasa's fixes); how well words survive noise (ICAO alphabet tests).
- **Design can't affect:** whether anyone adopts it, which is a coordination and politics problem; the prestige of English; wars; whether the founder keeps working; whether a community forms.
- **What this means for Apupiwe.** As a thought experiment for imagined native speakers, Apupiwe has stepped out of the part of the record that killed the others (adoption). Two parts still bind it. The first is what learners and native speakers do to designs, which is a design question. The second is governance, in the small form that applies to one author: change after people learn is costly, and a project that depends on one person's continuing attention ends with that attention. The record supports the project's own choice not to build adoption or governance machinery (roadmap, "Deliberately not built").

### 3.4 Lessons for Apupiwe

Each lesson names the language(s) that teach it and the goal item it serves. Lessons about adoption are left out on purpose: Apupiwe asks for no adopters, and building for them would be the moonbase the author's own rules warn against.

#### Adopt

| # | Lesson | Taught by | Goal item | What it means concretely |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | Give the language away, explicitly and completely. | Zamenhof's renunciation; Loglan's copyright fight and relexification; Lidepla's "all rights reserved" | B9 | Done: README and design log (2026-10-11) put text, data and code under CC0, which covers the word list Loglan fought over. Nothing to add. |
| A2 | Freeze a small core before others learn it; let use grow the rest; describe, don't legislate. | Esperanto (Fundamento + advisory committee + usage); Toki Pona (*ku*); Kotava ("a description ... at some point in time") | B9, N4 | When the freeze returns, freeze only the grammar words and the base roots, and say in advance what stays open (new words, idiom, style). |
| A3 | Test perception and comprehension with people outside the project, across native languages. | ICAO alphabet: criteria across three languages, then listeners from six nations at five noise levels (1952-56); Interslavic's cloze test (1,822 people) | B1, B2, C1 | The deferred listening test is the most important missing evidence. Interslavic shows a cheap form: a written cloze test run online. |
| A4 | Make derivation a small published algorithm. | de Wahl's rule; Interlingua's "specific technique" rather than "subjective decisions" | B7 | Apupiwe's ladders and markers already do this; publishing them as rules of the language (not only tools) is what lets a successor continue. |

#### Open risks

| # | Risk | Taught by | Goal item | Suggested check |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| R1 | **Native speakers will be bilingual, and their other language will reshape Apupiwe.** Five grammar-word pairs differ only in voicing (**bu/pu, bi/pi, ke/ge, pen/ben, din/tin**), a contrast many languages lack. | Esperanto's natives (always bilingual; changes by transfer); Hebrew's founder principle; Rhiemeier's functional-load rule | P3, B2 | Add an "L1 category merger" key (at least voicing) to the grammar-word checks, alongside the drift keys. |
| R2 | **Class-free roots hide complexity, and users may rebuild classes.** | Sapir 1925 ("masked complexity"); Esperanto's verbal roots (Jansen 2013); Globasa's two classes; STE's one part of speech per word | B7, B1 | Keep the add-back trigger (a dictionary hint for each root's usual role) and watch learner texts for it. |
| R3 | **Derivation markers will widen.** | Elefen (*-or*, *-eria*, *-ador*); Ido's single vague *-i-* proposal | B7 | Define **no ge du pu** loosely enough that widening doesn't break them; add them to the drift watch list. |
| R4 | **Frequent optional particles will reduce and harden.** | Tok Pisin *baimbai* > *ba*; Bergen's reduced function words | B9 | The drift rules handle distinctness; the watch list should also expect optional markers to become habitual. |
| R5 | **Loans and their adapted forms.** | Tok Pisin's loan pressure; vecderg on deformed loans (*tawa*) | L4, B1 | Apply the L4 panel test to the *adapted* form (is *intelunetu* recognised?), not only to the source word. |
| R6 | **Structure carried by pauses.** | Rhiemeier on suprasegmentals; Lojban's experimental particles as escape hatches | B4, B2 | Wherever a pause is the only cue (heavy subject before its verb, end of a relative clause), keep a segmental alternative like **kiyo**, and include such sentences in any listening test. |
| R7 | **Pace.** 194 commits in six days with the freeze lifted. | Pandunia's "play dough"; Volapük under Schleyer | B9 | Harmless while only the author learns; the freeze trigger in rationale §1.2 is the right control. |

## 4. The ten most important lessons

1. Give the language away and freeze a small core before anyone else learns it; every change after that costs learners (Volapük, Ido, Pandunia; the 1894 reform debate and vote halved Esperanto's readership).
2. Let usage lead and describe it: binding academies and logic-first rules were overruled by speakers every time (Ido, Lojban's *lo*, Esperanto's uncodified change, Toki Pona's *ku*).
3. A computed score is not a human result: test word distinctness with listeners of several native languages in noise, as ICAO did for its alphabet in 1952-56, and comprehension with a cloze test, as Interslavic did.
4. Native speakers of a designed language are bilingual children shaped by their other language, so no grammar-word contrast should rest on voicing (five pairs do now: **bu/pu, bi/pi, ke/ge, pen/ben, din/tin**).
5. "Fair" algorithms inherit their inputs (Globasa, Pandunia and Interlingua came out European), so Apupiwe must check its English-centred instruments, not only its sources.
6. Precision has to stay optional: obligatory logic gets replaced or dropped in use (Lojban's articles, Ido's affixes, Volapük's moods, Esperanto's accusative), and B3 is the right formula.
7. Class-free roots hide complexity (Sapir's "masked complexity") and users rebuild classes (Esperanto's verbal roots, Globasa's word classes), so keep the dictionary-hint add-back ready.
8. Narrow derivation markers widen in use (Elefen's *-or*, *-eria*, *-ador*; Ido's vague *-i-*), so define **no ge du pu** loosely and watch them.
9. Frequent concepts need short words of their own, because long phrases get replaced, usually by prestige loans (Tok Pisin, Basic English), and loans should pass the panel test in their *adapted* form.
10. Design never decided adoption (Garvía; the League of Nations; Toki Pona's free course), so as a thought experiment Apupiwe's measure of success is the evidence it collects, not the users it gets.
