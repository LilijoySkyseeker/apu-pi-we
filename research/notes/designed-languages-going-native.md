# Designed languages going native: what drifts

Evidence on what happens when planned, revived or newly formed languages get native or heavy users. Compiled 2026-10-08. Labels: **sourced** = read in the cited text or abstract; **secondary** = reported by a source I read, original not read; **UNVERIFIED** = from memory or not found.

## 1. Native Esperanto (denaskuloj)

- **Bergen 2001** (J. Child Lang. 28:575–595) studied 8 children aged 6–14. Lindstedt adds that they were recorded at a 1998 children's meeting and had six adstrates: Croatian, French, Hebrew, Russian, Slovak, Swiss German. Sourced: [Bergen PDF](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~bkbergen/papers/NEJCL.pdf). Bergen says native Esperanto:
  - "uses no compound tenses nor aspectual affixes" (*ek-*, *-iĝ-*, *re-*), though the L2 adults around the children used them;
  - "undergoes a good deal of phonological reduction, especially in grammatical suffixes and other unstressed syllables": pronouns, *la*, and one-syllable prepositions reduce to schwa or are dropped;
  - is "mostly SVO". The L2 speakers are SVO too, and the native/L2 difference was "not significant";
  - "uses the accusative in only approximately half of its 'appropriate' contexts". By child, the accusative was kept fully, used sporadically, kept only on pronouns, or lost except in idioms. The French child used it only in *saluton*;
  - has stress that "varies radically depending on the adstrate". Proper nouns resist the *-o* ending and Esperanto stress.
- **Lindstedt 2006** ("Native Esperanto as a test case for natural language", Festschrift Karlsson pp. 47–55) is a rebuttal. Sourced: [PDF](https://journal.fi/finjol/article/view/153130/98645).
  - His own three children had "practically no cases of missing accusatives" after the two-word stage, and they corrected non-natives' accusative errors. In another family's notes, a child used *-n* as a free morpheme.
  - The mother of Bergen's French subject said the child used Esperanto "reluctantly and seldom".
  - His daughter's double negation (*Tio ne estas nenio*) looked creole-like, but he traces it to Finnish.
  - His verdict: no convincing nativization changes. The differences come from "(1) transfers from the children's other native languages, (2) differences between the spoken and written registers… (3) incomplete acquisition." Esperanto had "already been adjusted" to natural-language constraints by decades of non-native use.
  - The 16 rules never specified word order. Usage fixed it as SVO with fairly free order.
  - He estimates about 1,000 native speakers, all bilingual or more. Non-native Esperanto also changes spontaneously (Schubert 1989; Philippe 1991).
- **Corsetti, Pinto & Tolomeo 2004** (LPLP 28:261–282) used parent diaries on five children aged 1–5. They overregularized even an already regular language. Secondary: search-result summary only; paper not read.
- **Versteegh 1993** (Linguistics 31:539–555) predicted little change in accusative use. Secondary, via Bergen.

## 2. Modern Hebrew

- **Who acquired first.** Izre'el, ["The Emergence of Spoken Israeli Hebrew"](https://www.tau.ac.il/~izreel/publications/Emergence_Hary2003(corr).pdf) (2003). Sourced:
  - By 1916–18, ">75% of the young population in the new settlements" used Hebrew as sole or primary language (citing Bachi 1955).
  - Acquisition came "first with the children and subsequently with their parents."
  - Teachers "did not speak a fluent vernacular". "The children became the teachers of their parents" (Bar-Adon 1975).
  - Bergsträsser (1928) called the result "a European language in transparent Hebrew clothing". In 1914 Lipschütz predicted that the parents' foreign syntax would pass to the children.
  - Izre'el treats the outcome as creole-like "creation of a new language". He records that creolists resisted this framing.
- **What survived and what didn't.** Zuckermann 2006, ["A new vision for Israeli Hebrew"](http://www.zuckermann.org/pdf/new-vision.pdf) (J. Modern Jewish Studies 5:57–71). Sourced:
  - "Phonetics, phonology and syntax are primarily European, its morphology and basic vocabulary are mainly… Semitic." Verbs keep their discontinuous (root-and-pattern) conjugations but are pronounced with Yiddish phonetics.
  - The **Founder Principle**: the founders' L1 (Yiddish) fixed the core grammar, and later Arabic-speaking immigrants had "relatively negligible" influence.
  - Native shifts the purists condemn:
    - analytic possession *sába shel-ì* replaced suffixed *sav-í*;
    - the construct state became rarer, and the article moved outward (*ha-òrekh dín*);
    - gender-polarity numerals were lost (*éser shékel*);
    - spirantization alternations were levelled (*bekitá*);
    - most speakers lack the pharyngeals.
  - Zuckermann's "hybrid" thesis is contested by Hebraists (recalled, not checked).
- **Phonology.** Ben-Yehuda recommended alveolar [r]. "Nearly all Israeli Jews" use a uvular. /ʕ/ merged with /ʔ/, and /ħ/ merged with /x/. Secondary: [Wikipedia, Modern Hebrew phonology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Hebrew_phonology).
- **Galilean dialect.** A children's variety in the Galilee kept the pharyngeals and a stop-only /b/, then declined. Source: Bar-Adon 1975, *The Rise and Decline of a Dialect*. Secondary, via search abstracts ([archive.org](https://archive.org/details/risedeclineofdia0000bara)).
- **Word order.** VSO became SVO. Pereltsvaig notes that colloquial Arabic made the same shift without European input. Secondary: [Languages of the World](https://www.languagesoftheworld.info/southwest-asia-and-north-africa/modern-hebrew-old-or-new-part-2.html).

## 3. Nicaraguan Sign Language and ABSL

- **Senghas & Coppola 2001** (Psych. Sci. 12:323–328): within two decades, "sequential cohorts of learners systematized the grammar". The spatial modulations "originate in children aged 10 and younger." Sourced: abstract (PMID 11476100, via Europe PMC).
- **Senghas, Kita & Özyürek 2004** (Science 305:1779–82): children "began analyzing complex events into basic elements, and sequencing these elements into hierarchically structured expressions". Later cohorts segmented manner and path, where gesturers had produced them simultaneously. Sourced: [WRAP abstract](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/66237/).
- **Kocab, Senghas & Snedeker 2016** (Cognition 156:147–163). Sourced: [PMC5027136](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5027136/).
  - Only the second and third cohorts could convey complex temporal structure.
  - Ordinal use went from 26% in cohort 1 to 70% and 71% in cohorts 2 and 3.
  - **No tense markers emerged in any cohort.**
- **ABSL** (Sandler et al. 2005, PNAS 102:2661–5): a consistent word order (SOV) emerged "in the space of one generation". It differs from the ambient languages. Sourced: abstract (PMID 15699343). Sandler et al. 2011 (NLLT 29:503–543) found a phonological level "has not yet emerged". Source: [search abstract](https://scispace.com/papers/the-gradual-emergence-of-phonological-form-in-a-new-language-368dctgl7u).

## 4. Creoles

- **Bickerton 1984** (BBS 7:173–188): creoles "are largely invented by children" from a bioprogram. Sourced: abstract via search. The anterior/irrealis/nonpunctual TMA ordering is a recalled detail.
  - Seuren's commentary in the same issue: Bickerton's TMA system "is, again, limited to the Caribbean Creoles. It does not occur in Hawaiian Creole." Sourced: [PDF](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2471494/component/file_2471493/content).
- **Roberts 1998** (Language 74:1–39): Hawaiian Creole's TMA system and *for*-complementation "developed late and primarily (though not entirely) within the population of native-born speakers… HCE did not form entirely within a single generation." Sourced: [Cambridge abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language/article/abs/role-of-diffusion-in-the-genesis-of-hawaiian-creole/8C3DBC82C1E992AA03226D2085C2772E). This conflicts with Seuren: Roberts treats the Hawaiian TMA as a native-born addition.
- **McWhorter 1998** (Language 74:788–818): the creole prototype is little or no inflection, little or no lexical or syntactic tone, and semantically transparent derivation. Sourced: search summary.
- **The other side.**
  - DeGraff 2004 (Language 80:834–9): Haitian Creole has French-derived affixes from its earliest records, so the "morphological bottleneck is empirically untenable". Sourced: [PDF](http://lingphil.mit.edu/papers/degraff/degraff-lang-80-04.pdf).
  - Blasi, Michaelis & Haspelmath 2017 (Nat. Hum. Behav. 1:723–9) compared 48 creoles with 111 non-creoles. The creole profile "stems from an over-representation of Western European and West African languages". The authors question "a pidgin stage… and creole-specific innovations". Sourced: abstract.
  - A 2020 reply, ["Not all grammatical features are robustly transmitted"](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-020-00611-x), was not readable (redirect wall).
- **Net.** What creoles lose is inflection and opaque morphology. What natives add is preverbal TMA and complementation, over about two generations. How much of that is "children's invention" and how much is substrate transfer is unresolved.

## 5. Iterated learning and communication

- **Kirby, Cornish & Smith 2008** (PNAS 105:10681–6). Sourced: [PMC2504810](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2504810/).
  - Design: 4 chains × 10 generations.
  - With learning alone, distinct words collapsed and "single strings are re-used to express more than one meaning". This underspecification made the languages easy to learn but useless.
  - When homonyms were filtered out (a stand-in for expressivity pressure), compositional morphemes emerged, plus a few frequent irregulars.
- **Kirby, Tamariz, Cornish & Smith 2015** (Cognition 141:87–102): structure comes from "a trade-off between pressures for compressibility (imposed during learning) and expressivity (imposed during communication)". Sourced: abstract.
- **Smith & Wonnacott 2010** (Cognition 116:444–9): chains, but not isolated learners, made unpredictable plural marking predictable "by lexicalising the choice of plural marker". Variation gets conditioned rather than deleted. Sourced: abstract.
- **Hudson Kam & Newport 2005** (LLD 1:151–195): with inconsistently used determiners, "children, but not adults, regularized". Sourced: search abstract.
- **Fedzechkina, Jaeger & Newport 2012** (PNAS 109:17897–902). Sourced: [PMC3497763](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3497763/).
  - The input language had optional case marking independent of animacy.
  - Learners shifted case marking onto *atypical* arguments, such as animate objects, recreating differential object marking.
- **Winters, Kirby & Smith 2015** (Lang. & Cogn. 7): languages became underspecified, holistic or systematic depending on which distinctions the context made necessary. Sourced: [search abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-and-cognition/article/abs/languages-adapt-to-their-contextual-niche/83E9F516875C340E0A9263B4A7C38F43).
- **Raviv, Meyer & Lev-Ari 2019** (Proc. R. Soc. B 286:20191262): larger groups built more systematic languages, faster and more consistently. Small groups "are more vulnerable to drift". Sourced: abstract.

## 6. Toki Pona and Lojban

- **Toki Pona.** Community usage overrode the design. Sources: Wikipedia, [Toki Pona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toki_Pona); community wiki [sona pona](https://sona.pona.la/wiki/number_of_words).
  - Words that the 2014 book (*pu*) dropped (*leko, monsuta, kipisi*) stayed in use. They were reinstated, along with *oko*, as *nimi ku suli* in the 2021 dictionary.
  - The 2021 dictionary was built from Discord surveys of "10–30 participants" each. Its methodology has been criticized.
  - The 2024 Linku survey has 118 core words (≥90% of speakers), 13 common, 10 uncommon and 40 obscure. Lang's minimalism held at roughly 120 core words, but the lexicon grew at the edges.
  - Speakers are young: 51% were under 20 in the 2022 census ([census results](https://tokiponacensus.github.io/results2022/), via search).
- **Lojban.** No reliable usage study found.
  - The community's CLL-update tracker says modern usage defaults to *lo* where CLL used *le* ([issue #22](https://github.com/int19h/cll/issues/22)).
  - The "xorlo" reform of descriptors is described as following "a natural shift in Lojban usage" ([Gleki](https://mw.lojban.org/papri/User:Gleki/xorlo_as_seen_by_La_Gleki)).
  - These are weak community sources. No native speakers are documented.

## 7. Standards, schooling, media

- **Icelandic.** Friðriksson 2008 (Gothenburg PhD, 108 informants) concludes that Icelandic "can still be characterized as a stable language". Sourced: search abstract.
  - Change still runs under purism. The "dative sickness" has been "for decades… a pet peeve" (Angantýsson & Friðriksson 2023, [Acta Didactica Norden](https://journals.uio.no/adnorden/article/download/9736/8882)).
  - The New Impersonal is "predominantly used by children and diminishes with increased parent education" (Maling & Sigurjónsdóttir 2002, JCGL 5:97–142; survey of 1,731 students; search abstract).
  - Schools *can* kill a sound change. In 1929, 42% of Reykjavík children had flámæli (a vowel merger). A school campaign ran 1940–60, backed by the national radio broadcaster (RÚV), and the merger was gone among children by 1960. Secondary: [Wikipedia, Flámæli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fl%C3%A1m%C3%A6li).
- **Media doesn't level dialects.** Labov finds the dialects of US cities "more different from each other than they were a hundred years ago". Source: [UVA Press, *Dialect Diversity in America*](https://www.upress.virginia.edu/title/4510/) and search summary of the *Atlas*.
  - TV is a weak accelerant, not a leveller. Engagement with *EastEnders* added to contact and local meaning in Glaswegian TH-fronting (Stuart-Smith et al. 2013, Language 89:501–36, [abstract](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/44403/)).
- **Lexical rates.** Frequency explains "approximately 50%" of the variation in replacement rates: frequent words change slowly (Pagel, Atkinson & Meade 2007, Nature 449:717–20). Small numerals are the most stable of all (Pagel & Meade 2018). Larger populations gain words faster, and smaller ones lose more (Bromham et al. 2015, PNAS 112:2097–102). All abstracts via Europe PMC.

## Implications for the design

**Survived nativization:**
- Core morphology that is *used every utterance*: Hebrew's Semitic verb templates and basic vocabulary.
- Esperanto's regular, agglutinative lexicon.
- A word order that L2 use had already fixed.
- Most of a small core lexicon (Toki Pona).
- Children add or keep discrete, segmented, sequential encoding (NSL, ABSL, Kirby 2008).

**Changed:**
1. **Phonology, almost always toward the founders' L1.** Hebrew's pharyngeals and alveolar r went. Esperanto stress followed the adstrate. Prescribed sounds are the least durable design layer.
2. **Unstressed grammatical words reduce** toward schwa or cliticize (Bergen).
3. **Redundant obligatory marking erodes or becomes conditional.** Examples: the Esperanto accusative when word order already does the work, Hebrew gender polarity and construct state, and Fedzechkina's optional case moving to atypical arguments.
4. **Rarely used compound or periphrastic tense/aspect forms disappear** (Bergen). Temporal devices are built over cohorts, and in NSL tense never appeared (Kocab).
5. **Synthetic forms become analytic** (Hebrew possession).
6. **Lexicon is set by usage, not by the designer** (Toki Pona; Ben-Yehuda's r).

**Lessons:**
- **Ship it pre-nativized.** Lindstedt's point is that L2 use had already naturalized Esperanto, so natives changed little. The conlang's choices already match creole outcomes (SVO, no inflection, no tone, preverbal aspect, optional tense). That is drift pre-empted. *Inferred.*
- **Pre-reduce grammar words.** Unstressed one-syllable particles will reduce anyway. Keep their vowels maximally distinct, or expect **yun/fu/gon/to** to merge in fast speech. *Inferred from Bergen and Hebrew.*
- **Audit every *obligatory* distinction for redundancy.** Obligatory marking that context or word order makes redundant gets dropped or made conditional. The obligatory **lu/tan** rule is the top candidate; natives will likely mark it only when there is real ambiguity (Winters; Fedzechkina). Consider writing it as "obligatory when ambiguous", the form learners would converge on anyway. *Inferred.*
- **Graded scales compress.** Expect **mon** + digit to settle on a few conventional values, as in Kirby 2008's underspecification under learning pressure. Test it with an iterated-learning pilot. *Inferred.*
- **Phonology is set by the founder population.** The Founder Principle and the Galilee case show it. Choose sounds the world's L1s already share, which the 15-consonant, 3-place inventory does. Expect stress to drift anyway.
- **Standards slow change selectively.** Schools eliminated flámæli, a salient, stigmatized merger. They have not stopped syntactic change (the New Impersonal), and media doesn't stop divergence. A written standard can freeze what people notice, not the grammar. Large, well-mixed populations drift less (Raviv), so a global language would lose little to random drift. Regional divergence, though, will happen (Labov). *Inferred.*
- **Frequent words are the stable core.** Spend design effort on the highest-frequency items. They are both the most stable and the most reduced.
