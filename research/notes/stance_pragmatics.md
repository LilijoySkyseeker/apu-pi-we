# Miscommunication of stance, confidence, source of knowledge, and intent (pragmatics) across languages

Scope note: notes are for requirements of a designed language aiming at faithful transmission of thought (candidate features: grammatical evidentiality, confidence marking). "Verified" means I read the primary text or an abstract/publisher page. "Secondary" means only a summary, a search snippet, or another paper's citation of it. Unless stated otherwise, samples are WEIRD (Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic), mostly English-speaking.

## 1. Verbal probability expressions: how much do numeric readings of "likely", "probably", "possible" vary?

### Takeaway
Hedging words carry a rough direction but spread widely when converted to numbers. Phrases in the middle of the scale ("serious/real possibility") span about 20–80% across readers. Readers also pull calibrated terms toward 50%: "very likely" (IPCC >90%) gets read as about 60–65%. That pattern holds in 24 countries and 17 languages. Putting the number next to the word is the one fix with consistent evidence. It roughly doubles agreement with the intended meaning.

### Cited Findings
- **Kent 1964 (primary, verified from CIA PDF text):** a 1951 National Intelligence Estimate said a Soviet attack on Yugoslavia "should be considered a serious possibility." Kent personally meant odds of about 65:35 in favour. A State Department official read it as "very considerably lower." When Kent polled the Board of National Estimates who had agreed the wording, "the low man was thinking of about 20 to 80, the high of 80 to 20." — [Kent, Words of Estimative Probability, Studies in Intelligence 1964 (CIA PDF)](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Words-of-Estimative-Probability.pdf)
- **Kent's proposed fix (verified):** a fixed scale. Certain 100%; almost certain 93% ±~6; probable 75% ±~12; chances about even 50% ±~10; probably not 30% ±~10; almost certainly not 7% ±~5; impossible 0%. He split analysts into "mathematicians", who wanted numbers, and "poets", who argued that phrases like "may well" and "distinctly possible" convey a "flavor" and that numbers would claim precision the evidence couldn't support. Kent called the poets' position "fundamentally defeatist" and said it was really about being able to "fudge it." — [Kent 1964](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Words-of-Estimative-Probability.pdf); table also at [Wikipedia: Words of estimative probability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Words_of_estimative_probability)
- **Barclay et al. (1970s) NATO study (secondary only):** 23 NATO officers converted sentences, which differed only in the probability phrase, into numbers. This is the source of the widely reproduced "perception of probability" spread charts. — [GlobalSecurity: Statements of Estimative Probability](https://www.globalsecurity.org/intell/ops/probability.htm); the officers' numeric spreads were not verified from a primary source.
- **Wallsten, Budescu, Rapoport, Zwick & Forsyth 1986:** a method paper. It showed that the vague meaning of a term ("doubtful", "probable", "likely") can be measured as a membership function over [0,1], and that meanings differ between individuals and with context. — [J Exp Psych: General 115(4):348–365 (PDF)](https://faculty.ucr.edu/~ramiz/papers/Vague_meanings_of_probability_terms.pdf) (I only verified the abstract-level description. I did not extract its N or effect sizes.)
- **Mauboussin & Mauboussin 2018, HBR (secondary, via a summary of the article):** about 1,700 online respondents, mostly English-speaking. The range covering the middle 90% of answers was 55–90% for "likely", 20–80% for "real possibility" (the authors call it "nearly meaningless") and 90–100% for "always". Women on average read phrases as indicating higher probabilities than men did. — [HBR 2018](https://hbr.org/2018/07/if-you-say-something-is-likely-how-likely-do-people-think-it-is); figures from [Jason Collins summary](https://corporate.jcx.au/words-of-estimative-probability). The HBR page itself was paywalled to my fetcher.
- **Budescu, Broomell & Por 2009 (Psychological Science 20(3):299–308):** participants read 13 sentences from the IPCC AR4 report and estimated what probability the authors meant. Readers took the statements as **less extreme than intended**, i.e. closer to 50%. They underestimated high probabilities and overestimated low ones. Consistency rose when each word came with its numerical range. — [Budescu et al. 2009 (PDF host)](https://moodle2.units.it/pluginfile.php/232175/mod_resource/content/1/Budescu,%20Broomell,%20Por%20(2009).pdf) (summary verified via search abstract. The PDF would not load, so I could not confirm N or the % consistent per condition.)
- **Budescu, Por, Broomell & Smithson 2014 (Nature Climate Change 4:508–512):** 25 samples, 24 countries, 17 languages, about 11,000 valid responses. Readers interpreted IPCC terms as closer to 50% than intended. Showing the verbal term together with its numeric range (the "dual format") brought interpretations closer to the guidelines and better separated the terms. The qualitative pattern was "remarkably stable across all samples and languages", and the dual format also made interpretations more similar across languages. — [Nature Climate Change 2014](https://www.nature.com/articles/nclimate2194); N from [ResearchGate summary](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/275118595_The_Interpretation_of_IPCC_Probabilistic_Statements_Around_the_World). The mean of **62%** for "very likely" (IPCC: >90%) comes from press coverage — [Mother Jones 2014](https://www.motherjones.com/environment/2014/04/ipcc-global-warming-doubt-psychology/) — and was not verified in the paper.
- **Harris, Corner, Xu & Du 2013 (Climatic Change), China vs UK:** Chinese participants' readings of the IPCC terms were more regressive (closer to the middle) and less differentiated than UK participants'. There is no standardized Chinese lexicon, and direct translations of the English phrases can mislead. — [Climatic Change 2013](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10584-013-0975-1) (verified via abstract-level summary only.)
- **Wintle, Fraser, Wills, Nicholson & Fidler 2019 (PLOS ONE):** N=924 members of the public, 53% Australian, the rest from the UK, Canada, NZ, US and elsewhere. Readers interpreted terms from the US intelligence standard ICD 203. Overlap between their interval and the ICD 203 interval was 32% with no guidelines, 66% with numbers in brackets in the text, 40% with a tooltip and 39% with a click-to-open table. Best estimates fell inside the guideline 59% of the time (control) and 82% (brackets). Only 46–47% of participants ever opened the tooltip or table. — [PLOS ONE 2019 (verified)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0213522)
- **Friedman, Baker, Mellers, Tetlock & Zeckhauser 2018 (International Studies Quarterly 62(2)):** in a large geopolitical forecasting tournament, rounding forecasts into coarser bins reduced accuracy. On average about 6 bins were needed to avoid losing accuracy, and about 11 for "superforecasters". This means coarse verbal scales (~5–7 words) throw away information that good forecasters actually have. — [secondary summary via search](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-organization/article/abs/behavioral-consequences-of-probabilistic-precision-experimental-evidence-from-national-security-professionals/9C7D7A3E5F960BA0DF3D8E70BC5DB0EF). The bin counts come from a search snippet, not the paper.
- **Related work found but not read:** a study of the Dutch-language press on variability in probability phrases ([JCOM 2020](https://jcom.sissa.it/article/pubid/JCOM_1902_2020_A03/)), a study on negated uncertainty terms affecting trust ([Climatic Change 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10584-020-02737-y)), and Bonnefon & Villejoubert 2006, "Tactful or doubtful?" ([Psych Science](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01776.x)). Bonnefon & Villejoubert show that "possible" said about a severe medical outcome is read as a *polite softening* of a higher probability. The same word gets a different number depending on what the listener thinks the speaker is trying to do socially. Title and topic verified; numbers not extracted.

### Inferences
- There are two separate failures. (a) *Dispersion*: listeners differ from each other, worst for mid-scale "possibility" words. (b) *Regression*: everyone pulls terms toward 50%, worst at the extremes. Defining the words more tightly fixes (a) better than (b). Showing the number fixes both.
- The 24-country result suggests regression toward 50% is not specific to any language. Translation adds its own error on top (China vs UK).
- A confidence-marking system with a handful of verbal levels would sit below the precision humans can actually use (6–11 bins). That points to number-like confidence markers, or at least many levels with fixed, taught values, rather than a few vague words. Bonnefon & Villejoubert also suggest confidence markers should be kept separate from politeness and softening, because listeners reinterpret a hedge as tact.

### Gaps
- I couldn't load the Budescu 2009 PDF or the 2014 Nature full text. Exact N and % consistent per condition are unverified, apart from about 11,000 responses in 2014.
- The NATO/Barclay numbers and the Mauboussin figures come from secondary sources only.
- I found no systematic cross-language lexicon study beyond the IPCC/Budescu programme and China vs UK, e.g. whether Japanese or Arabic hedges are read more regressively.

## 2. Grammatical evidentiality: prevalence, distinctions, cognitive and communicative effects, and strategic use

### Takeaway
Somewhere between a quarter and more than half of languages mark how the speaker knows something, depending on whether you count only systems built into the grammar (Aikhenvald) or broader coding (WALS). The evidence that this helps communication or cognition is weak and mixed. Children master evidentials late (about age 6–7 in Turkish). One study found adult Turkish speakers remember second-hand information *worse*. In real conversation, evidentials are used heavily to manage responsibility, authority and blame, not just to report a source neutrally.

### Cited Findings
- **WALS chapter 77 (de Haan), "Semantic distinctions of evidentiality" (verified):** 418 languages. 181 have no grammatical evidentials, 166 only indirect ones, 71 both direct and indirect, so about 57% have some. Evidentials are "almost completely absent in Africa". Languages with both direct and indirect evidentials cluster in the western US, the western Amazon, the Caucasus and the Himalayas. Languages of the Americas are "very likely to have at least indirect evidentials". De Haan concludes evidentiality is "more of an areal feature than a genetic feature". — [WALS 77](https://wals.info/chapter/77)
- **Aikhenvald 2004 (Evidentiality, OUP; secondary):** about a quarter of the world's languages have grammatical evidentiality. Her stricter definition counts only obligatory, grammaticalized systems, which explains why her figure is lower than WALS's. Tariana (Arawak, Brazil) has a five-term system. Aikhenvald also reports new evidential uses arising among Tariana speakers for information from radio, TV, phone and internet. — [Wikipedia: Evidentiality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evidentiality); [Aikhenvald, "Evidentiality and the ecology of language"](https://www.academia.edu/112663058/Evidentiality_and_the_ecology_of_language). Five-term systems are usually described as visual / non-visual sensory / inferred / assumed / reported (my recollection of Aikhenvald; **unverified here**).
- **Turkish acquisition, Aksu-Koç et al. 2009 (as cited by Kandemirci et al. 2023):** direct *-DI* appears in spontaneous speech at about 1.5–2 years, inferential *-mIş* a few months later. In experimental tasks, children produce *-DI* accurately at about 3–3.5 years and *-mIş* at about 4. — [Kandemirci et al. 2023, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10953015/)
- **Öztürk & Papafragou 2016 (Language Learning and Development 12:199–230):** Turkish children don't acquire the semantics and pragmatics of evidential morphology until about 6–7. 5-year-olds still couldn't reliably tell the two markers apart. Children understood *how they had come to know something* before they linked that understanding to the morphemes. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/251757382_The_Acquisition_of_Evidentiality_and_Source_Monitoring); [T&F abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/15475441.2015.1024834)
- **Papafragou, Li, Choi & Han 2007 (Cognition 103(2):253–299), Korean vs English 3–4-year-olds:** Korean children failed to comprehend the hearsay evidential, and showed **no advantage in source monitoring** over English-speaking children. — [as cited in Kandemirci et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10953015/)
- **Kandemirci, Theakston, Boeg Thomsen & Brandt 2023 (Child Development):** N=100 (50 Turkish children aged 42–59 months, 50 English aged 36–59 months). Turkish children's use of the direct evidential correlated with source monitoring (r=.34). Source monitoring predicted false-belief understanding only in Turkish children, and Turkish children did better on false belief overall. This partly echoes Lucas et al. 2013, where Turkish 3–4-year-olds beat English and Chinese peers on false belief. These are correlations, and the samples come from different countries. — [PMC (verified)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10953015/)
- **Tosun, Vaid & Geraci 2013 (Journal of Memory and Language 69(2):121–134), adult Turkish vs English:** English speakers remembered first-hand and non-first-hand sources equally well. Turkish speakers were *worse* at remembering and monitoring non-first-hand information than first-hand, and worse than English speakers on non-first-hand. The authors read this as obligatory marking privileging first-hand sources and discounting indirect ones. — [ResearchGate abstract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/260794783_Does_obligatory_linguistic_marking_of_source_of_evidence_affect_source_memory_A_TurkishEnglish_investigation) (N not extracted.)
- **Social and strategic use (Nuckolls & Michael, intro to a Pragmatics and Society special issue; verified from the full text):**
  - Early Western work blurred evidentiality with confidence ("epistemic modality") because speakers of languages without evidentials "found it difficult to understand evidentials as anything other than a proxy for epistemic modality"; the two are now held to be distinct.
  - Michael (2008): in Nanti (Arawakan, Peru) a major function of evidentials is "to subtly disclaim responsibility for mishaps and unfortunate events".
  - Gipper (2011): Yurakaré evidentials mainly express participants' intersubjective relationships to the topic.
  - Mushin: Garrwa (Aboriginal Australian) speakers prefer to claim authority from what they witnessed, but show "a marked disinclination to index source", and rights to reveal knowledge matter as much as having it.
  - Sidnell: in English conversation, asserting certainty also claims social asymmetry.
  - Proposed social explanations for why evidentials exist include avoiding direct confrontation in small interdependent societies (Fortescue 2003) and witchcraft and blame attribution (Aikhenvald 2004:358).
  - — [Nuckolls & Michael, Pragmatics & Society intro (PDF)](http://www.cabeceras.org/ldm_publications/l_michael_Pragmatics&Society_intro.pdf)
- **Turkish *-mIş* and Quechua reportative *-sh(i)* (secondary):** *-mIş* also expresses cognitive or emotional distance and surprise (mirativity). The Quechua reportative appears in folktales and riddles and is used for "casting doubt, framing gossip, distancing oneself, or indicating empathy". — [Wikipedia: Evidentiality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evidentiality); [Frontiers 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.1019359/full)

### Inferences
- Making the source obligatory doesn't automatically make transmission more faithful. Speakers learn these markers slowly, they absorb social meanings (blame avoidance, politeness, surprise), and in one adult study they *discounted* second-hand content in memory. A designed language should expect any evidential marker to be repurposed socially, as hedges are (see section 1).
- Language design can't settle whether an evidential is used honestly, because its social pay-off (deflecting blame) rewards misuse. The source of information and the speaker's confidence are separate dimensions in natural languages, and a design should keep them separate too.
- The cognitive-benefit evidence is mixed (Korean: null; Turkish adults: a selective deficit; Turkish children: correlations with false belief). It doesn't support claiming that evidentiality improves source memory.

### Gaps
- I didn't find quantitative studies on whether evidentials improve *listeners'* calibration or trust, i.e. whether hearers in evidential languages weight hearsay appropriately. A 2026 arXiv preprint on Turkish evidential trust ([arXiv 2604.24665](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.24665)) and a study on hearsay and speaker commitment using facial EMG ([Open Mind](https://direct.mit.edu/opmi/article/doi/10.1162/OPMI.a.382/138850/The-Effect-of-Hearsay-on-Speaker-Commitment-An)) turned up but were not read.
- Ünal & Papafragou's adult studies (including the Topics in Cognitive Science 2020 review) were blocked (403). Their findings are not summarized here.
- I didn't verify Aikhenvald's typology of system sizes (A1–D1) from the primary text.

## 3. Implicature and indirectness failures: scalar implicature, indirect requests, cross-cultural pragmatic failure, high/low context, sarcasm in text

### Takeaway
Even inside one language, listeners don't reliably draw the implied meaning. In Noveck's study, adults rejected "some X are Y" when all are about 59% of the time, and children almost never did. In text, sarcasm comes across only slightly better than chance (56%) while senders expect 78%. Across cultures, failures split into wrong linguistic form and wrong social norms (Thomas 1983). The popular high/low-context country rankings rest on weak evidence.

### Cited Findings
- **Scalar implicature, Noveck 2001 (secondary):** asked to judge "Some giraffes have long necks" (true, but says less than it could), 8–10-year-olds accepted it about 89% of the time, adults about 41%. So roughly 6 in 10 adults draw the "not all" reading, and 4 in 10 don't, at least in this kind of task. French-speaking participants. — [search summary of Noveck 2001](https://www.sfu.ca/~hedberg/earlysteps); context in [Guasti et al. 2005](https://boa.unimib.it/bitstream/10281/1132/2/Guastietal_2005uncorrected_proofs.pdf)
- **Kruger, Epley, Parker & Ng 2005 (Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 89(6):925–936):** senders predicted that receivers would correctly identify sarcastic vs serious messages 78% of the time. Receivers got 73% right with voice recordings but **56% with email**, where chance is 50%. Senders expected the same success whether they wrote or spoke. — [Kruger et al. (PDF)](https://web-docs.stern.nyu.edu/pa/kruger_email_ego.pdf); [APA Monitor](https://www.apa.org/monitor/feb06/egos.html) (numbers from the search summary. US undergraduates; N per study not extracted.)
- **Thomas 1983, "Cross-cultural pragmatic failure" (Applied Linguistics 4(2):91–112):** defines pragmatic failure as failing to understand what is meant by what is said. *Pragmalinguistic* failure means mistaken beliefs about the pragmatic force of a form, e.g. a literal translation of a conventional request. *Sociopragmatic* failure comes from different beliefs about rights, what may be mentioned, social distance and so on. — [OUP abstract](https://academic.oup.com/applij/article-abstract/4/2/91/167524)
- **The CCSARP project, Blum-Kulka, House & Kasper 1989:** a discourse completion test (16 items) eliciting requests and apologies from about 400 informants in 8 languages or varieties (Australian, American and British English; Canadian French; Danish; German; Hebrew; Russian). Requests were coded as direct, conventionally indirect ("Could you…") or hints. Conventionally indirect was the most preferred strategy in most languages. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/31338837_Requests_and_Apologies_A_Cross-Cultural_Study_of_Speech_Act_Realization_Patterns_CCSARP1) (the list of languages comes from a search summary, and the per-language % of direct requests wasn't found.)
- **Hall's high/low-context model, critique:** Kittler, Rygl & Mackinnon 2011 (International Journal of Cross Cultural Management 11(1):63–82) reviewed research using Hall's classification. Most country rankings rest on "less-than-adequate evidence", and the classification isn't built on rigorous, substantiated findings. — [Stirling repository](https://dspace.stir.ac.uk/handle/1893/3174?mode=full)
- **Hedges are reinterpreted socially:** see Bonnefon & Villejoubert 2006 in section 1. Listeners decide whether "possible" is a probability estimate or politeness, and change their numeric reading accordingly.

### Inferences
- Senders overestimate how clear their tone and intent are (78% expected vs 56% achieved in text). Natural-language users are miscalibrated about their own clarity, so a feature that marks intent explicitly (an irony or sarcasm marker, or a speech-act marker) targets a measured failure.
- Inside one language and culture, about 40% of adults don't draw the "some ≠ all" implicature. Any meaning carried only by implicature should be treated as unreliably transmitted even between native speakers.
- Treat high/low-context as a hypothesis about specific situations, not a property of countries.

### Gaps
- I didn't get primary numbers for CCSARP's per-language directness, or for indirect-request misunderstanding rates between native and non-native speakers.
- I found no large cross-cultural study of sarcasm detection in text across languages.

## 4. Speech-act ambiguity: questions vs statements vs requests

### Takeaway
About 18% of sampled languages tell yes/no questions from statements by intonation alone. When the voice is gone, as in writing or a flat delivery, that distinction disappears. Question particles are the most common strategy in the world's languages (about 61%).

### Cited Findings
- **WALS chapter 116 (Dryer), polar questions (verified):** 955 languages. Question particle 585; interrogative verb morphology 164; particle plus verb morphology 15; interrogative word order 13; absence of declarative morphemes 4; **intonation only 173**; no question/statement distinction 1. Intonation-only languages are "more common in South America, Africa, the Middle East, Australia and New Guinea" than in Europe, much of Asia and North America. Intonation isn't always a rise: Sesotho uses lowered pitch on the final syllable. — [WALS 116](https://wals.info/chapter/116)
- **Indirect requests:** the conventionally indirect form ("Can you…?") is grammatically a question but functions as a request, and it is the preferred request type in most CCSARP languages (see section 3). That makes question vs request a standing ambiguity, resolved by convention. — [CCSARP](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/31338837_Requests_and_Apologies_A_Cross-Cultural_Study_of_Speech_Act_Realization_Patterns_CCSARP1)

### Inferences
- A language that marks question vs statement vs request with a segment (particle or affix), rather than prosody alone, survives writing and noisy channels. 61% of languages already do this with particles. Conventional indirectness (questions used as requests) works against explicit marking unless the request marker is separate from the politeness marker.

### Gaps
- I found no quantified study of how often intonation-only questions are misheard as statements, in speech or in transcripts.

## 5. Emotion and attitude through prosody; politeness systems

### Takeaway
Voice carries emotion across cultures better than chance but far from reliably: 66% overall across nine countries. Accuracy falls with cultural and linguistic distance from the speakers (74% in Germany vs 52% in Indonesia for German actors), and joy is poorly recognized (42%). Text strips out most of this (see the sarcasm result in section 3).

### Cited Findings
- **Scherer, Banse & Wallbott 2001 (Journal of Cross-Cultural Psychology):** German professional actors portrayed anger, sadness, fear, joy and neutral speech using nonsense, language-free sentences. Listeners in nine countries in Europe, the US and Asia identified them with 66% accuracy overall, ranging from 74% in Germany to 52% in Indonesia. Joy was 42%, the other emotions about 70%. Confusion patterns were similar across countries, suggesting shared rules for reading vocal cues. — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Emotion-Inferences-from-Vocal-Expression-Correlate-Scherer-Banse/818cfcf6afabe5bd5f44c49686d0f20ba0b2c3d2); [ISCA 2000 version](https://www.isca-archive.org/icslp_2000/scherer00_icslp.pdf) (numbers from the search summary. Total N not extracted.)
- **Voice vs email:** listening to recordings gave 73% accuracy for sarcastic vs serious tone, email 56% (Kruger et al. 2005, section 3).
- **Politeness and evidentials:** the Japanese evidential and "territory of information" system (Kamio 1994) and "discernment" politeness (Ide 1989) are cited as cases where evidential marking is bound up with cultural ideology and politeness. — [Nuckolls & Michael](http://www.cabeceras.org/ldm_publications/l_michael_Pragmatics&Society_intro.pdf)

### Inferences
- Emotion and attitude carried only by prosody is lost in text and degrades across cultures. If the designed language must work in writing, attitude needs explicit segmental marking, or it should be accepted as a channel that isn't transmitted.
- Politeness systems repeatedly take over the same tools used for epistemic marking: hedges (section 1), evidentials (section 2) and indirect questions (section 4). Separating politeness from epistemic marking may be the central design problem.

### Gaps
- I didn't gather quantitative data on misreading politeness levels across cultures (e.g. honorific errors by L2 speakers) or on how well prosodic attitude (doubt, certainty) is recognized across languages, as opposed to emotion.

## 6. Sign languages: marking stance and questions with non-manual markers

### Takeaway
Across 35 sign languages, the face and head (non-manual markers) carry question type with notable cross-linguistic regularity. Raised brows usually mark yes/no questions and lowered brows mark wh-questions. This marking is more consistent across sign languages than question-word systems are.

### Cited Findings
- **Zeshan 2004, "Interrogative constructions in signed languages: crosslinguistic perspectives" (Language 80(1):7–39):** data from 35 geographically and genetically diverse sign languages. Question-word paradigms vary widely, but the non-manual marking of questions "exhibits more similarities (or less variability)" across sign languages. In many sign languages raised eyebrows mark polar questions and lowered eyebrows mark content questions. Non-manuals were less obligatory for content questions than for polar questions. — [Zeshan 2004 (MPI PDF)](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_58963_3/component/file_58964/Zeshan_2004_interrogative.pdf); [Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/53555) (via abstract and search summary.)
- **ASL:** yes/no questions use raised brows and a forward head tilt, wh-questions furrowed brows. — [Lifeprint](https://www.lifeprint.com/asl101/pages-layout/nonmanualmarkers.htm) (teaching resource, not research.) Raised vs lowered brows in ASL questions have been quantified in [a 2008 UT Arlington thesis](https://www.academia.edu/68371155/Questions_in_American_Sign_Language_A_quantitative_analysis_of_raised_and_lowered_eyebrows_The_University_of_Texas_at_Arlington_2008_) (not read).

### Inferences
- Sign languages show that a separate articulator running in parallel with the content (the face) can carry speech-act type and stance at the same time as the propositional content, and fairly uniformly across unrelated languages. That is a working precedent for a dedicated, simultaneous stance channel.

### Gaps
- I didn't verify sign-language marking of evidentiality or confidence (e.g. epistemic facial markers, "squint" for shared knowledge in some sign languages), and found no data on how often non-manual markers are misperceived or dropped, e.g. in video calls or by L2 signers.
