## Background

Apupiwe is a constructed language (repo /home/agent/work/repos/universal-language). Goal C3 (docs/goal.md, reworded 2026-10-15): "No form sounds like one of the strongest, most common offensive words in a major language (a short reviewed list). For other offensive matches, prefer the form that doesn't match when two forms cost about the same; that never overrides a cheaper or clearer form." The decision and its reasons are the last entry of docs/design-log.md; read it.

Sounds (docs/language.md §2): vowels a e i o u; 13 consonants p b t d k g m n f s l w y; syllables (C)V(n), n the only final consonant; p t k aspirated word-initially, b d g voiced. Grammar words are one syllable, most content roots two (e.g. **tisan**, **yawu**), loans longer. The current filter is tools/offensive.py with data/offensive.tsv: Wiktionary entries tagged vulgar/offensive/slur/derogatory or under taboo topics, in 34 languages (the LANGS list in offensive.py), each pronunciation mapped into our sounds (`segs`). Its weaknesses: Wiktionary tags don't grade strength or currency; the frequency check (wordfreq Zipf) measures the whole word, not its offensive use (Vietnamese *mẹ* "mother" counts as common); it matches 80 of the 140 one-syllable forms, mostly noise (initialisms *FA*, *FU*; spellings *buh*; neutral words like "they", *San*, Thai "Japan").

## What to do

1. **Sources on strength.** Find and use evidence on how offensive words are *today*, by language and region: published rating studies and norms (for example Ofcom's UK audience research on offensive language; Janschewitz 2008 taboo word norms for US English; similar norms for Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, German, Chinese and others where they exist; Dewaele's work on the perceived force of swearwords), broadcast or regulator guidance, dictionaries with register labels, slur databases, cross-linguistic swearing studies, and well-sourced usage notes. Say what each source covers (country, year, method). Where a language has no rating study, say so and use the best available evidence (dictionary register labels, usage notes, native-speaker resources), marking the confidence. Mark anything from your own memory as memory, not a source.
2. **The strongest words per language.** For each of the 34 languages, list the words that are both strongly offensive *and* common in everyday speech today: core curses, vulgar words for genitals, sex and excrement, and slurs. Usually 5–20 per language. Grade each: strongest / strong / medium / mild-or-dated, with the source. Note regional differences (US vs UK English, Spain vs Latin America, Brazil vs Portugal, Mandarin vs Cantonese) and words that have weakened (like *pee* in the US, which the author says is comedic now). Note the words' speaker populations.
3. **Which of our forms each strong word blocks.** For every word graded strongest or strong: which Apupiwe forms its own speakers would hear as that word. Use the word's real pronunciation, the language's own sound contrasts (does it separate p/b by voicing, by aspiration, or not at all word-initially? does it drop final consonants? how would its speakers hear our CV(n) syllables?), and `segs()` in tools/offensive.py as a starting point. Keep this narrow: forms a listener of that language would actually take for the word, not every near-key match. Cover one-syllable forms, two-syllable forms (CV(n)CV(n)) and anything longer the word maps to.
