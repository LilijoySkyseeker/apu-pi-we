# Names and loans: core–periphery phonology, adaptation, name marking

Scope: should adapted names get a looser "name phonology" (extra final consonants), or stay in the core (C)V(n) phonology with an echo vowel? What evidence bears on recognisability and on name/word collisions?

## 1. Core–periphery lexicons: do they exist, and are they stable?

- **Japanese.** Itô & Mester describe nested strata: Yamato (native) obeys all three voicing constraints studied, Sino-Japanese is exempt from some, and "the Foreign vocabulary is exempt from all three." [primary] [Itô & Mester 2009, Oxford Handbook of Japanese Linguistics ch. 4](https://people.ucsc.edu/~mester/papers/2009_ito_mester_lexical_classes.pdf). The strata are synchronic, not just history: some old loans (*karuta*, *kappa*) and even a recent one (*sukii* → *take-zukii*) now undergo native compound voicing, so they have "changed stratal membership". Nativization is "a gradual affair". [primary, same]
- **The periphery can grow.** Before about 1890, Japanese palatalised /ti, di/ in loans (*chiketto*, *rajio*). After 1890, /ti/ and /di/ "became allowable sequences in loanwords". [primary, abstract] [Crawford 2009, Cornell PhD](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/90cc8c5f-e4bd-4be9-9263-6dc4e217da7b/content). English /v/ became a phoneme partly through French loans with initial /v/ (*fine*/*vine*). [secondary] [Wikipedia: Middle English phonology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_English_phonology)
- **But syllable structure barely moved.** Itô & Mester: across 150 years of Japanese loans, "the syllable structure restrictions have not changed". Only the repair changed, from deletion (19th-c. *fai* "five") to epenthesis (*faibu*). [primary, Itô & Mester 2009]. Even Japan's official two-tier spelling standard has a looser Table 2, for writing foreign words and names "as close as possible to the original sound", and it adds only segments (*ye*, *wi*, *v-*), no final consonants. Proper names (people, companies, products) are exempt from the standard altogether. [primary] [Cabinet Notice 2, 1991, 外来語の表記](https://www.bunka.go.jp/kokugo_nihongo/sisaku/joho/joho/kijun/naikaku/gairai/pdf/94380801_03.pdf)
- **Finnish.** /b, g, f/ occur "only in relatively new loanwords" and are mostly pronounced *p, k, v/hv*. A clearly voiced *b* appears "typically only in educated public speech". New loans get "an *i* after a final consonant" (*bussi*), even though Finnish allows some final consonants. [secondary, reference handbook] [Korpela, Handbook of Finnish: letters](https://jkorpela.fi/finnish/Pronunciation_of_letters.html); [loanwords](https://jkorpela.fi/finnish/Native_words,_loanwords,.html)
- **Hawaiian.** Some loans were adapted. Others "motivated … a division in its lexicon between native, core words and peripheral, foreign ones" (Parker Jones 2018). *Kristo* "Christ" keeps its cluster but still ends in a vowel. [secondary] [Wikipedia: Hawaiian phonology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaiian_phonology); [Hieber 2025](https://linguisticdiscovery.com/posts/hawaiian-loanwords/). The missionary alphabet used extra letters for foreign and Biblical words. [secondary] [Wikipedia: Hawaiian alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaiian_alphabet)
- **Who keeps peripheral sounds:** bilinguals. Monolingual chiShona speakers nativize English loans, while bilinguals import /l/ and clusters (Kadenge & Mudzingwa 2012). The rate of importation tracks bilingualism (Haugen 1950). [secondary] [Smith, Loanword Phonology, Cambridge Handbook of Phonology 2e draft](https://users.castle.unc.edu/~jlsmith/home/pdf/smith2024_CHoP2_LoanwordPhonology_circulate.pdf)

**Summary (inferred):** peripheral *segments* can survive and even spread, mostly where speakers are bilingual or where many loans carry them. Peripheral *codas* are the most resisted part, even in languages with deliberately loose foreign tiers. Casual and monolingual speech nativizes toward the core.

## 2. Loanword adaptation: perception vs phonology; repairs

- **Perception account.** "All loanword adaptations are phonetically minimal transformations that apply during speech perception". Japanese listeners hear an illusory vowel inside clusters (Dupoux et al. 1999), so the epenthesis already happens in perception. [primary] [Peperkamp 2005, BLS 30:341](https://journals.linguisticsociety.org/proceedings/index.php/BLS/article/download/919/701/893)
- **Phonology account and mixed views.** Paradis & LaCharité's Preservation Principle says to keep input material unless that is too costly, so epenthesis should beat deletion. Kang's review finds that perception "underdetermines adaptation": Mainland Mandarin *Friedman* → *fu.li.te.man* but Taiwanese *fu.li.man*. Orthography and bilingualism also matter. [primary] [Kang 2011, Blackwell Companion to Phonology ch. 95](https://www.yoonjungkang.com/uploads/1/1/6/2/11625099/tbc_100.kang.pdf)
- **Clusters vs codas.** Word-initial clusters are repaired "predominantly" by epenthesis. For final codas, "it is not clear whether epenthesis is cross-linguistically the preferred strategy". Japanese, Korean and Sesotho insert a vowel. Vietnamese, Thai, Burmese and White Hmong delete, and all of the deleters "have a strong preference for monosyllabic morphemes". Hawaiian and Fijian mix the two (*east* → *isi*). [primary, Kang 2011]
- **Which epenthetic vowel.** Languages use a default vowel (Japanese *u*, Finnish *-i*), copy a neighbouring vowel, or take the vowel's quality from the consonant. Uffmann found all three "frequently found" in Shona, Sranan, Samoan and Kinyarwanda corpora. [abstract] [Uffmann 2006, Lingua 116](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0024384105001051). Even Japanese's default vowel is coloured by coarticulation in the cluster. [primary, abstract] [Guevara-Rukoz et al. 2017, JASA-EL](http://www.lscp.net/persons/peperkamp/Guevara-Rukoz_Lin_Morii_Minagawa_Dupoux_Peperkamp_(2017)_Which_epenthetic_vowel._Phonetic_categories_versus_acoustic_detail_in_perceptual_vowel_epenthesis.pdf)
- **Recognisability: epenthesis vs deletion.** Itô & Mester: deletion forms keep the source's syllable count, but "the phonemic (and orthographic) form of the source word is hard to recover". Epenthesis forms keep all consonants, so the source is "largely recoverable". [primary, scholarly judgement, not an experiment]. This fits Cutler et al. 2000: when listeners repair a nonword they change vowels faster and more accurately than consonants, because vowels "constrain lexical selection less tightly". [primary] [Memory & Cognition 28:746](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03198409.pdf)
- **Copy vs default vowel:** I found no recognisability study that compares them. **Gap.**

## 3. Proper-name marking

- **Personal articles exist in unrelated families:** Tagalog *si/ni/kay* (by case) [secondary] [tagaloglang](https://www.tagaloglang.com/si/); Māori *a* (*a Pita*) and Catalan *en/na*, *el/la* [secondary] [Wikipedia: Article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Article_(grammar)); Fijian proper article *o* [secondary] [Swarthmore LING073, after Dixon 1988](https://wikis.swarthmore.edu/ling073/Fijian/Grammar). The Catalan article grew out of Latin *dominus/domina* and lost its honorific meaning. [abstract] [OUP chapter](https://academic.oup.com/book/36485/chapter/321157672)
- **Names are phonologically special.** Names resist alternations through "name-specific faithfulness". They can have larger inventories, e.g. English [gwɛ] only in *Gwen*. [abstract/snippet] [Tanaka 2023, Lang. & Ling. Compass](https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/lnc3.12502) (page 403). Learners "are generally willing to accept idiosyncratic patterns … in proper nouns", but they extend only natural patterns to *new* names. [primary] [Tanaka 2023, Phonology, final draft](https://www2.ninjal.ac.jp/yutanaka/papers_cv/Tanaka2023_Learning_biases_in_proper_nouns.pdf)
- **Do listeners use name marking?** Two-year-olds read "This is Zav" (no article) as a proper name for familiar kinds of referent (Katz, Baker & Macnamara 1974; Hall 1991). [secondary, snippet] [Hall 1991, Child Dev.](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-8624.1991.tb01595.x). A name that is also a word stays hard to learn *as a name*: Baker the surname is recalled worse than baker the job. [abstract/secondary] [McWeeny et al. 1987, BJP 78:143](https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.2044-8295.1987.tb02235.x). I found no study of whether an obligatory name marker speeds adult comprehension. **Gap.**

## 4. Recognisability of heavily adapted names

- Direct evidence is thin. A pilot study found that Japanese learners could spell and translate English words that have Japanese loanword counterparts at "more than twice the rate" of words without them, so knowing the adapted form helps people recover the source. [primary] [Delve & Broderick 2022, JALT](https://jalt-publications.org/sites/default/files/pdf-article/jalt2021-pcp-022.pdf). I found no experiment on English speakers recovering *Kalikimaka*-type forms by ear. **Gap; UNVERIFIED** that they mostly can't.

## Implications for the design

1. **The motivating collision isn't a coda problem.** Mara → *mala* collides because of r → l. It has no final consonant, so a name phonology with extra codas would not fix it. Collisions are structural: the lexicon fills 210 of 4,550 possible (C)VCV shapes (4.6%), so about **46 of every 1,000** disyllabic names would land on a word [computed]. Only marking catches all of them.
2. **Name marking: recommend a personal article** (one CV grammar word before every name, like *si*/*a*/*o*). It has precedent in Austronesian and Romance, costs one syllable, and children use article cues to spot names. It also makes a collision harmless in the way English copes with *Rose*, *Bill* and *Baker* [inferred].
3. **Name codas: propose not adding them** [inferred from §1]. Even Japan's loosest tier never admitted codas. Finnish adds *-i* to new loans and still nativizes loan segments in casual speech. Importation depends on bilingualism, which most native speakers won't have. The likely result is doublets (a coda form beside an echo-vowel form) plus leakage into the core through brand names that become ordinary words. *Add-back trigger:* tests show marked names are still often confused with each other or with words, in ways a final consonant would fix.
4. **Epenthetic vowel: keep echo/copy, or switch to a fixed default; neither is shown better.** Copy is the most common pattern in Uffmann's corpora. A default (Finnish *-i*) is predictable, so listeners can strip it. Collision risk is the same either way: the lexicon's final vowels and same-vowel CVCV shapes are both at chance, about 20% each [computed]. Whichever you choose, **insert a vowel; never delete a consonant**, because consonants carry word identity (Cutler 2000; Itô & Mester 2009).
