# Fixed stress and sound change: what initial stress buys, and how this sound system would drift

Scope: the case for and against fixed initial stress, and the drift to expect when children speak a p b t d k g m n f v s z l w y / a e i o u, (C)V(n) system natively for generations.

Legend. **[primary]** means I read the number in the source text. **[abstract/secondary]** means it comes from an abstract, a review or a search snippet. **[computed]** means I computed it from a dataset or from this repo. **[UNVERIFIED]** means it is from memory or a weak source; treat it as a lead, not a fact.

## 1. Demarcative stress and segmentation

**Typology.** WALS 14A (Goedemans & van der Hulst) covers 502 languages. 220 have no fixed stress, 110 are penultimate, 92 initial, 51 final, 16 second-syllable, 12 antepenultimate and 1 third-syllable. "The most frequent fixed pattern, then, is penultimate, followed by initial." European weight-insensitive systems are mostly initial, and Austronesian systems are mostly penultimate. [primary] [WALS ch. 14](https://wals.info/chapter/14)
- For a lexicon that is about 90% two-syllable roots (177 CVCV + 49 CVCVn + 43 CVCCV + ... of 297) [computed], initial and penultimate stress fall on the same syllable. The choice matters only for roots of three or more syllables and for clipped compounds.

**English (the metrical segmentation strategy).** About 90% of lexical words in 190,000 words of British conversation begin with a strong (full-vowel) syllable. Dictionaries have about 3 strong-initial words for every weak-initial one. [abstract] [Cutler & Carter 1987](https://www.mpi.nl/publications/item68762/predominance-strong-initial-syllables-english-vocabulary). Listeners treat strong syllables as likely word onsets (Cutler & Norris 1988, JEP:HPP 14:113). [secondary] [PDF](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_68765_5/component/file_68766/Cutler_1988_The+role+of+strong.pdf)

**Finnish (fixed initial stress).** Vroomen, Tuomainen & de Gelder (1998, JML 38:133–149) used word spotting: listeners hear a nonsense string and press a button when it contains a real word. When the pitch accent fell on the prefix (misleading), RTs to high-frequency targets were about 807 ms. When it fell on the target's first syllable (correct), they were about 277 ms, and vowel-harmony cues stopped mattering. These figures come from the replication report. In that replication, the RT was predicted by a *local F0 change* at the stressed syllable, not by the F0 difference between neighbouring syllables. [primary for the replication; secondary for Vroomen's numbers] [Tuomainen, Vroomen & de Gelder 1998, ICSLP](https://www.isca-archive.org/icslp_1998/tuomainen98_icslp.pdf). Vowel harmony helps segmentation when stress gives no cue. [abstract] [Suomi, McQueen & Cutler 1997](https://www.mpi.nl/publications/item68852/vowel-harmony-and-speech-segmentation-finnish)

**Hungarian.** Pseudowords with the native first-syllable stress elicited no mismatch negativity (MMN, a brain response to unexpected sounds), while second-syllable stress elicited two. Hungarian listeners therefore hold a stored template of "stress = word start". [abstract] [Honbolygó et al. 2020, Psychophysiology](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/psyp.13541)

**"Stress deafness".** Speakers of fixed-stress languages stop encoding stress in words. Finnish, French and Hungarian listeners made significantly more recall errors on stress contrasts than on phoneme contrasts (Hungarian F(1,9)=37.4, p<.0001). Polish listeners did not, at least not reliably (p<.056). Peperkamp & Dupoux explain this with how infants learn stress. Finnish infants can read the rule off utterance onsets. Hungarian infants must first strip off unstressed utterance-initial function words (*az émberek*), and they still succeed. Polish penultimate stress needs word boundaries before it can be learned, so Polish listeners keep stress in their representations. [primary] [Peperkamp & Dupoux 2002, LabPhon 7](http://www.lscp.net/persons/peperkamp/Peperkamp_Dupoux_(2002)_A_typological_study_of_stress_'deafness'.pdf)
- This design is the Hungarian case: unstressed CV(n) grammar words precede stressed content words. The evidence says children learn it.

**How much does it help? Less than the Finnish RTs suggest.** Listeners weight cues hierarchically: lexical first, then segmental, then metrical/prosodic. Stress drives segmentation mainly when context is missing or the signal is noisy. [abstract] [Mattys, White & Melhorn 2005, JEP:General 134:477](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Integration-of-multiple-speech-segmentation-cues:-a-Mattys-White/7a3707658ae2d742e5df9097ad4803a9704a5c2c). In artificial-language learning, English, French and Dutch listeners all benefited from vowel *lengthening* at the right edge of words, and none benefited from it at the left edge. Only the Dutch listeners used a pitch cue in an uncharacteristic position. [abstract] [Tyler & Cutler 2009, JASA 126:367](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/26672440_Cross-language_differences_in_cue_use_for_speech_segmentation)
- Inference: initial stress pays off in noise, which is the design's target condition. It should be cued by a pitch movement at the onset, not by lengthening, because lengthening is read universally as a right-edge cue.

## 2. Costs: initial stress and erosion of what follows

**The pattern.** Strong initial stress is followed, over centuries, by reduction or loss of non-initial vowels in many lineages:
- Proto-Germanic fixed stress on the root, after which "nonaccented syllables became subject to reduction and even total loss" (*kuningaz* → German *König*, English *king*). [secondary] [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Germanic-languages/Vowels)
- Old Irish shows apocope (loss of final syllables) and rhythmic syncope (loss of every second post-tonic vowel). [secondary] [Wikipedia summary of Stifter, McCone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonological_history_of_Old_Irish)
- Latvian took first-syllable stress (attributed to Finnic contact), shortened final long vowels and dropped final short ones (Latvian *sak-a* = Lithuanian *sãk-o*). [secondary] [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Baltic-languages/Comparison-of-Lithuanian-and-Latvian)
- Southern Finnic (Estonian, Livonian) shows apocope, syncope and shortening of non-initial long vowels, linked to the "rise in the prominence of word-initial lexical stress". Livonian loses all vowels from the third syllable on. [secondary] [Pajusalu, SUST 266](https://www.sgr.fi/sust/sust266/sust266_pajusalu.pdf)
- **Hungarian is not a counterexample.** It apocopated final high vowels by the end of the 13th century. [secondary] [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonological_history_of_Hungarian)

**Why Finnish (and largely Czech) kept their vowels.** The mechanism is duration. Barnes (2006) argues that vowel neutralisation starts as gradient shortening and then becomes categorical: once phonologised, "no amount of additional phonetic duration will avert this merger." Absolute word-initial position resists reduction because it is longer. [primary review] [Nevins 2007, review of Barnes, Phonology 24:461](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037c-5179-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content)
- Languages with contrastive vowel length avoid duration as a stress cue (Hayes 1995, via Peperkamp & Dupoux above) [secondary]. Finnish stress is cued by modest durational enhancement within the first two morae [abstract] ([Suomi & Ylitalo 2004, J. Phon. 32:35](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0095447003000056)), while non-initial syllables keep full quantity contrasts.
- Inference: the brake was contrastive length in every syllable, not initial stress as such. Estonian had length too and still eroded, so the brake is not a guarantee. **This design has no length contrast, so it lacks the strongest known brake.**

**Is "no vowel reduction" stable?** It can be, but it is not guaranteed:
- Spanish has kept five full vowels in unstressed syllables for centuries [UNVERIFIED as a dated claim; standard description].
- European and Brazilian Portuguese started from one system and diverged. EP now has vowel reduction and deletion and mixed stress/syllable-timed metrics, while BP is syllable-timed with epenthesis. [secondary, via a 2024 Frontiers paper citing it] [Frota & Vigário 2001, Probus 13:247](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2024.1415854/full). Placing EP's reduction in the 18th century is [UNVERIFIED].
- Even Japanese, which is mora-timed with no stress, devoices /i u/ between voiceless consonants [UNVERIFIED as a citation; standard].
- Rhythm-class metrics (%V and ΔC from [Ramus, Nespor & Mehler 1999](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S001002779900058X)) reflect syllable complexity and the materials used, so "syllable-timed" is a tendency, not a switch [abstract] ([Arvaniti 2012, J. Phon. 40:351](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-usefulness-of-metrics-in-the-quantification-of-Arvaniti/586ebca1f8e91ed5f12e362c082bea3af11039a3)).

## 3. Alternatives, and how focus gets marked

- **Seoul Korean** has no lexical stress or accent. Prominence sits at the *edges* of accentual phrases (AP). The AP pattern is LHLH, or HHLH when the AP starts with an aspirated or tense consonant. That gives a phrase-level, not word-level, boundary cue. [primary] [Jun, K-ToBI](https://sunahjun.humspace.ucla.edu/ktobi/K-tobi.html). Focus starts a new phrase and the material after it is dephrased. [secondary]
- **Post-focus compression (PFC)**, the shrinking of pitch range and loudness after the focused word, is reported in Japanese, Korean and French (none with lexical stress), in Finnish, Turkish, Greek, Dutch, German, Hindi, Persian and Arabic. It is absent in Cantonese, Taiwanese, Yucatec, Hausa, Zulu, Wolof and others. Focus is perceived better when PFC can apply. *Sentence-final focus is often confused with no focus.* PFC has been observed to be **lost through contact** (Taiwan Mandarin, L2 learners) and **never gained**. [primary] [Xu 2011, ICPhS](https://www.internationalphoneticassociation.org/icphs-proceedings/ICPhS2011/OnlineProceedings/SpecialSession/Session6/Xu/Xu.pdf)
- **Fixed-stress languages lean on syntax for focus:**
  - Hungarian has an obligatory immediately-preverbal slot for identificational (exhaustive) focus. [secondary] [É. Kiss 1998, Language 74:245](http://www.lel.ed.ac.uk/~ronnie/JoS-paper_forESRC.pdf)
  - Finnish uses word order plus an expanded pitch range on the focus and compressed range around it. [abstract; author name from memory] [Arnhold 2016, J. Phon.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0095447016000127)
  - French uses clefts (*c'est X qui*). [UNVERIFIED citation; standard]

## 4. Positional asymmetries: what survives and what erodes

- Word beginnings drive recognition. Initial fragments led to correct retrieval with probability 0.89, final fragments 0.61. [abstract] [Nooteboom 1981, J. Phon. 9:407](https://research.tue.nl/en/publications/lexical-retrieval-from-fragments-of-spoken-words-beginnings-vs-en/). In the cohort model, the onset activates candidates in parallel. [Marslen-Wilson 1987, Cognition 25:71](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3581730)
- Across hundreds of languages, words front-load information. [abstract] [Pimentel, Cotterell & Roark 2021, EACL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.02183)
- Root-initial syllables, stressed syllables and onsets keep contrasts that other positions neutralise; this is "positional faithfulness". [abstract] [Beckman 1998](https://roa.rutgers.edu/files/234-1297/234-1297-BECKMAN-0-0.PDF). Barnes credits this to initial-strengthening duration. Final syllables are mixed: open phrase-final syllables are protected by lengthening, while final codas and devoiced vowels are weak. [primary review, Nevins 2007 above]
- Inference: the order of erosion is unstressed function words, then non-initial vowels, then codas, then medial (intervocalic) consonants. Initial CV is last.

**Velar palatalisation** (k/ɡ before i/e becoming tʃ/dʒ). Velars before front vowels sound like palato-alveolars, and [ki] is misheard as [tsi] while [ka] is not. [abstract] [Guion 1998, Phonetica 55:18](https://doi.org/10.1159/000028423). Implicational universal: if low front vowels trigger palatalisation, high front vowels do too. **Nasal codas.** VN → Ṽ goes low > mid > high: low vowels nasalise first. [secondary] [Hajek 1997](https://books.google.com/books/about/Universals_of_Sound_Change_in_Nasalizati.html?id=bXJg7oY6sUsC). **Concrete histories of the same kind:**
- Spanish devoiced all its voiced sibilants (z, dz, ʒ) in the 16th century. [secondary] [Moore, UCSD notes](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~moore/strucspan/sibilants.pdf)
- Latin s → z → r (rhotacism). [UNVERIFIED citation; standard]
- Japanese merged zi/di and zu/du (the *yotsugana*), shifted initial *p → ɸ → h, and turned intervocalic p into w and then ∅. [secondary] [Yotsugana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yotsugana); Frellesvig, *A History of the Japanese Language*

**Functional load.** Across languages, phoneme pairs that merged distinguished significantly fewer minimal pairs than pairs that did not merge. [abstract] [Wedel, Kaplan & Jackson 2013, Cognition 128:179](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010027713000541). Segments lenite where their informativity is low (Indonesian /k/, English /t/, Spanish /s/). [primary, accepted manuscript; venue not checked] [Cohen Priva 2017](https://urielcpublic.s3.amazonaws.com/Informativity-and-the-actuation-of-lenition-accepted.pdf)

**Function words.** Reductive sound change reaches high-frequency words first, while analogy reaches low-frequency words first. [abstract] [Bybee 2002, LVC 14:261](https://www.unm.edu/~jbybee/downloads/Bybee2002WordFreq.pdf). The negator cycle (Jespersen's cycle: the negator wears down, gets reinforced, and the reinforcement replaces it) is the classic case [UNVERIFIED citation; standard].

## 6. What stays stable

- Pagel, Atkinson & Meade (2007, Nature 449:717) studied 200 meanings in 87 Indo-European languages. Frequency of use explains about 50% of the variance in replacement rate. Half-lives run from 750 years to over 10,000. From fastest- to slowest-changing: prepositions and conjunctions, then adjectives, verbs, nouns, special adverbs, pronouns and numbers. [primary] [PDF](https://cognitionandculture.net/wp-content/uploads/FrequencyofwordusepredictsratesoflexicalevolutionthroughoutIndo-Europeanhistory.pdf)
  - Caveat: this measures which word is used, not its sound. Frequent words keep their identity but erode phonetically fastest (Bybee).
- Structurally, consonant and vowel inventories are among the most stable WALS features. "Phonology cover[s] the whole spectrum." [primary page] [Dediu & Cysouw 2013, PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0055009)

## Implications for the design

**Should stress be fixed, and where?** Yes, on the first syllable. Devil's-advocate verdict: no alternative clearly beats it.
- For two-syllable roots it is identical to penultimate stress, the world's most common pattern.
- It stacks prominence on the position that perception (cohort, Nooteboom) and diachrony (positional faithfulness) already protect.
- Children acquire the Hungarian-type version, with unstressed function words in front.
- Its segmentation payoff is largest in noise, which is the design's target condition.
- The serious case against it is erosion (§2). Phrase-edge prosody of the Korean or French kind would avoid erosion, but it gives up the word-level boundary cue the rule exists for.

**Proposed refinements** (all are Claude's additions, sourced to the evidence above, and need the owner's sign-off):
1. **Specify stress as a pitch movement at the stressed syllable's onset, with no lengthening of it and no shortening of the others.** Source: Tuomainen et al. (local F0 change drives segmentation), Tyler & Cutler (lengthening is read as a right-edge cue), and Barnes (shortening is the seed of reduction). Cost: pitch is not fully free, but Finnish and Hungarian combine it with intonation. Add-back trigger for durational stress: listening tests show the onset cue is too weak in noise.
2. **Keep the lexicon "erosion-safe" as a standing rule:**
   - no minimal pairs that differ only in a non-initial vowel;
   - no minimal pairs in which voicing of a medial (second-syllable) consonant is the only difference.

   The current lexicon passes the vowel test except for 3 pairs. It fails the voicing test on 4 pairs (nite/nide, itu/idu, duta/duda, yako/yago). Reforming those 7 words would cost little.
3. **Expected drift, ranked by likelihood × damage:**
   1. Grammar words cliticise onto the next word, vowel-only ones (e, u, in, on) first. Pronouns may fuse into verb prefixes. NOT (na) wears down and gets reinforced; the existing doubling rule (na na) is the natural reinforcer.
   2. Non-initial vowels centralise; e/i and o/u merge first (e → i is the commonest vowel change counted).
   3. Final -n becomes a nasal vowel or a placeless nasal, starting after /a/. Harmless now, with 0 collisions.
   4. Medial p t k voice, and b d g spirantise; b → β would collide with v.
   5. k/g palatalise before i/e. That stays an allophone because the inventory has no /tʃ/.
   6. z devoices, or v and w merge. Low impact in content words, but each would kill grammar-word pairs.
4. **Pre-empt by stating tolerances, not by forbidding change.**
   - Declare [tʃ] for k before i, nasal vowels for Vn, and [β] for intervocalic v as acceptable variants. Then the drift that does not merge anything (92% of lenitions, per Gurevich) is not "change".
   - Protect only what carries minimal pairs.
   - **Deletion candidate:** /z/. It is the rarest consonant here (29.7% of languages), is 98% of the time accompanied by /s/, and carries only the 2 grammar-word pairs. Add-back trigger: the grammar-syllable space runs out, which the design log says is close (4 free).
5. **Focus.** "Nothing moves" rules out the Hungarian and Finnish solution. PFC is fragile and fails sentence-finally. Add no focus particle yet. Test it instead: the next back-translation text should contain contrastive focus on a final object. Add a particle only if focus is lost there. **bin** ONLY already covers exhaustive focus.
