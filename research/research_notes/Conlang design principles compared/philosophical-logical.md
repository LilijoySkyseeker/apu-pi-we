# Philosophical, a priori and logical constructed languages: design principles and what happened in use

Scope: Wilkins (1668) and Dalgarno (1661); Solresol; Ro; aUI; Blissymbolics; Loglan/Lojban. Compiled 2026-10-09. Wilkins examples were checked against an OCR scan of the 1668 *Essay* on archive.org (cited as "Wilkins 1668 scan"). Where a claim comes only from a search-engine summary or Wikipedia, the note says so.

## Q1. Taxonomic languages (Wilkins, Dalgarno, Solresol, Ro, aUI): how meaning was encoded, documented problems, recorded use

### Takeaway
Each of these languages encoded a classification of concepts directly in the letters or sounds, so related meanings got near-identical forms. Commentators from the 17th century to now name the same problem: words for neighbouring concepts differ by one letter, so they are hard to remember and easy to confuse. None of them gained a working speech community. Wilkins's character was used on paper by a small circle (cipher-like letters, one translated text) and then dropped.

### Cited Findings

**Wilkins, *An Essay towards a Real Character, and a Philosophical Language* (1668): encoding (checked against the primary text)**
- Wilkins says all notions "are reduced to forty Genus's. The first six of which do comprehend such matters as by reason of their Generalness… are above all those common heads of things called Predicaments… Substance doth take in fourteen Genus, Quantity three, Quality five, Action four…" — [Wilkins 1668 scan, archive.org](https://archive.org/details/AnEssayTowardsARealCharacterAndAPhilosophicalLanguage)
- The mechanism, in Wilkins's words: the Genus is a syllable, the "Differences under each of these Genus's may be expressed by these Consonants B, D, G, P, T, C, Z, S, N in this order 1–9", and "the Species may be expressed by putting one of the seven Vowels after the Consonant… to which may be added (to make up the number) two of the Diphthongs" (vowel order: α, a, e, i, o, u, y, then diphthongs) — [Wilkins 1668 scan](https://archive.org/details/AnEssayTowardsARealCharacterAndAPhilosophicalLanguage)
- Wilkins's own worked examples (primary text, Part IV): "If (De) signifie Element, then (Deb) must signifie the first difference; which (according to the Tables) is Fire: and (Debα) will denote the first Species, which is Flame. (Det) will be the fifth difference under that Genus, which is Appearing Meteor; (Detα) the first Species, viz. Rainbow; (Deta) the second, viz. Halo." Also: "(Ti) … Sensible Quality, then (Tid) … Colours; and (Tida) … Redness; (Tide) the third Species, which is Greenness." — [Wilkins 1668 scan](https://archive.org/details/AnEssayTowardsARealCharacterAndAPhilosophicalLanguage). (The OCR garbles the vowel symbols. The α readings follow Wilkins's stated vowel order, and "Halo. El. V. 2." in his index confirms that Deta = Element, 5th difference, 2nd species. Wikipedia writes "Deba" for flame. Wilkins's notation is Debα.)
- **The one-letter-difference problem appears in Wilkins's own showcase examples:** Tida "redness" vs Tide "greenness", and Detα "rainbow" vs Deta "halo", each differ by one vowel — [Wilkins 1668 scan](https://archive.org/details/AnEssayTowardsARealCharacterAndAPhilosophicalLanguage)
- Overflow hack: where a difference has more than 9 species (plants, animals), Wilkins inserts L or R after the first consonant. "If Gαde be Tulip, viz. the third Species in the first Nine… So if Zana be Salmon, viz. the second species in the first Nine, then Zlana must signifie Gudgeon… the eleventh Species." Wilkins admits that "there is one inconvenience in this Contrivance… they are scarce capable of the derivation of Adjectives." — [Wilkins 1668 scan](https://archive.org/details/AnEssayTowardsARealCharacterAndAPhilosophicalLanguage)
- **Folklore check, Borges's "zana = salmon": genuine.** Wilkins himself uses "Zana" for salmon as an illustration (above). His fish table describes the salmon group as "common to fresh and salt water… the biggest, of a reddish flesh" — [Wilkins 1668 scan](https://archive.org/details/AnEssayTowardsARealCharacterAndAPhilosophicalLanguage). Borges's gloss, "a scaled river fish, with ruddy meat", is a loose paraphrase ("river" is not Wilkins's wording). Borges's essay first appeared in *La Nación*, 8 Feb 1942. Per Wikipedia, Borges had not seen Wilkins's book and worked from others' accounts — [Wikipedia: The Analytical Language of John Wilkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Analytical_Language_of_John_Wilkins). Borges's "Celestial Emporium" animal list is Borges's invention, not Wilkins's.
- "Zi" = genus of beasts, "Zit" = rapacious beasts of the dog kind, "Zitα" = dog. This is as quoted on Wikipedia. I could not locate it in the OCR; the scan shows "Zit…" at one point, but I could not confirm — [Wikipedia: An Essay…](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Essay_Towards_a_Real_Character,_and_a_Philosophical_Language)
- Eco notes Wilkins himself slipped: he used *Gαde* (in the tables, barley) where *Gαpe* (tulip) was apparently intended — [Wikipedia, citing Eco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Essay_Towards_a_Real_Character,_and_a_Philosophical_Language). Primary check: the text does use "Gαde" = Tulip as its illustration, and the dictionary places Tulip at "HL. IV. 3." I did not verify which herb table Gαde actually decodes to, so Eco's barley reading is not independently checked — [Wilkins 1668 scan](https://archive.org/details/AnEssayTowardsARealCharacterAndAPhilosophicalLanguage)
- Collaborators: John Ray did the botany, Francis Willughby the zoology. William Lloyd compiled the alphabetical dictionary (Part V). Influences were Dalgarno's *Ars Signorum* (1661) and Francis Lodwick's 1652 *Ground-Work* — [Wikipedia: An Essay…](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Essay_Towards_a_Real_Character,_and_a_Philosophical_Language)
- Contemporary criticism quoted on Wikipedia (footnote 18): "words expressing closely related ideas have almost the same form, differing perhaps by their last letter only… it would be exceedingly difficult to remember all these minute distinctions" — [Wikipedia: An Essay…](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Essay_Towards_a_Real_Character,_and_a_Philosophical_Language)
- Wikipedia says Wilkins regarded the work as a "proof of concept". Enthusiasm declined in the medium term — [Wikipedia: An Essay…](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Essay_Towards_a_Real_Character,_and_a_Philosophical_Language)

**Wilkins: recorded use (Notes and Records of the Royal Society, 2018)**
- "Enthusiasts for John Wilkins's artificial language were numerous in the later seventeenth century, but practitioners less so." — [Notes Rec. R. Soc., "Seventeenth-century 'double writing' schemes…" (PMC5906426)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5906426/)
- Known practitioners named in that article:
  - Robert Hooke wrote his pocket-watch spring secret in the real character (late Aug 1675; engraved in *Helioscopes*, 1676).
  - Richard Towneley deciphered it ("Decipherd right", per Hooke) and attempted Aesop's fable of the Cock and the Jewel, which he abandoned.
  - Thomas Pigot made an interlinear translation and devised a set of 12 dice for learning the characters.
  - Andrew Paschall wrote a whole letter to Aubrey in the character (1677) and drew botanical tables. The rediscovered 17 Jan 1676 letter to Hooke is also his.
  - Francis Lodwick reportedly read the character fluently and translated a Petty discourse.
  - John Wallis replied with a romanized text.

  — [PMC5906426](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5906426/)
- Hooke praised the character as "so perfectly and thoroughly Methodical… perfectly free from all manner of ambiguity". Yet the article finds Paschall "is merely using the script as a way of writing English in code" — [PMC5906426](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5906426/)
- Encyclopedia.com (search summary) says only Hooke showed lasting commitment and the Royal Society committee set up to improve the *Essay* "never reported" — [Encyclopedia.com: John Wilkins](https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/history/british-and-irish-history-biographies/john-wilkins). Vivian Salmon's article "John Wilkins' Essay (1668): critics and continuators" is the standard scholarly treatment; I did not read it — [Benjamins](https://benjamins.com/catalog/hl.1.2.02sal)
- Okrent (2009) treats Wilkins as one of her six case studies. Reviews summarise her conclusion: the scheme did not help people understand each other, but Wilkins had "unwittingly stumbled upon a type of thesaurus" (Roget used it as a model) — [Wikipedia: In the Land of Invented Languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_the_Land_of_Invented_Languages); [Zócalo review](https://www.zocalopublicsquare.org/book-review-in-the-land-of-invented-languages/)

**Dalgarno, *Ars Signorum* (1661)**
- Scottish schoolmaster at Oxford. Published in 1661 with a fold-out taxonomy chart, sample texts (Bible passages, Aesop) and a Latin dictionary — [FrathWiki via search summary](https://www.frathwiki.com/Ars_signorum) (page returned 403 on fetch; content from search summary)
- Encoding example (Eco's account): *k* = terrestrial animals, *η* = uncleft hoof, so **Nηk** = whole-hoofed animals (horse, elephant). Horse = *Nηk-pot*, "animal with uncleft hoof / courageous". Sub-species such as horse, elephant, mule and donkey get added letters — [therealsamizdat, quoting Eco](https://therealsamizdat.com/2016/07/05/eco-george-delgarno-2/) (from search summary). The Nηk/horse example appears consistently, but I did not read the primary text.
- Jaap Maat's *Philosophical Languages in the Seventeenth Century: Dalgarno, Wilkins, Leibniz* (Kluwer 2004; based on his 1999 Amsterdam dissertation) is the standard scholarly treatment. Dalgarno and Wilkins built complete systems for communication across language groups. Leibniz aimed at a language that would improve reasoning and never finished it — [ILLC abstract](https://www.illc.uva.nl/Research/Publications/Dissertations/DS-1999-03.abstract.txt); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-94-007-1036-8). The book is reviewed by Matthew Honig in *Language* 83(4), 2007 — [Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/233565). Full text was paywalled.

**Solresol (François Sudre)**
- Sudre (1787–1862), a French music teacher, began it in 1817. *Langue Musicale Universelle* was published posthumously in 1866. It had a brief vogue in the later 19th century, with patronage associated with Victor Hugo and Napoleon III — [Wikipedia: Solresol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solresol)
- Encoding: words are built only from the 7 solfège syllables, at most 5 per word, which gives 11,732 possible words. The first syllable and the word length mark the semantic class: for four-syllable words, *sol-* is arts and sciences (*soldoredo* "art") and *re-* is clothing and family. Reversing a word gives its opposite: *fala* "good" / *lafa* "bad", *domisol* "God" / *solmido* "Devil" — [Wikipedia: Solresol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solresol)
- Part of speech is marked by stress position (stress on the first syllable makes a noun). Word order is strict. The same words can be carried by colours, numbers, Curwen hand-signs or a 7-glyph script — [Wikipedia: Solresol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solresol)
- Problems:
  - With only 7 syllables, speakers must pause between words so they don't run together — [Wikipedia: Solresol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solresol); [Atlas Obscura](https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/what-is-solresol)
  - Boleslas Gajewski's *Grammaire du Solrésol* (1902) changed vocabulary: *fasol* went from "here" to "why?", so versions now diverge (Sudre, Gajewski, "Modern Solresol") — [Wikipedia: Solresol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solresol)
  - An ISO 639-3 request was rejected in 2018 — [Wikipedia: Solresol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solresol)

**Ro (Rev. Edward Powell Foster)**
- Begun in 1904, first booklet 1906, a 6,000+ word dictionary in 1928 — [Wikipedia: Ro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ro_(language))
- Encoding: shared prefixes mark categories, e.g. colours start with *bofo-*: *bofoc* "red", *bofof* "yellow". Foster's rationale was that an unseen word would "like a painting seen for the first time, convey at least some of the meaning to the eye" — [Wikipedia: Ro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ro_(language))
- Documented weakness: "usually one consonant makes the word different in meaning, but still similar enough that the intended meaning often cannot be guessed from context" — [Wikipedia: Ro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ro_(language))
- Sponsors included Melvil Dewey, Vice-President Charles G. Dawes and Alice Vanderbilt Morris (IALA). Speaker numbers are unknown. It never caught on — [Wikipedia: Ro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ro_(language))

**aUI (W. John Weilgart)**
- Weilgart (1913–1981) was a Viennese-born philosopher and psychoanalyst. He built it in the 1950s as "the Language of Space"; the 4th edition textbook appeared in 1979 — [Wikipedia: aUI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AUI_(language))
- Encoding: 31 "morpheme-phonemes", each sound one meaning. Vowels carry space, movement, light, life, human, time, matter, sound, feeling and spirit. Consonants carry together, existence, through, this, inside, question, equal, above, quality, quantity, before, positive, thing, toward, active, power, relation, part. There are also negation and condition markers, and numerals use nasal vowels — [Wikipedia: aUI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AUI_(language))
- Use: Weilgart used aUI in psychotherapy, having clients "spell out" troubling concepts in aUI elements. An ISO 639-3 request was rejected in 2019 — [Wikipedia: aUI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AUI_(language)). The Wikipedia article itself is flagged for conflict of interest and self-published sources, so treat its details as weakly sourced.

### Inferences
- Wilkins's own textbook examples (Tida/Tide, Detα/Deta) are minimal pairs between sibling concepts. Under that design, a single misheard or miswritten vowel produces a plausible wrong meaning in the same field (red becomes green), which context can't easily repair. Ro's critics state the same failure. This is the strongest historical evidence for the comparison language's choice of arbitrary, maximally distinct roots.
- Wilkins also shows the stale-category problem in its earliest form. A fixed 9-slot numbering forced the L/R overflow hack and broke adjective derivation. Insertions or reclassifications (new species, revised botany) would mean renaming words.
- Real use of Wilkins was written, by a handful of Royal Society virtuosi, often as a cipher or a puzzle. No one is recorded speaking any of these taxonomic languages conversationally.
- Solresol's antonym-by-reversal makes opposites maximally confusable (fala/lafa), the worst case for a noisy channel.

### Gaps
- I did not obtain the Knowlson (1975) or Maat (2004) full texts, or Eco's chapter text directly. Eco's barley/tulip claim and Dalgarno's genus count and radical inventory are unverified at primary level.
- I did not verify Wikipedia's "Zitα = dog" in the scan. The widely repeated quote that Ray was "constrained… not to follow the lead of nature" appeared only in a search summary with no traceable source, so it is not used as a finding.
- I found no historian's assessment of Solresol's real usage numbers (e.g., how many people ever conversed in it) and no primary Sudre text.
- No independent scholarly assessment of aUI was found.

## Q2. Blissymbolics: design, AAC use for people with cerebral palsy, evidence, why the niche worked

### Takeaway
Bliss designed a meaning-based, soundless ideographic script for international communication, and it failed in that role. It found a durable niche from 1971 as a pointing-board system for non-speaking children with cerebral palsy, because users need neither speech nor reading and can combine a few hundred elements into new meanings. Controlled studies consistently find Blissymbols less transparent and slower to learn than pictographic sets such as PCS. In AAC, the more pictorial sets have largely displaced it.

### Cited Findings
- Charles K. Bliss (1897–1985) published "Semantography" in 1949. He was motivated by inter-ethnic hatred in multilingual Chernivtsi and inspired by Chinese characters seen as a refugee in Shanghai (1942–49) — [Wikipedia: Blissymbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blissymbols)
- Design: "several hundred basic symbols, each representing a concept, which can be composed together to generate new symbols". It has no phonology and is purely written. Three marker shapes classify words, giving noun/verb/adjective-like roles: matter (square), energy (cone) and human evaluation (inverted cone) — [Wikipedia: Blissymbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blissymbols)
- In 1971 Shirley McNaughton's team at the Ontario Crippled Children's Centre (OCCC) found Bliss in Elizabeth Helfman's *Signs and Symbols Around the World* (1967) and applied it to children with cerebral palsy — [U of T archives / search summary](https://discoverarchives.library.utoronto.ca/index.php/blissymbolics-communication-institute-canada-fonds); [Wikipedia: Blissymbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blissymbols). The symbols served as "visual keys to understand the meaning of the English words, especially the abstract words" — [Wikipedia: Blissymbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blissymbols)
- Governance conflict: Bliss objected to the clinicians' practical modifications. In 1975 he licensed the new Blissymbolics Communication Foundation (later BCI). A 1982 settlement gave OCCC an exclusive, perpetual licence, and Bliss received $160,000 — [Wikipedia: Blissymbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blissymbols)
- Size and standards:
  - BCI's 1991 reference held 2,300 vocabulary items — [Wikipedia: Blissymbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blissymbols)
  - BCI now says there are "over 6,500" symbols and that the system is "quick and easy to learn" and usable "at a pre-reading level". It publishes symbol files in 2022–2026 versions and a 2020 fundamental-rules document — [BCI: About](https://www.blissymbolics.org/index.php/about-blissymbolics)
  - Bliss got an ISO-IR character-set registration in 1993 and the ISO 639 code `zbl`. It is not in Unicode (as of the Wikipedia account) because of layout problems — [Wikipedia: Blissymbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blissymbols)
  - Main present use is AAC in Canada, Sweden and some other countries — [Wikipedia: Blissymbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blissymbols)
- Evidence:
  - **Hurlbut, Iwata & Green (1982), *JABA* 15:241–258.** Three nonvocal adolescents with CP were trained on Bliss vs an iconic picture system. "Students learned iconic symbols faster, maintained a higher percentage of iconic pictures, gave more correct responses during generalization probes… and used iconic responses more often" in daily life. A summary gives mastery as about 4× faster for coloured line drawings — [ERIC EJ267186](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ267186). **Conflict:** BCI's research page describes this same study as indicating Bliss is a "viable communication alternative" — [BCI Research](https://www.blissymbolics.org/index.php/research)
  - **Mizuko (1987)**, *AAC* 3:129–136, tested typically developing 3-year-olds on the transparency and learning of Blissymbols vs PCS vs Picsyms — [ERIC EJ360900](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ360900). Secondary summaries report PCS and Picsyms were more transparent and easier to learn than Blissymbols — [search summary of AAC literature](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/transparency-and-ease-of-learning-of-symbols-represented-by-bliss/)
  - **Alant et al. (2005)**, *Int J Lang Commun Disord*: CyberGlyphs had higher learnability and retention than Blissymbols "irrespective of the group or the sequence of exposure" — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1080/13682820400009980); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7659806_Comparison_of_the_learnability_and_retention_between_Blissymbols_and_CyberGlyphs)
  - An iconicity hierarchy often cited in AAC teaching runs: objects > photos > line-drawing sets (Picsyms, PCS, Rebus) > Blissymbols > written words. One cited rating puts mean iconicity at PCS 6.36 vs Bliss 2.18 — from a search summary of [Miami U thesis / AAC literature](https://etd.ohiolink.edu/acprod/odb_etd/ws/send_file/send?accession=miami1144112971&disposition=inline). The originating study for those numbers was not confirmed.
  - BCI's own bibliography lists studies of factors in Bliss learning (Fuller & Lloyd 1992; Luftig & Bersani 1985; Raghavendra & Fristoe 1995). It also lists Udwin & Yule (1990), a longitudinal CP study; Dahlgren Sandberg (1996) on literacy; and McNaughton (1993, 1995) linking Bliss to literacy acquisition — [BCI Research](https://www.blissymbolics.org/index.php/research)
- Linguistic status is contested. DeFrancis and Unger argue that ideographic systems with natural-language capacity do not exist — [Wikipedia: Blissymbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blissymbols)

### Inferences
- Why the niche worked:
  - The users can't speak and often can't yet read, so a sound-free, meaning-composing board beats spelling.
  - Composition lets a fixed board of a few hundred elements express thousands of meanings, which matters when every selection is physically costly.
  - Teachers and therapists formed a small institutional community (OCCC/BCI) that could standardise and train.
- Its meaning-transparency did not translate into faster learning. Pictorial sets beat it on first-learning speed, and its remaining advantage is generativity and a claimed bridge to abstract words and literacy.
- For the comparison language: Bliss succeeded only as a written, pointed-at system, where there is no acoustic channel to garble similar forms. That is consistent with the taxonomic-language failures being mainly a speech and memory problem.

### Gaps
- I found no recent (2015–2026) systematic review quantifying current Bliss user numbers or market share against PCS and SymbolStix.
- I did not read the effect sizes in Mizuko 1987 or Alant 2005 beyond abstracts and summaries.
- I found no robust outcome evidence for McNaughton's literacy-bridge claim.

## Q3. Loglan/Lojban: goals, root-making algorithm, grammar, attitudinals/evidentials, the split, community, whether unambiguity held, reforms

### Takeaway
Loglan (1955) and its 1987 fork Lojban have syntax that a machine can parse unambiguously and roots generated by a speaker-weighted algorithm from six major languages. Lojban also has optional attitudinal and evidential particles. In practice:
- the Sapir-Whorf test that motivated the language was never run;
- the unambiguity claim is explicitly limited to morphology and syntax, and tanru compounds are semantically ambiguous by design;
- the speaker community stayed at "a few dozen" conversational users;
- the 1997 "frozen baseline" was followed by decades of reform (BPFK 2003, xorlo 2004/2007, dotside 2015, CLL 1.1 2016), and a 2026 unofficial CLL revision is still catching the book up to usage.

### Cited Findings

**Goals and origin**
- James Cooke Brown began Loglan in 1955 at the University of Florida. A *Scientific American* article followed in June 1960 (vol. 202 no. 6), and an NIMH grant (M-4980) came in 1961 — [Lojban timeline](https://mw.lojban.org/index.php?title=Lojban_timeline)
- Purpose: to test Sapir-Whorf. Brown wanted "a language so different from natural languages that people learning it would think in a different way if the hypothesis were true". The grammar was based on predicate logic and meant to be parseable only one way — [Wikipedia: Loglan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loglan)
- Brown's proposed experiment: English-speaking students randomly assigned to summer workshops in French, Chinese or Loglan, plus a no-language control. He predicted the largest effect for Loglan and measured "creativity" and "performance" effects. The text is entirely prospective: "it is only if sufficient numbers of us do accept this calculated wager that we will ever find out" — [Loglan 1, ch. 7](https://www.loglan.org/Loglan1/chap7.html)
- No such experiment has been documented. The Lojban wiki cites the self-selected speaker pool as a confound, "the only way to fix that is to get more Lojban speakers" — [La Lojban: Sapir-Whorf Hypothesis](https://mw.lojban.org/papri/Sapir-Whorf_Hypothesis). The LLG brochure presents only proposals — [Lojban brochure ch. 4](https://lojban.org/publications/level0/brochure/lingissues.html)

**Root-making (cultural neutrality)**
- Loglan drew roots from 8 languages: English, Chinese, Hindi, Russian, Spanish, French, Japanese and German — [Wikipedia: Loglan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loglan)
- The Lojban gismu algorithm ran in five steps — [CLL 1.1 §4.14](https://lojban.org/publications/cll/cll_v1.1_xhtml-section-chunks/section-gismu-making.html):
  1. Source words in Chinese, English, Hindi, Spanish, Russian and Arabic were Lojbanized, with clusters simplified and endings dropped.
  2. Each candidate CVCCV/CCVCV form was scored against each source word. Three or more letters matching in order scored the match count. Two letters scored 2 if adjacent or separated by one letter in both words.
  3. The score was divided by the Lojbanized source length and multiplied by the language weight.
  4. Candidates conflicting with existing gismu were removed: identical forms, and forms differing only in the final vowel or by one "too similar" consonant per a table.
  5. The highest score usually won.
- Weights: originally 1985 data, Chinese 0.36, English 0.21, Hindi 0.16, Spanish 0.11, Russian 0.09, Arabic 0.07. A later revision used Chinese 0.347, Hindi 0.196, English 0.160, Spanish 0.123, Russian 0.089, Arabic 0.085. Second-language speakers counted at half — [CLL 1.1 §4.14](https://lojban.org/publications/cll/cll_v1.1_xhtml-section-chunks/section-gismu-making.html)
- Lojban concedes the semantic inventory is not neutral: "many of its conceptual distinctions, for example the color set, are clearly biased towards particular languages", e.g. it has a word for "brown" but only one "blue" (Russian has two) — [Lojban brochure ch. 4](https://lojban.org/publications/level0/brochure/lingissues.html)

**Grammar and unambiguity**
- The language is designed to be morphologically and syntactically unambiguous, so each text has exactly one parse. But "the claim for semantic unambiguity is a limited one only". Names and tanru are deliberately ambiguous — [Lojban brochure ch. 4](https://lojban.org/publications/level0/brochure/lingissues.html); [Wikipedia: Lojban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lojban)
- Tanru (modifier-head compounds) are open-ended: *nixli ckule* "girl school" covers many relations. CLL says all tanru are semantically ambiguous, and the speaker who uses them (as "pragmatically all speakers must") risks being misunderstood. Removing the ambiguity needs relative clauses or lujvo, and full disambiguation "can require too much effort" — [Lojban brochure ch. 4](https://lojban.org/publications/level0/brochure/lingissues.html); [Lojban Reference Grammar ch. 5 (via search summary)](https://lojban.org/publications/reference_grammar/chapter5.html)
- Parsers: the original grammar was YACC-based. The community later moved to PEG grammars (camxes). Loglan's current maintainer, Randall Holmes, is likewise writing "Loglan as a PEG grammar" — [loglan.org](https://www.loglan.org/); [int19h/cll issue #35 (PEG morphology)](https://github.com/int19h/cll/issues/35)

**Attitudinals and evidentials**
- Indicators may appear at the start of an utterance or "after any word". At the start they cover the whole utterance; otherwise they attach to the preceding word. They are grammatically optional. Intensity runs on the scale *cai, sai, ru'e, cu'i, nairu'e, naisai, naicai* — [Lojban Reference Grammar ch. 13](https://www.lojban.org/publications/reference_grammar/chapter13.html)
- The ten evidentials are *ja'o* "I conclude", *ca'e* "I define", *ba'a* "I expect/experience/remember", *su'a* "I generalize", *ti'e* "I hear (hearsay)", *ka'u* "I know by cultural means", *se'o* "I know by internal experience", *za'a* "I observe", *pe'i* "I opine" and *ru'a* "I postulate". They draw on American-Indian-language descriptions and on Láadan. CLL says the attitudinal system's ramifications in actual usage "are unknown" — [Lojban Reference Grammar ch. 13](https://www.lojban.org/publications/reference_grammar/chapter13.html); [Wikipedia: Lojban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lojban)

**The split and the trademark dispute**
- The Lojban project began in May 1987 (Bob LeChevalier's group). The Logical Language Group was founded in July 1987, and the name "Lojban" adopted on 1 Aug 1987 — [Lojban timeline](https://mw.lojban.org/index.php?title=Lojban_timeline)
- Trademark: Brown registered "Loglan" as a trademark in February 1988 and threatened suit. The trademark was cancelled on 4 Feb 1991, and the U.S. Court of Appeals upheld the cancellation on 28 Apr 1992 — [Lojban timeline](https://mw.lojban.org/index.php?title=Lojban_timeline). Wikipedia words it as non-trademarkability "upheld by the United States Patent and Trademark Office", noting the point is contested within the community — [Wikipedia: Loglan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loglan). The root cause was that Brown claimed copyright over the language's components and restricted their use — [Wikipedia: Lojban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lojban)
- Brown died on 13 Feb 2000 in Argentina — [Wikipedia: James Cooke Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Cooke_Brown). Loglan continues under Randall Holmes, with a new "Loglan Center, Inc." based in Boise, Idaho. Its newest substantive status report is from Fall 2015, it runs a mailing list, and members meet weekly in Second Life (page modified 2 Feb 2026) — [loglan.org](https://www.loglan.org/)

### Inferences
- Unambiguity held where it was mechanically defined (morphology, parse) and gave way where speakers needed brevity (tanru, articles). The largest reform, xorlo, redefined the default article. The community's shift from *le* to *lo* indicates that speakers prefer the less committal option.
- The gismu algorithm's conflict-removal step rejects forms differing only in the final vowel or by one similar consonant. Lojban already enforces a minimum phonological distance between roots, which directly supports the comparison language's "maximally distinct roots" rule. Lojban got there by filtering after scoring, and the speaker-weighted resemblance gave little recognisability in practice: no source found claims learners recognise roots from their own language.
- Optional attitudinals and evidentials are the closest precedent for the comparison language's optional evidence and confidence particles. Even CLL describes their real-use effects as unknown, and no usage study was found.
- The 1997 five-year "freeze" didn't stop drift. It produced a frozen book alongside a moving usage, a gap still being closed in 2026, which is a caution for any "baseline" promise.
- Governance conflict (the Brown trademark, Bliss vs OCCC) appears in two of the six projects. Inventor control over a language has repeatedly split or stalled communities.

### Gaps
- I found no published corpus study measuring actual ambiguity or misunderstanding rates in Lojban conversation, nor any quantitative learnability study.
- I found no reliable 2025–26 count of fluent speakers; the best is the 2008 "few dozen".
- Details of the 2016 tag+selbri vote and the CGV ban were seen only in issue-tracker summaries.
- The exact year of the weight revision (often cited as 1994 or 1995) is not stated on the fetched CLL page.
- Okrent's specific judgements on Lojban learnability were not retrieved from the book text.

## Q4. Studies or careful commentary on how these designs fared with users

### Takeaway
The only controlled user studies found are AAC studies of Blissymbols. They show that a compositional, meaning-encoding symbol system is learned more slowly than plainly pictorial symbols, though it remains usable. For the spoken taxonomic and logical languages, the record is historical: small numbers of practitioners, mostly written use, recurring complaints about near-identical forms, and in Lojban's case a self-documented retreat from semantic unambiguity.

### Cited Findings
- Bliss vs iconic pictures, adolescents with CP: the iconic pictures were learned faster, generalised better and were used more in daily life — [ERIC EJ267186 (Hurlbut et al. 1982)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ267186)
- Bliss vs PCS/Picsyms with 3-year-olds — [ERIC EJ360900 (Mizuko 1987)](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ360900). Bliss vs CyberGlyphs: CyberGlyphs were better learned and retained — [Alant et al. 2005](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1080/13682820400009980)
- Wilkins: "enthusiasts… numerous… practitioners less so". Surviving uses are a handful of letters, transcriptions and a learning-dice game, some of it used as English-in-cipher — [PMC5906426](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5906426/)
- Wilkins, near-synonyms: "closely related ideas have almost the same form… exceedingly difficult to remember" — [Wikipedia: An Essay… (fn 18)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Essay_Towards_a_Real_Character,_and_a_Philosophical_Language). Ro: one consonant separates meanings that context can't recover — [Wikipedia: Ro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ro_(language))
- Lojban in conversation: ad-hoc compounds and coinages fail without prior agreement — [Lojban forum 2008](https://tiki.lojban.org/tiki-view_forum_thread.php?forumId=5&comments_parentId=7196&comments_per_page=1&thread_style=commentStyle_plain). Tanru ambiguity is accepted by design — [Lojban brochure ch. 4](https://lojban.org/publications/level0/brochure/lingissues.html)

### Inferences
- Across all six projects the consistent pattern is that encoding meaning in form improves guessability on paper and hurts discrimination and memory in use. The one success (Bliss) is visual, used with a pointing board, and in a population with no better alternative.
- Each design's "perfection" claim (Hooke's "free from all manner of ambiguity", Loglan's single parse) was true only in the formal layer the designer controlled.

### Gaps
- I found no experimental study of spoken taxonomic vocabularies (e.g., confusion rates for minimal-pair roots in Ro-like or Wilkins-like lexicons). This is the most directly relevant missing evidence.
- I found no Sapir-Whorf result from Loglan/Lojban, since the experiment was never run.
