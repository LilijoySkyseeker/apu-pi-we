# Classical international auxiliary languages: design principles and outcomes

Scope: Volapük (1879), Esperanto (1887), Ido (1907), Occidental/Interlingue (1922), Novial (1928), Interlingua (1951), Basic English (1930). Researched 2026-10-09.

Source-quality note: most findings come from English Wikipedia articles, which summarise the interlinguistics literature (Garvía, Large, Okrent, Blanke and others) rather than primary texts. The primary and academic sources actually read are Wandel 2015 (full text), the Public Domain Review essay on Volapük, and abstracts or snippets for Fiedler and Lindstedt. Garvía's *Esperanto and Its Rivals* (2015), Okrent, Large, Blanke and Tonkin could not be fetched (the PDF link was blocked). Claims that rest only on a search snippet are marked "(snippet)".

---

## 1. Creators, dates, stated goals, intended users

### Takeaway
All seven came from single individuals or small committees between 1879 and 1951. All aimed at a neutral second language for international contact, not at replacing native languages. They split into "schematic" designs (Volapük, Esperanto, Ido), which aim for regularity and ease of production, and "naturalistic" designs (Occidental, Interlingua), which aim for instant readability by Western Europeans. Basic English is different again: a cut-down natural language.

### Cited Findings
- **Volapük**: created by Johann Martin Schleyer, a Roman Catholic priest in Baden. He published a sketch in May 1879 in *Sionsharfe* and a full German book in 1880 — [Wikipedia: Volapük](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volap%C3%BCk)
- **Esperanto**: created by L. L. Zamenhof, a Jewish ophthalmologist from Białystok. *Unua Libro* appeared on 26 July 1887 under the pseudonym "Doktoro Esperanto" ("one who hopes"). His goal was "an easy and flexible language that would serve as a universal second language, to foster world peace and international understanding" — [Wikipedia: Esperanto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esperanto)
- **Ido**: came out of the Delegation for the Adoption of an International Auxiliary Language (Paris, October 1907), led by the mathematician Louis Couturat. The project was submitted anonymously; its main author turned out to be Louis de Beaufront, who was Zamenhof's own chosen Esperanto representative to the Delegation — [Wikipedia: Ido](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ido_language)
- **Occidental/Interlingue**: Edgar de Wahl announced it in 1922 in the magazine *Kosmoglott* (Tallinn), after experimenting from 1906 to 1921 under the name "Auli". His aim was "maximal grammatical regularity and natural character" — [Wikipedia: Interlingue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interlingue)
- **Novial**: Otto Jespersen, a Danish professional linguist (unlike Zamenhof), presented it in *An International Language* (1928). He wanted a language that was "euphonious and regular" while avoiding the "arbitrary and artificial character" of Esperanto and Ido — [Wikipedia: Novial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novial)
- **Interlingua**: the International Auxiliary Language Association (IALA) was founded in 1924 by the American heiress Alice Vanderbilt Morris to study auxiliary languages scientifically. It concluded that existing languages were inadequate and published Interlingua in 1951 — [Wikipedia: Interlingua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interlingua)
  - E. Clark Stillman built the vocabulary-selection method with Alexander Gode's help, after IALA's research moved to New York in WWII (snippet) — [Wikipedia: Eligibility of international words in Interlingua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eligibility_of_international_words_in_Interlingua)
  - The *Interlingua–English Dictionary* (IED), directed by Gode, was published by Storm Publishers in 1951 — [Wikipedia: IED](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interlingua%E2%80%93English_Dictionary)
- **Basic English**: created by C. K. Ogden in 1925 and formally presented in *Basic English: A General Introduction with Rules and Grammar* (1930). It grew out of Ogden and I. A. Richards' *The Meaning of Meaning* (1923). After WWII it was promoted as a tool for international peace — [Wikipedia: Basic English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_English)

### Inferences
- Every language with a living founder ran into trouble over who controlled changes: Schleyer's proprietary claim, de Beaufront's concealed authorship, Jespersen's 1934 respelling. Esperanto is the exception because Zamenhof gave up control: the Fundamento was frozen in 1905 and the community, not the author, owned it.

### Gaps
- Primary texts (Jespersen 1928, Gode's IED introduction, Ogden 1930, the Fundamento) were not read directly. The goals above come from secondary summaries.

## 2. Vocabulary source: a priori vs a posteriori, root selection, recognisability

### Takeaway
All seven are a posteriori and European-sourced. They differ in how much they distort their source roots:
- Volapük clipped roots to monosyllables until they were unrecognisable.
- Esperanto and Ido keep roots recognisable to Europeans but reshape them to fit their schemes.
- Occidental and Interlingua keep the "natural" Romance forms.

None was designed for recognisability by non-Europeans. Critics say Esperanto's "international" vocabulary is hard for many Asians.

### Cited Findings
- **Volapük**
  - Vocabulary is mainly English, plus German, Romance and Russian, but "many words were altered beyond recognition, as Schleyer sought to make most of the vocabulary monosyllabic". Example: *vol* (world) + *pük* (speak), both from English — [Wikipedia: Volapük](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volap%C3%BCk)
  - Schleyer avoided the sound /r/ "for the sake of children and old people, also for some Asiatic nations". Examples: *pöp* (paper), *bil* (beer), *blöf* (proof), *löf* (love) — [Public Domain Review](https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/truth-beauty-and-volapuk/)
  - Arie de Jong's 1931 reform brought /r/ back, so *lilöm* became *rein* (rain) — [Wikipedia: Volapük](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volap%C3%BCk)
- **Esperanto**
  - Roughly 80% of the vocabulary is Romance, the rest Germanic, Greek and Slavic — [Wikipedia: Esperanto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esperanto)
  - Another summary says "about three-quarters" Romance, the rest mainly German, Greek and Slavic (snippet). **The exact share is contested and depends on whether you count roots or running text** — [HandWiki: Esperanto](https://handwiki.org/wiki/Social:Esperanto)
  - "Both the grammar and the 'international' vocabulary are difficult for many Asians" — [101languages criticism page (derivative of Wikipedia's "Criticism of Esperanto")](https://www.101languages.net/esperanto/criticism.html)
- **Ido**: roots were chosen for "maximum internationality" across English, French, German, Italian, Spanish and Portuguese. In one analysis, 38% of roots belong to all six languages, 91% are found in French, and 79% in Spanish and English. The stated aim was to serve "the greatest number of people possible" — [Wikipedia: Ido](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ido_language)
- **Occidental**: designed to be readable by Western Europeans, especially Romance readers. De Wahl's rule regularly derives nouns and adjectives from the verb stem, which reproduces the natural Latin "double-stem" forms (e.g. the *-t-* forms behind *-ion* and *-or* words) — [Wikipedia: Interlingue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interlingue)
- **Novial**: Romance and Germanic vocabulary — [Wikipedia: Novial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novial)
- **Interlingua**
  - The control languages are English, French, Italian, and Spanish/Portuguese (counted as one unit), with German and Russian as secondary controls — [Wikipedia: Interlingua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interlingua)
  - A word's standard form is its "nearest etymological prototype": the common ancestor of its variants in the contributing languages (snippet) — [Wikipedia: Eligibility of international words](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eligibility_of_international_words_in_Interlingua)
  - The IED has about 27,000 words from about 10,000 roots and lists 125 affixes — [Wikipedia: IED](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interlingua%E2%80%93English_Dictionary)
  - The philosophy was to record an international vocabulary that already exists, not invent one. Proponents claim written Interlingua is understandable to "over 900 million native speakers of Romance languages" — [Wikipedia: Interlingua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interlingua)
- **Basic English**: a subset of English with 850 core words (600 "things", 150 "qualities", 100 "operations"). Further layers:
  - 200 "international" words assumed known;
  - 150-word lists for specialist fields;
  - about 1,200 words as a first target and about 2,000 for "standard" English.

  — [Wikipedia: Basic English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_English)

### Inferences
- Volapük is the only one whose roots were deliberately reshaped away from their sources (monosyllables, no /r/). That is the closest historical precedent to a priori roots chosen for robustness. It failed on recognisability, but the record blames its collapse mostly on governance, not on vocabulary (see section 6).
- Interlingua's "prototype" method is the reverse of an a priori design: it gives up neutrality to get zero learning cost for readers of one language family.
- "Recognisable to Europeans" is the measure all six European designs optimised for. None tested recognisability for speakers of Chinese, Hindi, Arabic, Swahili and so on. No study measuring that was found.

### Gaps
- No quantitative study was found of how recognisable Esperanto, Ido or Interlingua roots are to non-European speakers.
- The "80% Romance" figure has no traceable primary study in the sources reached.

## 3. Morphology and grammatical categories

### Takeaway
The trend runs from heavy agglutination (Volapük: 4 cases, 1,584 verb forms), through moderate agglutination with mandatory accusative and agreement (Esperanto), to optional accusative and no agreement (Ido), to analytic tense auxiliaries (Novial), to near-natural simplified Romance (Interlingua). Basic English drops the verb as a lexical class.

Each successor deleted something its predecessor kept. The categories that kept being deleted:
- **case**: Ido and Novial made the accusative optional; Interlingua has none;
- **adjective agreement**: dropped by Ido, Novial and Interlingua;
- **person/number conjugation**: none of them have it;
- **grammatical gender**: none of them have it.

### Cited Findings
- **Volapük**: agglutinative, with four cases (nominative, genitive, dative, accusative) modelled on German. "A Volapük verb can be conjugated in 1,584 ways (including infinitives and reflexives)" — [Wikipedia: Volapük](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volap%C3%BCk)
- **Esperanto**
  - Agglutinative, with part-of-speech endings: noun *-o*, adjective *-a*, adverb *-e*, infinitive *-i*.
  - Verb endings: present *-as*, past *-is*, future *-os*, conditional *-us*, volitive *-u*.
  - Direct objects take accusative *-n* after plural *-j* (*-ojn*). Adjectives agree with nouns in number and case.

  — [Wikipedia: Esperanto grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esperanto_grammar)
- **Esperanto regularity claim**: the Fundamento's 16 rules have no systematic exceptions. The disputed areas are participle interpretation (the long *-ata*/*-ita* debate) and gender-neutral pronouns — [Wikipedia: Esperanto grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esperanto_grammar)
- **Esperanto gender**: there is no grammatical gender, but there is lexical gender. Root nouns such as *patro* (father) default to male, and the female is derived with *-ino* (*patrino*, mother). Pronouns are *li* (he), *ŝi* (she) and *ĝi* (it) — [Wikipedia: Gender reform in Esperanto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_reform_in_Esperanto)
- **Ido**: made the accusative optional (marked with *-n* only when needed), dropped adjective agreement, changed the plural from *-oj* to *-i*, and adopted gender-neutral roots.
  - It also adopted a "reversible derivation" principle (a derivation can be undone by removing the affix).
  - Piron criticised Ido's multiple causative suffixes (*-ifar*, *-izar*, *-igar*) as subtleties that "inhibit natural expression".

  — [Wikipedia: Ido](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ido_language)
- **Novial**: analytic. SVO order; accusative optional; no mandatory gender; verbs regular with no person/number agreement; tenses formed with auxiliary particles rather than inflections; invariant article *li*; adjectives precede nouns without agreement — [Wikipedia: Novial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novial)
- **Interlingua**: "no noun–adjective agreement by gender, case, or number", no subject–verb conjugation, invariant definite article *le* — [Wikipedia: Interlingua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interlingua)
- **Occidental**: aims at "maximal grammatical regularity and natural character". De Wahl's rule restored regularity to naturalistic derivation — [Wikipedia: Interlingue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interlingue)
- **Basic English**
  - Ogden allowed 18 verbs, called "operators", and said "There are no 'verbs' in Basic English."
  - Rules: plural *-s*; nouns in *-er*/*-ing*; adjectives in *-ing*/*-ed*; adverbs in *-ly*; negation with *un-*; comparison with *more*/*most*.

  — [Wikipedia: Basic English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_English)
  - The standard list of the 18 operators is come, get, give, go, keep, let, make, put, seem, take, be, do, have, say, see, send, may, will. The fetched article confirmed the number 18 but did not print this list, so the list is from general knowledge and should be verified against Ogden.

### Inferences
- In the historical sequence, mandatory inflectional categories are what reformers attacked most. Volapük's 1,584 verb forms and Esperanto's accusative and agreement were both cited as burdens.
- Novial is the nearest historical precedent for an isolating auxiliary language with particle-marked TAM. It went dormant when its single author died (section 6), so its design was never tested at scale.
- None of the seven grammaticalised evidentiality or confidence marking. No precedent was found in this set.

### Gaps
- No usage study was found showing which Ido or Novial "optional" features speakers actually used.
- Basic English's handling of phrasal verbs could not be sourced (see section 8).

## 4. Phonology and spelling

### Takeaway
Volapük's umlauts were an aesthetic choice by its author, defended against reformers. They became a symbol of the 1889–90 reform dispute. Esperanto's six new circumflex letters are its most-cited "artificial" feature, and Ido's first reform removed them. Esperanto's phoneme inventory and clusters are Slavic-European, and critics single out phonemes that are rare outside Europe.

### Cited Findings
- **Volapük umlauts**
  - Schleyer: "A language without umlauts sounds monotonous, harsh, and boring". Umlautless languages had an "endlessly gloomy u and o".
  - Contemporaries say the reform dispute centred partly on umlauts.

  — [Public Domain Review](https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/truth-beauty-and-volapuk/)
  - Unicode alternatives for the letters were added in 2014 — [Wikipedia: Volapük](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volap%C3%BCk)
- **Esperanto inventory**
  - A 28-letter alphabet including ĉ ĝ ĥ ĵ ŝ ŭ.
  - 22–24 consonants depending on analysis, 5 vowels, 2 semivowels, 6 diphthongs. /dz/ is the only consonant without its own letter.
  - Stress always falls on the second-to-last vowel.

  — [Wikipedia: Esperanto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esperanto)
- **Esperanto criticism**: some find the circumflex letters "odd or cumbersome", partly because they were invented for Esperanto. A critic called the phonology "essentially Belorussian with regularized stress", with phonemes such as /x/, /ʒ/ and /ts/ that are rare outside Europe — [101languages criticism page](https://www.101languages.net/esperanto/criticism.html); [HandWiki](https://handwiki.org/wiki/Social:Esperanto) (snippet)
- **Ido**: no diacritics. It uses digraphs *ch*, *sh* and *qu*, and *x* for /ks/ or /gz/, which made typing and printing easier — [Wikipedia: Ido](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ido_language)
- **Novial**: Jespersen's 1934 spelling reform added the letter *c*, which "displeased several of Novial's old and faithful friends" — [Wikipedia: Novial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novial)

### Inferences
- Spelling choices became fault lines for both schisms and community friction: umlauts in 1889–90, circumflexes in 1907, Jespersen's *c* in 1934. Each was a visible, low-stakes feature that people could rally around.
- None of these designs set out to resist sound change. Their phonologies were picked for European familiarity, not robustness. Only Volapük's /r/ avoidance (for children, the elderly and "some Asiatic nations") gestures at cross-linguistic pronounceability.

### Gaps
- No source was found that names the critic behind the "Belorussian with regularized stress" quote. Wikipedia's criticism article is the likely origin.
- Esperanto's consonant clusters (e.g. *scienco*, *kv-*, *ŝtr-*) are a common complaint, but no specific sourced criticism of them was found in the pages reached.

## 5. Adoption: peak size, current size, native speakers, institutions

### Takeaway
Only Esperanto has a sustained community, with a native-speaker cohort and institutions. Its size is genuinely uncertain: estimates run from tens of thousands to about two million, and the high estimates rest on weak methods. Volapük peaked in the late 1880s, with an unverified claim of about a million adherents, then collapsed within a few years. Ido, Occidental, Interlingua and Novial all stayed in the hundreds to low thousands at most.

### Cited Findings
- **Volapük**
  - By 1889: about 283 clubs, 25 periodicals and 316 textbooks in 25 languages. The movement claimed "nearly a million adherents" (**an enthusiast claim, unverified**).
  - Congresses: 1884 Friedrichshafen, 1887 Munich, 1889 Paris. The Paris congress was described as the first international convention conducted in an international language.
  - About 20 speakers remained in 2000.

  — [Wikipedia: Volapük](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volap%C3%BCk)
  - Another count: "over 200" clubs, 25 journals and more than 1,500 diplomas by the end of the 1880s — [Public Domain Review](https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/truth-beauty-and-volapuk/)
  - By 1894 only about fifty clubs were active; by 1910 the language was "practically dead" (snippet) — [Wikipedia: International Volapük Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Volap%C3%BCk_Academy)
- **Esperanto speaker estimates (contested)**
  - Sidney Culbert (University of Washington), about 2 million. Culbert interviewed people in dozens of countries and tested for "professional proficiency".
  - Amri Wandel, above 2 million.
  - Svend Vendelbo, 30,000–180,000.
  - "Two estimates" put the total at around 100,000.

  — [Wikipedia: Esperanto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esperanto)
- **Wandel 2015 (primary, read in full)** — [Wandel, *INDECS* 13(2):318–321, 2015](https://www.indecs.eu/2015/indecs2015-pp318-321.pdf)
  - **Method**: about 320,000 Facebook users listed Esperanto at the end of 2014. Wandel assumed only ½ of those claims are reliable, that only ⅓ of real speakers on Facebook list it, and that Facebook users are about 10% of the population. Restricting to the 40% of people online gives "approximately 2 million" among internet users: 320,000 × 10 × ½ × 3 × 0.4 = 1.92 million.
  - **Context**: past estimates ranged "from a modest 100 000 up to 16 million". No worldwide census has ever been done. Lernu.net had 150,000 registered users in 2013.
  - **Validation**: for French, German and Hebrew, Facebook counts are 10–20% of known speaker totals.
  - **Weakness the paper admits**: Klingon showed 250,000 Facebook "speakers", which Wandel attributes to Star Trek fandom. The ½ and ⅓ factors are assumptions, not measurements.
  - **Author's position**: Wandel's own references are his lectures at World Esperanto Congresses (Havana 2010, Hanoi 2012) and an article in Esperanto, so he writes from inside the movement. **Treat as an enthusiast-adjacent estimate, despite peer review.**
- **Esperanto native speakers (denaskuloj)**
  - About 350 attested families in 1996; about 1,000 Esperanto-speaking families with perhaps 2,000 children by 2004.
  - Notable native speakers include the 1957 Nobel laureate Daniel Bovet and Petr Ginz.

  — [Wikipedia: Native Esperanto speakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_Esperanto_speakers)
  - The main Esperanto article gives "estimated 2,000" but also cites "c. 1,000 (2022)". **Contested and undercounted either way** — [Wikipedia: Esperanto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esperanto)
- **Esperanto institutions and recognition**
  - Fundamento de Esperanto (1905); first World Congress at Boulogne-sur-Mer (1905); Akademio de Esperanto.
  - UNESCO's 1954 Montevideo Resolution supported Esperanto. UEA has consultative status with the UN and UNESCO.
  - Google Translate added Esperanto in 2012 (as its 64th language), and it is on Duolingo.
  - Esperanto Wikipedia had about 390,000 articles in August 2026 (37th largest).

  — [Wikipedia: Esperanto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esperanto)
- **Ido**
  - About 20% of Esperanto's "major promoters" but only 3–4% of ordinary Esperantists switched in 1907–08.
  - About 100–200 speakers in 2000; a current estimate of 1,000–5,000. **The current estimate is unsourced in the summary and likely generous.**
  - 25 people in Finland listed Ido as their native language in 2024. That is a registry artefact, probably not true native acquisition.
  - The Uniono por la Linguo Internaciona Ido has held annual conventions since 1921.

  — [Wikipedia: Ido](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ido_language)
- **Occidental/Interlingue**
  - De Wahl corresponded with about 30 users in 1924. The movement was run from Vienna from 1927 and spread to Germany, Austria, Sweden, Czechoslovakia and Switzerland.
  - The journal *Cosmoglotta* lapsed in the 1980s–90s. The internet brought a revival: a Yahoo group (1999), Wikipedia (2004), meetings since 2013, and *Cosmoglotta* no. 333 (2024).

  — [Wikipedia: Interlingue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interlingue)
- **Interlingua**
  - "A few hundred" active speakers.
  - From the mid-1950s to the late 1970s, about thirty scientific and medical journals printed abstracts in Interlingua, and Gode wrote a monthly column in *Science Newsletter*.
  - Union Mundial pro Interlingua runs biennial conferences and *Panorama in Interlingua*.

  — [Wikipedia: Interlingua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interlingua)
- **Novial**: "became dormant" after Jespersen's death in 1943 — [Wikipedia: Novial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novial)

### Inferences
- Even by the most generous estimate, the most successful auxiliary language reaches about 0.025% of the world's population (2 million out of 8 billion) after 139 years.
- Interlingua's one real niche was passive. Scientific abstracts used it as a read-only medium, which matches its design goal of comprehension without study.
- Native-speaker cohorts form only when a community is large and lasts long enough for marriages within it. Esperanto is the only case here.

### Gaps
- Current UEA membership figures, Duolingo learner counts and Ethnologue's current Esperanto figure were not retrieved.
- No peak figures were found for Occidental or Novial. Interlingua's "few hundred" has no traceable survey behind it.
- No independent (non-movement) peer-reviewed estimate of Esperanto L2 speakers was found beyond the methods above. The Culbert data is unpublished in the peer-reviewed sense, as far as the sources reached show.

## 6. Schisms and reforms, and why they happened

### Takeaway
The recurring failure was governance, not grammar. Volapük collapsed when its author claimed ownership and vetoed reforms the community demanded. Ido split from Esperanto through a committee process tainted by concealed authorship, and then lost followers through its own constant reforms. Occidental was absorbed by Interlingua in 1951. Novial died with its author. Esperanto survived by freezing its core (the Fundamento) and allowing growth only by addition.

### Cited Findings
- **Volapük, 1889–90**
  - Auguste Kerckhoffs and the Academy wanted reforms. Schleyer insisted on his proprietary rights.
  - The 1889 Paris congress passed statutes giving the Academy the job of improving the grammar and vocabulary. Schleyer rejected this limit on his rights, dissolved the Academy and set up a rival one. "By 1890 the movement was in disarray" (snippet).

  — [Wikipedia: International Volapük Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Volap%C3%BCk_Academy); [Wikipedia: Volapük](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volap%C3%BCk)
  - Splinter projects (Nal Bino, Balta, Bopal, Spelin, Dil, Orba) "immediately became obscure". Esperanto, which had no umlauts, took the new recruits — [Public Domain Review](https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/truth-beauty-and-volapuk/)
- **Ido, 1907**
  - The Delegation found no language "completely acceptable" and proposed adopting Esperanto "with several modifications" along Ido's lines.
  - De Beaufront and Couturat at first denied his authorship. Couturat accidentally forwarded Jespersen a letter admitting it, and de Beaufront took four months to confess publicly.
  - Zamenhof had proposed similar reforms himself in 1894 (they were rejected then). In 1907 he backed the Esperanto Committee's rejection, and most Esperantists followed.
  - Ido then suffered "constant modifications seeking to perfect it", which "caused many Ido speakers to abandon trying to learn it". It stabilised after 1922.
  - Couturat died in 1914, and Jespersen left in 1928 to launch Novial.

  — [Wikipedia: Ido](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ido_language)
- **Occidental**
  - Banned in Nazi Germany, Austria and Czechoslovakia, and the Gestapo destroyed materials. De Wahl was cut off in Estonia from 1939 to 1947.
  - In 1949 it was renamed Interlingue, with 91% support, partly to signal neutrality to the USSR and partly to ease a merger with IALA's project.
  - After 1951, most Occidentalists "made the short pilgrimage to the shrine of Interlingua", starting decades of decline.

  — [Wikipedia: Interlingue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interlingue)
- **Novial**: Jespersen's 1934 spelling reform alienated supporters, and the language went dormant after his death in 1943 — [Wikipedia: Novial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novial)
- **Esperanto**: Piron (1989) ascribes the persistence of features like the accusative to a "social contract on the untouchability of the basic core of Esperanto" (snippet) — [HandWiki: Esperanto](https://handwiki.org/wiki/Social:Esperanto)
  - The Akademio de Esperanto has rejected every gender reform proposal — [Wikipedia: Gender reform in Esperanto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_reform_in_Esperanto)

### Inferences
- The pattern across all four schisms: whoever can change the language becomes the point of failure.
  - A single owner (Schleyer, Jespersen) means the language dies or fractures with that person.
  - An open reform process (Ido before 1922) churns away its learners.
  - A frozen core plus additive growth (Esperanto's Fundamento) survives, at the price of locking in early mistakes such as the accusative and gendered roots.
- Reform-driven schisms split communities that were already small. Each successor drew mainly on the previous language's speakers rather than new ones: Volapük→Esperanto, Esperanto→Ido, Ido→Novial, Occidental→Interlingua.

### Gaps
- Garvía (2015) is the standard sociological account of these rivalries, but its full text could not be reached. The sociological causes it proposes, such as Esperanto's "hope" ideology and the *interna ideo*, are not covered here.

## 7. Drift in actual use

### Takeaway
Esperanto is the only one with documented drift. Fluent speakers often drop the accusative in speech. Native-speaking children reduce suffixes and weaken the accusative, but research suggests the non-native majority drives change, not native speakers. Gender-neutral innovations (*ri*, *-iĉ-*) are spreading informally against the Akademio's position.

### Cited Findings
- "Many fluent speakers drop the accusative marker in conversation." Its survival is ascribed to a social contract (Piron 1989) (snippet) — [HandWiki: Esperanto](https://handwiki.org/wiki/Social:Esperanto)
- **Bergen (2001) on native-speaking children**:
  - phonological reduction of grammatical suffixes in about 5% of utterances;
  - weakened or absent accusative;
  - limited use of compound tenses and aspect affixes;
  - productive word formation (e.g. *mal-* antonyms).

  — [Wikipedia: Native Esperanto speakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_Esperanto_speakers)
- Lindstedt (2006), "Native Esperanto as a test case for natural language": native speakers have no special prestige and do not set the standard of correctness. Research suggests the non-native community drives language change (snippet) — [ResearchGate: Lindstedt 2006](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/49912635_Native_Esperanto_as_a_test_case_for_natural_language)
- Fiedler: native speakers' status "cannot be equated with" that of native speakers of ethnic languages, given their small number and that Esperanto is one of several mother tongues (snippet) — [Fiedler, *Language Problems & Language Planning* 36(1)](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/lplp.36.1.04fie)
- The gender-neutral pronoun *ri* was "the most popular" proposal as of 2019 and is used informally, especially by younger speakers. The suffix *-iĉ-* appears in published works by Jorge Camacho and Luiz Portella. The Akademio rejects both — [Wikipedia: Gender reform in Esperanto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_reform_in_Esperanto)

### Inferences
- In practice, drift in a planned language moves towards removing redundant mandatory marking (the accusative). This mirrors the direction Ido and Novial took by design, which suggests their reforms tracked real speaker preferences.
- The only "sound change" documented here is reduction of unstressed suffixes in children's speech. That is the kind of erosion a sound-change-resistant design would most need to anticipate.

### Gaps
- No corpus study of drift in Ido, Interlingua or Interlingue was found.
- No quantitative measure was found of how often accusative *-n* is dropped in adult speech.

## 8. Criticisms by linguists and users

### Takeaway
The criticisms cluster on four points:
- Eurocentrism of vocabulary, phonology and semantics (all six European designs);
- mandatory redundant morphology (Esperanto's accusative and agreement, Volapük's verb forms);
- gender asymmetry (Esperanto's male-default roots plus *-ino*);
- for Basic English, that it is "not basic, and not English": restricting the vocabulary pushes complexity into idiom and circumlocution.

### Cited Findings
- **Esperanto: Eurocentrism.** The orthography, phonology and semantics are "thoroughly European", and the vocabulary and semantics "infuse the language with a European world view" — [101languages criticism page](https://www.101languages.net/esperanto/criticism.html); [HandWiki](https://handwiki.org/wiki/Social:Esperanto) (snippet)
- **Esperanto: gender.** Deriving women from male roots leaves women "linguistically excluded… or else named negatively" — [Wikipedia: Gender reform in Esperanto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_reform_in_Esperanto)
- **Esperanto: failure and culture.** Critics say the share of the world speaking it after more than a century "remain[s] minuscule" and that it "does not encapsulate a specific culture" — [101languages](https://www.101languages.net/esperanto/criticism.html)
  - Counterpoint: the Hungarian Academy of Sciences found Esperanto "fulfills all the requirements of a living language" — [101languages](https://www.101languages.net/esperanto/criticism.html)
- **Ido**: Piron argued that semantic precision such as distinct causative suffixes inhibits natural expression — [Wikipedia: Ido](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ido_language)
- **Interlingua**: it targets instant comprehension by Romance speakers, so it is explicitly not neutral. "A few hundred" speakers versus a potential readership of 900 million shows that ease of reading did not create speakers — [Wikipedia: Interlingua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interlingua)
- **Basic English**
  - Rudolf Flesch (1944): "It's not basic, and it's not English". He found the vocabulary arbitrary and the resulting text awkward and unnecessarily hard.
  - Claude Shannon (1948) contrasted Basic English with *Finnegans Wake* as an example of very high redundancy.
  - Orwell supported Basic English from 1942 to 1944, but it later inspired Newspeak.

  — [Wikipedia: Basic English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_English)
- **Basic English, Roosevelt's jibe to Churchill**: "I wonder what the course of history would have been if in May 1940 you had been able to offer the British people only blood, work, eye water and face water, which I understand is the best that Basic English can do with the five famous words" — [International Churchill Society, *Finest Hour* 135](https://winstonchurchill.org/publications/finest-hour/finest-hour-135/riddles-mysteries-enigmas-basic-english/)

### Inferences
- Roosevelt's "eye water and face water" for "tears and sweat" is a period illustration of the core Basic English problem. Replacing words with operator-plus-noun or compound paraphrases hurts register and expressiveness, and the learner must still learn which paraphrase is idiomatic.
- The phrasal-verb problem follows from the design. With 18 operators, meanings like "tolerate" or "rise" must be expressed through operator + particle combinations (*put up with*, *get up*). These are notoriously idiomatic and hard for learners. However, **no source reached states this criticism explicitly for Basic English**; this is an inference.

### Gaps
- No published linguist criticism specifically about Basic English's phrasal-verb load was located (e.g. from Okrent, or applied linguists such as Howatt). It is often asserted but went unsourced here.
- The named critics behind the Esperanto "Eurocentrism" and "Belorussian" claims were not identified. Piron and Okrent are likely sources but were not confirmed.

## 9. Basic English: 850-word design, operators, failure, legacy

### Takeaway
Basic English was a cut-down subset of English (850 words, 18 operators) designed to fit on a single sheet. Churchill's endorsement in 1943 backfired. The Ogden-specific system faded, but the idea of controlled-vocabulary English survived in VOA Special/Learning English, Simplified Technical English and Simple English Wikipedia. Some of those links are weaker than commonly claimed.

### Cited Findings
- **Design**: 850 words (600 things / 150 qualities / 100 operations) and 18 operators. The rule system is in section 3 — [Wikipedia: Basic English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_English)
- **Churchill's endorsement**
  - In his Harvard speech of 6 September 1943, Churchill described it as "about 650 nouns and 200 verbs or other parts of speech… no more… than can be written on one side of a single sheet of paper". He persuaded the Cabinet to set up a ministerial committee to study it (snippet) — [International Churchill Society: "The Gift of a Common Tongue"](https://winstonchurchill.org/resources/speeches/1941-1945-war-leader/the-gift-of-a-common-tongue/)
  - He also proposed printing the Atlantic Charter in Basic English — [*Finest Hour* 135](https://winstonchurchill.org/publications/finest-hour/finest-hour-135/riddles-mysteries-enigmas-basic-english/)
  - Note: Churchill's "200 verbs" contradicts Ogden's claim of only 18 verbs. He was presumably counting the 100 "operations" words loosely.
- **Why the endorsement backfired** (snippet; the source is probably the Ogden biography page, which was not fetched): it
  - created demand for books that wartime shortages left unmet;
  - led American backers to withdraw, believing the British government would now fund everything;
  - started more than three years of negotiations over a Basic English Trust.

  — [C. K. Ogden biography (zbenglish.net)](https://zbenglish.net/sites/basic/ckobio.html)
- **Legacy claimed by Wikipedia**: VOA Learning English, Simplified Technical English, and Simple English Wikipedia, which "uses Ogden's 2,000-word list" — [Wikipedia: Basic English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_English)
- **VOA Special English**
  - First broadcast 19 October 1959. Core vocabulary of about 1,500 words "plus any terms needed to explain a story"; reporters avoid idioms; read one-third slower than normal VOA English; renamed Learning English in 2014.
  - VOA's own Wikipedia article does not substantiate a direct line from Basic English.

  — [Wikipedia: Learning English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Learning_English_(version_of_English))
  - Short sentences, delivered at about 90 words per minute — [Wikipedia: Specialized English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specialized_English)

### Inferences
- What survived is not Basic English's operator design but its looser principle: a controlled core vocabulary plus domain terms. VOA's "plus any terms needed to explain a story" is close to "small core plus imported technical words", the same split the comparison language makes by borrowing technical words from English.
- Basic English failed partly from politics: Churchill's endorsement made it look like an instrument of Anglo-American power, and the funding collapsed. This is inferred from the endorsement timeline. The Eurocentrism and neutrality criticisms of the European auxiliary languages apply even more strongly to a subset of one national language.

### Gaps
- No primary source was found for Ogden assigning his rights to the Crown (commonly given as 1946, with compensation), so that claim is left out.
- I. A. Richards' work in China and the Basic English Bible (1949/1965) are not covered by the sources reached.
- VOA Learning English's operating status in 2025–2026, after the March 2025 executive order cutting USAGM, was not verified. Do not assume it is still broadcasting.
- The claim that Simple English Wikipedia uses Ogden's lists rests only on the Basic English Wikipedia article. Simple English Wikipedia's own guidance was not checked.

### Inferences: what the whole set means for the comparison language

- **A priori roots.** None of the seven is a priori. Volapük's distorted roots are the only partial precedent, and it is remembered as unrecognisable. The record gives no evidence for or against learnability of a priori roots; the failures were governance failures.
- **Isolating grammar.** Novial (analytic TAM auxiliaries, optional case) and Interlingua (no agreement or conjugation) are the closest precedents. Esperanto's drift (accusative dropping) points the same way. Speakers seem to shed redundant mandatory marking.
- **English technical vocabulary.** Interlingua's scientific abstracts and VOA's "core plus story terms" are the precedents for splitting the vocabulary into a small core plus imported technical words.
- **Evidential and confidence particles.** No precedent was found in this set.
- **Sound-change resistance.** No precedent was found as a design goal. The only drift data is suffix reduction in Esperanto native speakers.
- **Governance lesson.** Single-author ownership or an open-ended reform process ended Volapük, Ido's momentum and Novial. A frozen core with additive growth (Esperanto) survived. This bears directly on how a new language's lexicon and rules are versioned.
