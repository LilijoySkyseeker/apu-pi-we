# Why constructed and planned languages succeed or fail, and what is known about their learnability

Scope note: this is a fact base that doesn't depend on any one conlang. Source quality is flagged throughout. Labels used: **[peer-reviewed]** = journal or academic monograph; **[advocacy]** = produced by or for the Esperanto movement; **[tertiary]** = Wikipedia or a general explainer, used only for dates and numbers that aren't contested. Several primary PDFs and paywalled papers returned 403; where only an abstract or snippet was available, that is noted.

## 1. Why did Volapük collapse, Esperanto persist, and the other auxlangs stay small? What decided it?

### Takeaway
Governance and community decided these outcomes, not grammar. Volapük had a large early following but broke up over who controlled the language. Esperanto survived because its author gave up ownership, froze a small core (the *Fundamento*), and a self-sustaining community of speakers formed early, with congresses, literature and an association. The forces that kept Esperanto small were outside it: French opposition in the League of Nations, persecution under Hitler and Stalin, and the rise of English after 1945. Interlinguists (Blanke) describe success as a social path from "project" to "language". Esperanto is the only planned language generally counted as having completed it.

### Cited Findings
**Volapük (Schleyer, 1879–80)**
- At its peak (1889), Volapük claimed nearly 1 million adherents, 283 clubs, 25 periodicals and "316 textbooks in 25 languages". Its 1889 Paris congress was held in Volapük itself; the 1884 and 1887 congresses were held in German. By 2000 there were said to be about 20 speakers. These are movement-reported numbers and are probably inflated. — [Wikipedia: Volapük](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volap%C3%BCk) [tertiary]
- The collapse came from a governance dispute. Auguste Kerckhoffs, director of the Volapük Academy, pushed to simplify the grammar (fewer cases and tenses). Schleyer "insisted strongly on retaining his proprietary rights" and refused to recognise the Academy's authority. Within a few years the movement broke apart, and most members moved to Esperanto, Idiom Neutral or other projects. — [Wikipedia: Volapük](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volap%C3%BCk); [Wikipedia: International Volapük Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Volap%C3%BCk_Academy) [tertiary]; Don Harlow, *The Esperanto Book* ch. 3 [advocacy]: [link](http://literaturo.org/HARLOW-Don/Esperanto/EBook/chap03.html)
- Design complaints such as umlauts, English roots distorted past recognition and heavy inflection are commonly cited as secondary factors. Arie de Jong's 1931 revision "rehabilitated the phoneme /r/" so that roots would be easier to recognise, which implicitly concedes the root-distortion problem. — [Wikipedia: Volapük](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volap%C3%BCk) [tertiary]

**Esperanto (Zamenhof, 1887)**
- Mark Fettes [peer-reviewed encyclopedia chapter, by an author with ties to the movement] says Zamenhof's "crucial insight" was that the language "must develop through collective use". He therefore published only "a minimalist grammatical sketch, a vocabulary of some 900 words, some samples of poetry and prose, and a persuasive introductory essay". For about 15 years the written norm grew through correspondence and periodicals. Spoken use "grew rapidly following the first international congress in 1905", and "by the outbreak of the First World War, the language and its speech community were firmly established." — [Fettes, "Esperanto and language awareness"](https://www.esperantic.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/Fettes_Esperanto-and-Language-Awareness.pdf) (Springer chapter: [link](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-94-011-4533-6_15))
- Institutional milestones: the *Fundamento de Esperanto* and the first World Congress (Boulogne-sur-Mer) in 1905. The League of Nations considered Esperanto, but French delegate Gabriel Hanotaux opposed it from the first resolution on 18 December 1920 onward. UNESCO's Montevideo Resolution of 1954 supported it. Esperanto was added to Google Translate in 2012. The Esperanto Wikipedia had about 390,000 articles in 2026. — [Wikipedia: Esperanto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esperanto) [tertiary]
- **The Ido schism (1907):** the Delegation's reformed Esperanto (Ido) "was felt as a betrayal by many Esperanto speakers", and most eventually returned to Esperanto. IALA's Interlingua (1951) gained a community "perhaps roughly the same size as that of Ido", which is far smaller than Esperanto's. — [Wikipedia: International auxiliary language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_auxiliary_language) [tertiary]. This is a natural experiment: the "improved" designs did not draw speakers away from the language that already had a community.
- **Persecution:** Ulrich Lins' *Dangerous Language: Esperanto under Hitler and Stalin* (Palgrave, English edition 2016) [peer-reviewed monograph] documents how Esperantists were harassed and persecuted, "especially in Germany and the Soviet Union". — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1057/978-1-137-54917-4). Fettes adds that after 1945 "two of the most vigorous national movements, in Germany and the Soviet Union, had been persecuted to the brink of extinction; the onset of the Cold War divided the European movements; and the rise of the United States to superpower status enhanced the status of English." — [Fettes](https://www.esperantic.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/Fettes_Esperanto-and-Language-Awareness.pdf)
- **Blanke's scale:** Detlev Blanke set out a 19-step scale of the "socialization process" of planned languages, running from manuscript, to published "project", to "language" (a stable speech community including family use). Community-building is the key transition, and Esperanto is the only planned language that reached "language" status. — summary of Blanke via search snippets of [Gobbo, "Language planning of a planned language: the case of Esperanto" (academia.edu)](https://www.academia.edu/34936044/Language_planning_of_a_planned_language_the_case_of_Esperanto) and Blanke, *International Planned Languages* ([Everand](https://www.everand.com/book/458003709/International-Planned-Languages-Essays-on-Interlinguistics-and-Esperantology)). I couldn't read the full text; the summary comes from snippets only.
- Fettes describes the community as turned inward: "The lack of a broad social base has in turn directed much of the energy of the Esperanto community to fostering its internal cohesion", and "a significant proportion of Esperanto speakers holds that a preoccupation with changing the world is actually a burden" (this is the *raŭmismo* or "finvenkismo" debate). He also cites Fettes 1996, "The Esperanto Community: A Quasi-Ethnic Linguistic Minority?", in LPLP. — [Fettes](https://www.esperantic.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/Fettes_Esperanto-and-Language-Awareness.pdf)
- **Current size (contested):** proficient-speaker estimates run from about 30,000–180,000 (Svend Vendelbo Nielsen) to "above 2 million" (Amri Wandel) or about 2 million (Sidney Culbert). There are about 1,000–2,000 native speakers. — [Wikipedia: Esperanto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esperanto); [Nielsen's per-country estimate](https://svendvnielsen.wordpress.com/2016/12/10/percountry-rates-of-esperanto-speakers/). Culbert's figure is widely repeated but rests on an unpublished method. The higher estimates should be read as an upper bound and come from advocacy sources.
- **Ongoing activity:** the World Congress draws about 2,000 participants from about 60 countries on average. Recent and planned venues are Montréal 2022, Torino 2023, Arusha 2024, Brno 2025, Graz 2026 and Melbourne 2027. — [UEA congress page](https://uea.org/kongresoj/partners) [advocacy, but factual]

**The EU policy angle (Grin report)**
- François Grin's 2005 report for France's Haut Conseil de l'évaluation de l'école compared three EU scenarios: English only, a trio of languages, and Esperanto. Under English hegemony, English-speaking countries would gain about €10 billion a year, and more likely €17–18 billion. The Esperanto scenario would save France about €5.4 billion a year and the EU about €25 billion a year. The Esperanto scenario assumed it is learned 3–10 times faster than national languages. Grin said its feasibility depends entirely on coordinated political will, and the report led to no policy change. — [Rapport Grin (fr.wikipedia)](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapport_Grin) [tertiary]; [full report PDF](https://www.observatoireplurilinguisme.eu/images/International/hceerapport_grin.pdf); [Espéranto-France summary](https://esperanto-france.org/le-rapport-grin) [advocacy]. Caveat: the €25bn figure depends on the speed-of-learning assumption, which comes from the weak studies covered in §3.

### Inferences
- Across Volapük, Ido and Interlingua, the language with better or more "scientific" design did not win. What decided the outcome was who controlled the norm (an owner, versus an open community with a frozen core) and whether a speech community had already formed. Reform attempts after a community exists split it up (Volapük 1889, Ido 1907).
- Esperanto's ceiling was set from outside: state opposition (France in the League), persecution (Germany, USSR), and the post-1945 network effect of English. None of these respond to grammar design.
- Grin's analysis shows the problem is a coordination failure. Even if the economics favour a neutral language, no single actor gains by switching first. That is a network-effects barrier, not a learnability one.

### Gaps
- I couldn't get full texts of Gobbo, Fiedler, Tonkin or Ammon setting out success factors (403s and paywalls). The Blanke-scale summary comes from search snippets.
- I found no quantitative network-effect model specific to auxlang adoption.
- No reliable 2025–26 figures on Duolingo Esperanto learners or UEA membership turned up.

## 2. Planned or revived languages that took hold: Hebrew, Indonesian, Norwegian (Nynorsk), Tok Pisin, Swahili

### Takeaway
Every case that took hold had a territory, a state or proto-state, a school system, and a concrete communicative or national need. In most cases it also had a pre-existing base: liturgical Hebrew, trade Malay, plantation pidgin, coastal Swahili. Choosing a language that belonged to no dominant group worked as a strategy (Indonesian). Planners still couldn't control the outcome where it competed with an established written norm (Nynorsk, Samnorsk).

### Cited Findings
**Modern Hebrew**
- **Schools came first.** Haviv school (Rishon LeZion, 1886) was "the first Hebrew school of modern times". By 1903 the Union of Hebrew Teachers had 60 members. Herzliya Gymnasium was founded in 1905. In the 1913 "War of the Languages", the Yishuv rejected German as the teaching language of the Technion. The 1922 Mandate made Hebrew an official language alongside English and Arabic. — [Wikipedia: Revival of the Hebrew language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revival_of_the_Hebrew_language) [tertiary]
- **Speed:** the first native speaker (Itamar Ben-Avi) was born in 1882. By 1916–18, 40% of the Jewish population spoke Hebrew (77% in Tel Aviv). By 1948, 80.9% of Palestine-born Jews used Hebrew exclusively. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revival_of_the_Hebrew_language) [tertiary]
- **Preconditions:** Hebrew was already a literary and liturgical language that men learned through religious education, and a market pidgin was used between communities in Jerusalem. The revival was decentralised; Ben-Yehuda did not organise it. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revival_of_the_Hebrew_language); [My Jewish Learning](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/eliezer-ben-yehuda/)
- **Outcome differs from design:** Zuckermann argues that "Israeli" is a hybrid with heavy Yiddish and European substrate, "not a revived Hebrew but a new language". His view is contested within Israeli linguistics. — [Wikipedia: Revivalistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revivalistics); Zuckermann, *Revivalistics* (OUP 2020) [peer-reviewed monograph]

**Bahasa Indonesia**
- It was proclaimed in the Youth Pledge (Sumpah Pemuda) of 28 October 1928 and made the national language by the 1945 constitution. At the time it was the mother tongue of only about 5% of the population. Choosing it over Javanese (the largest group) "avoided the factionalism" that a majority language would have caused. Its grammar lacks Javanese's speech levels and honorifics. — [Glossika/explainer and ScienceDirect topic summaries via search](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/bahasa-indonesia); [Foulcher, "Sumpah Pemuda" (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/248947048_Sumpah_Pemudathe_making_and_meaning_of_a_symbol_of_Indonesian_nationhood). The 5% figure is widely cited, but I only saw it in secondary sources.

**Norwegian (Nynorsk and Samnorsk)**
- Ivar Aasen built Landsmål (later Nynorsk) from rural dialects. The state tried to merge Bokmål and Nynorsk into "Samnorsk" through the reforms of 1917, 1938 and 1949. The attempt failed because of "widespread resistance from both sides", and the Samnorsk clause was formally removed in 2002. Today about 85% of pupils are taught in Bokmål and about 15% in Nynorsk. — [Wikipedia: Norwegian language conflict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_language_conflict); [Wikipedia: Nynorsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nynorsk) [tertiary]

**Tok Pisin**
- It arose from plantation labour and blackbirding (Queensland, Samoa, German New Guinea). It spread because Papua New Guinea has about 800 languages, through urbanisation and intermarriage, and through use by missions, government and the newspaper *Wantok*. It has about 4 million L2 speakers and about 130,000 L1 speakers (2004–16), with roughly 1 million using it as a primary language. It is one of three official languages and is often used in parliamentary debate. — [Wikipedia: Tok Pisin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tok_Pisin) [tertiary]; [APiCS survey chapter](https://apics-online.info/surveys/22) [peer-reviewed reference]
- Sankoff & Laberge (1973) documented native-speaking children grammaticalising *baimbai* into the preverbal future marker *bai*, with phonological reduction and loss of stress. — [Tung, "Grammaticalization in Tok Pisin" (BC)](https://ejournals.bc.edu/index.php/lingua/article/download/5419/5049/0)

**Swahili**
- Swahili was already an old coastal and trade lingua franca. In 1930 the British Interterritorial Language Committee standardised it, choosing Kiunguja (Zanzibar) as the base from about 17 dialects, fixing the orthography and coining terms. After independence (1961), Nyerere made Swahili the national language and compulsory in schools as part of *ujamaa* nation-building. — [Wikipedia: Taasisi ya Taaluma za Kiswahili](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taasisi_ya_Taaluma_za_Kiswahili); [Wikipedia: Standard Swahili](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Swahili_language); [OAPEN: *A Language for the World: The Standardization of Swahili*](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/handle/20.500.12657/86094/external_content.pdf) [peer-reviewed monograph, not read in full]

### Inferences
- Every success shares the same mechanism: a bounded population with a real communicative need (Indonesia, PNG, East Africa) or a strong national-ideological need (Hebrew), plus a state or proto-state running schools and administration in the language. Auxlangs have never had a territory, a state or compulsory schooling.
- Neutrality worked in Indonesia because it was neutral *within* a national boundary and served a nationalist project. Global auxlangs offer neutrality without a nation to build.
- Once children acquired these languages, they reshaped them toward their substrates and toward natural-language patterns: Yiddish in Hebrew, grammaticalisation in Tok Pisin. Designed or prescribed forms are only a starting point.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed comparative study (e.g., Fishman or Spolsky) that ranks success factors across these cases. The comparison above is my synthesis.
- I didn't get primary sources for the Indonesian 5% figure or current Indonesian L1 share.

## 3. Learnability evidence: propaedeutic studies, learning-time studies, artificial mini-language experiments

### Takeaway
The best evidence (the Essex studies, 2013–2019, quasi-experimental and peer-reviewed) supports a narrow claim: Esperanto is learned faster than French by English-speaking children with limited classroom input. It does **not** support a propaedeutic boost to later language learning. Earlier positive studies (Thorndike 1933, Halloran 1952, Williams 1965, Paderborn 1970s–80s, Bishop 1997) have weak controls, are mostly not peer-reviewed, and in every case used a European target language related to Esperanto. Artificial-language experiments show that learners impose regularity. Children regularise unpredictable variation more than adults do, and iterated transmission makes languages more structured and easier to learn without anyone designing them. Learners' biases match typological universals (harmony, trading word order against case marking).

### Cited Findings
**Learning-time studies**
- **Thorndike, Kennon & Eaton (1933)**, *Language Learning: Summary of a Report to the International Auxiliary Language Association*, Teachers College, Columbia. IALA commissioned a six-year study of Esperanto and Ido. It estimated that learning Esperanto takes about **one-fifth of the time** a college senior or graduate needs to reach the same level in French, German, Italian or Spanish. It also suggested Esperanto made later French learning easier. Fettes, citing Fantini & Reagan 1992, notes that "the experimental conditions were not adequate to allow strong conclusions to be drawn." — [Fettes](https://www.esperantic.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/Fettes_Esperanto-and-Language-Awareness.pdf)
- A widely quoted line from the 1933 summary: "with expenditures of from ten to a hundred hours, the achievement in the synthetic language will probably be from five to fifteen times that in a natural language, according to the difficulty of the latter." — quoted via search snippet of [interlanguages.net, "A Planned Auxiliary Language"](http://interlanguages.net/PALiala.html) (page fetch failed; quote not verified against the original).
- The Thorndike-related Eaton experiments in New York (1934) on French vocabulary had small samples and "control groups weren't completely comparable", with mixed results. — [Kunimune blog review](https://kunimune.blog/2022/02/21/on-the-propedeutic-value-of-esperanto/) (an informed blog that reviews the primary studies; not peer-reviewed)
- Maxwell 1988, "On the Acquisition of Esperanto", *Studies in Second Language Acquisition* 10:51–61 [peer-reviewed], noted that experimental and control groups in the early studies were not always comparable. — cited in [Roehr-Brackin & Tellier](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/24243/1/Roehr-Brackin_Tellier_LPLP_Repository.pdf) and [Fettes](https://www.esperantic.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/Fettes_Esperanto-and-Language-Awareness.pdf)

**Propaedeutic studies, oldest to newest**
- **Fisher, Bishop Auckland UK (1918–21):** girls studied Esperanto before French or German. It helped French and apparently hurt German, likely from small-sample noise. The sample size was not stated. — [Kunimune](https://kunimune.blog/2022/02/21/on-the-propedeutic-value-of-esperanto/)
- **Halloran (1952)**, *British Journal of Educational Psychology* 22:200–204 [peer-reviewed]. In Sheffield, 40 pupils did one year of Esperanto then three of French, against 46 who did four years of French. The benefit appeared mainly in lower-ability pupils, and the net effect was roughly neutral. It controlled for intelligence and teaching, making it the most credible of the old studies. — [Kunimune](https://kunimune.blog/2022/02/21/on-the-propedeutic-value-of-esperanto/); [Fettes](https://www.esperantic.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/Fettes_Esperanto-and-Language-Awareness.pdf)
- **Williams (1965)**, *Canadian Modern Language Review* 22(1). Over 18 years near Manchester, a headmaster reported that one year of Esperanto plus three of French beat four years of French. He compared non-concurrent cohorts and said himself that his conclusions were "impressionistic". — [Fettes](https://www.esperantic.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/Fettes_Esperanto-and-Language-Awareness.pdf); [Roehr-Brackin & Tellier](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/24243/1/Roehr-Brackin_Tellier_LPLP_Repository.pdf)
- **Vilkki, Finland (1963):** the Esperanto track included pen-pals, congresses and other extras, which confounds the result. It used no objective measures and gave no sample size. — [Kunimune](https://kunimune.blog/2022/02/21/on-the-propedeutic-value-of-esperanto/)
- **Paderborn (Helmar Frank, Institute of Pedagogic Cybernetics, 1970s–80s):** German primary pupils in Group B did 160 hours of Esperanto in years 3–4, then English from year 5. Group A did English from year 3. The groups were equal by year 7, and B was ahead by year 8. Results are usually quoted as English learned 20% faster after one year of Esperanto and 35% faster after two. The work was published in Hanns-Seidel-Stiftung and Frank's own cybernetic-pedagogy venues, not mainstream SLA journals. Sample selection, sizes, and whether Esperanto replaced or added to English hours are all unclear. — [Wikipedia: Paderborn method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paderborn_method) [tertiary]; [Kunimune](https://kunimune.blog/2022/02/21/on-the-propedeutic-value-of-esperanto/). Frank was a leading Esperantist, so this counts as advocacy-adjacent.
- **Fukuda, Japan (1980)**, Esperanto before English, and **Formaggio, Italy (1989/90)**: positive, but texts are inaccessible and the methods can't be checked. — [Kunimune](https://kunimune.blog/2022/02/21/on-the-propedeutic-value-of-esperanto/)
- **Bishop, Melbourne / EKPAROLI project (1994–97):** about 21 Esperanto pupils against about 300 controls, measured by teacher ratings, with the teachers blind to which pupils had learned Esperanto. Esperanto pupils were rated as more motivated and better in L2. The controls mixed different languages, and the measures were subjective. — [Roehr-Brackin & Tellier](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/24243/1/Roehr-Brackin_Tellier_LPLP_Repository.pdf); [Kunimune](https://kunimune.blog/2022/02/21/on-the-propedeutic-value-of-esperanto/)
- **Overall assessment of the old studies:** they "often had poorly defined aims (Fantini & Reagan, 1992) or were somewhat superficial (Corsetti & LaTorre, 1995)… lacked methodological rigour, with experimental and control groups not always comparable (Maxwell, 1988)." — [Roehr-Brackin & Tellier](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/24243/1/Roehr-Brackin_Tellier_LPLP_Repository.pdf). Fettes, who is sympathetic to Esperanto, agrees that "Controls have frequently been inadequate, and in propaedeutic studies the 'target language' (L3) in all cases has been a Western European language lexically related to Esperanto." — [Fettes](https://www.esperantic.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/Fettes_Esperanto-and-Language-Awareness.pdf)

**Springboard to Languages and the Essex studies (Tellier & Roehr-Brackin) [peer-reviewed; best available evidence]**
- **Springboard to Languages (S2L), 2006–2011:** this was a language-awareness programme with five unpublished evaluation reports by independent researchers (Barton & Bragg). It used questionnaires and interviews and had no matched comparison groups. The authors themselves conclude that "all we can conclude from a research perspective is that offering children something is better than offering them nothing." A Comenius partnership with schools in Germany and Hungary used a pre/post design with "no inferential statistical analyses". — [Roehr-Brackin & Tellier, "Esperanto as a tool in classroom foreign language learning in England" (LPLP, repository version)](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/24243/1/Roehr-Brackin_Tellier_LPLP_Repository.pdf)
- **Tellier & Roehr-Brackin 2013a:** 28 Year-4 children (ages 8–9) were randomly assigned to Esperanto (14) or French (14), with 22.5 hours over one year and the same teacher. Both groups gained in metalinguistic awareness, with a larger effect in the Esperanto group. Gains in the taught language were **45% for Esperanto against 14% for French**, a significant difference. The authors' reading is that "learning Esperanto was easier for the participating children than learning French." Performance was also more homogeneous in the Esperanto group. — [same](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/24243/1/Roehr-Brackin_Tellier_LPLP_Repository.pdf)
- **Tellier & Roehr-Brackin 2013b (EUROSLA Yearbook):** a cross-sectional study of 225 Year-7 pupils. There was **no significant difference** in metalinguistic awareness between the ex-Esperanto group (35) and the others (168). The ex-Esperanto group was better on 1 of 11 tasks and had "by far the lowest standard deviation". Kunimune notes that a single significant task out of eleven could be chance. — [same](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/24243/1/Roehr-Brackin_Tellier_LPLP_Repository.pdf); [Kunimune](https://kunimune.blog/2022/02/21/on-the-propedeutic-value-of-esperanto/)
- **Tellier & Roehr-Brackin 2017 and Roehr-Brackin & Tellier 2019 (SSLA 41(5):1111–1131):** a quasi-experiment across 5 schools with 178 children in Part 1 and 116 in Part 2. Part 1 was 20 hours of German, Italian, Esperanto, or Esperanto plus focus-on-form; Part 2 was 20 hours of French for everyone. Only the Esperanto-plus-form group gained more in metalinguistic awareness, and that advantage "disappeared in the smaller sample". In French there were "no statistically significant differences between groups" on any skill. Aptitude predicted French gains in every group, and Esperanto did not compensate for low aptitude. Descriptively, the Esperanto groups were more homogeneous, a "levelling effect". — [same](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/24243/1/Roehr-Brackin_Tellier_LPLP_Repository.pdf); [RiPL summary](https://ripl.uk/research/one-page-summaries/roehr-brackin-k-tellier-a-2019/)
- **The authors' bottom line:** "although Esperanto may be easier to learn than another European L2, it was not a superior starter language… there is currently no evidence supporting the argument that Esperanto is a better tool than other European L2s in the foreign language classroom in England." They call these "sobering results". There is no research at all on novice adult learners. — [same](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/24243/1/Roehr-Brackin_Tellier_LPLP_Repository.pdf)
- Kunimune's review of all the studies reaches the same conclusion independently: "the current science inconclusive… this potential benefit has been overstated." No study tests adults, non-European target languages, or the propaedeutic value of natural languages as a comparison. — [Kunimune](https://kunimune.blog/2022/02/21/on-the-propedeutic-value-of-esperanto/)

**Artificial mini-language experiments (psycholinguistics) [peer-reviewed]**
- **Hudson Kam & Newport 2005**, *Language Learning and Development* 1:151–195. In a miniature language, determiners appeared probabilistically. Adults *probability-matched*: hearing determiners 75% of the time, they produced them about 75% of the time. Children "were very likely to regularize this unpredictable variation." — [Hudson Kam 2019 review, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7500460/); [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Regularizing-Unpredictable-Variation:-The-Roles-of-Kam-Newport/4f322a8a9eee4216be74c512ba6d164fdf01cbc1)
- **Hudson Kam & Newport 2009**, *Cognitive Psychology* 59:30–66 ("Getting it right by getting it wrong"). The main determiner appeared in 60% of noun phrases. Children over-regularised and adults did not. When several determiners competed in the same contexts, however, adults "overproduced the most common form, with the amount of overproduction increasing as the number of alternatives increased." The proposed mechanism is memory and cognitive limits. — [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7500460/); [ScienceDirect](https://sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010028509000048)
- **Follow-ups:** Ferdinand, Kirby & Smith (2019, *Cognition*, "The cognitive roots of regularization in language") found more regularisation as the number of variants per object rises, extending the effect beyond determiners. Hudson Kam (2019) found that interfering with retrieval at test raises regularisation "only to a small degree". — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7500460/); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0010027718303135). A *Journal of Child Language* paper is titled "children over-simplify language input only in production", which suggests regularisation shows up in output more than in learned representations. — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-child-language/article/abs/when-regularization-gets-it-wrong-children-oversimplify-language-input-only-in-production/2AF931F64C7C45F7E8F5205BD2952897) (abstract not read). A 2022 LLD paper re-examines child versus adult regularisation: [T&F](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/15475441.2021.1954927) (403; findings not verified).
- **Kirby, Cornish & Smith 2008**, *PNAS* 105(31):10681–6. Four diffusion chains of 10 "generations" each learned 27 meanings from partial data. Transmission error fell significantly (mean decrease 0.748, p<0.002) and structure rose (p<0.02). With no anti-ambiguity pressure, languages became underspecified (one word covered many meanings). When ambiguity was filtered out, they became compositional, with substrings for colour, shape and motion. "Just as biological evolution can deliver the appearance of design without the existence of a designer, so too can cultural evolution." — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2504810/)
- **Raviv, Meyer & Lev-Ari 2019**, *Proc. R. Soc. B* 286:20191262. Larger groups playing a communication game invented more systematic languages than small groups. — [MPI](https://www.mpi.nl/publications/item3138714/larger-communities-create-more-systematic-languages)
- **Culbertson, Smolensky & Legendre 2012**, *Cognition* 122:306–329, "Learning biases predict a word order universal". Adult learners of artificial noun-phrase orders regularised harmonic orders and disfavoured the order that Greenberg's Universal 18 marks as typologically rare, which links learning bias to typological frequency. A Bayesian model fits the data (*Cognitive Science* 2012). — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Learning-biases-predict-a-word-order-universal-Culbertson-Smolensky/26431edcecf7d6e37bda6368a5235d5579164d76); [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.1551-6709.2012.01264.x) (abstract only). A follow-up found the harmony bias even in speakers of non-harmonic languages. — [ScienceDirect 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0010027720302110)
- **Fedzechkina, Jaeger & Newport 2012**, *PNAS* 109(44):17897–902. Adults learning a miniature language with free word order and optional case marking restructured it in their production, adding more case marking where it carried more information (animate objects). This trades case marking against word order the way natural languages do. — [academia.edu](https://www.academia.edu/6644965/Balancing_effort_and_information_transmission_during_language_acquisition_Evidence_from_word_order_and_case_marking) (search abstract)
- **Population-level link (contested):** Lupyan & Dale 2010 (*PLoS ONE*, more than 2,000 languages, WALS) found that larger languages have simpler morphology. Languages with more than 100,000 speakers were about 6 times more likely to have simple verb conjugation, and they proposed adult L2 learners as the cause (the "linguistic niche hypothesis"). — [PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article/file?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0008559&type=printable). **Contradicted in part** by Koplenig 2019 (*R. Soc. Open Sci.*): complexity tracks the number of speakers, "but seemingly not… the proportion of non-native speakers". A 2023 follow-up is titled "Still no evidence…". — [RSOS](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rsos/article/6/2/181274/94767/Language-structure-is-influenced-by-the-number-of); [arXiv 2305.00217](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.00217)
- A 2025 PNAS paper reports that constructed languages (including Esperanto, Klingon, Na'vi and High Valyrian) are processed by the same brain language network as natural languages. — [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2313473122) (title and snippet only)

### Inferences
- **What is established:** a regular, transparent, Romance-lexicon language is learned faster *at the early stages* by learners whose L1 is European (English, German). Effect sizes of roughly 3–5× in early hours are plausible but come mostly from weak studies. Only the Essex 2013a 45%-versus-14% result is properly controlled, and it is small (n=28).
- **What is not established:** transfer benefits (the propaedeutic effect), benefits for non-European L1 speakers, benefits for adults, and anything at advanced proficiency.
- **For a language imagined as a native language:** the adult-L2 "ease" literature is mostly beside the point. The relevant evidence is the child-acquisition and iterated-learning work. Children regularise unpredictable variation, transmission across generations builds compositional structure, and learners lean toward harmonic orders and efficient trade-offs between case and word order. A designed native language will be pushed toward those attractors whatever the designers intend. Design choices that already match these biases (consistent head direction, predictable morphology, information-driven marking) are the ones most likely to survive transmission.

### Gaps
- I couldn't get Frank's original Paderborn reports, Thorndike's 1933 primary text, or the full text of Culbertson 2012 or Fedzechkina 2012; their figures come from abstracts or secondary reviews.
- I found no artificial-language study that directly compares agglutinative and fusional morphology for learnability. That question remains open in this fact base.
- I found no controlled study of Esperanto learning by speakers of East Asian, African or other non-Indo-European L1s; Fukuda (Japan) is inaccessible.

## 4. How planned languages change when they gain native speakers or large communities

### Takeaway
Native Esperanto (about 1,000–2,000 speakers, all bilingual) shows reduction and transfer from the other parent language more than invention of new structure. Lindstedt argues this is because Esperanto had already been adapted to natural-language universals by a century of L2 use. Fully nativised cases (Tok Pisin, Modern Hebrew) show grammaticalisation and substrate influence, and a planned or prescribed norm and the native vernacular drift apart.

### Cited Findings
- **Bergen 2001** (*Language Problems and Language Planning*, "Nativization processes in L1 Esperanto") studied 8 children aged 6–14 whose other L1s included Hebrew, Slovak, French, Swiss German, Russian and Croatian. He found loss of the tense/aspect system, phonological reduction, emerging dominance of SVO order, loss or modification of the accusative, pronominal cliticisation, and stress shifts. The aspect loss appeared even in Russian- and Slovak-speaking children, whose other languages have rich aspect. — [Esperantic Studies Foundation state-of-the-art](https://www.esperantic.org/en/research/state-of-the-art/state-of-the-art-esperanto-linguistics-2/); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/11560467_Nativization_processes_in_L1_Esperanto); [Wikipedia: Native Esperanto speakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_Esperanto_speakers)
- **Lindstedt 2006**, "Native Esperanto as a test case for natural language" (SKY Journal of Linguistics, Festschrift). Native-speaker changes come from transfer from the child's other L1, the gap between spoken and written registers, or incomplete acquisition. "Esperanto has already been adjusted to the requirements of language universals or Universal Grammar in the process of being used by non-native speakers, which is why native speakers need not introduce immediate changes into it." — [Wikipedia: Native Esperanto speakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_Esperanto_speakers); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/49912635_Native_Esperanto_as_a_test_case_for_natural_language) (403; quotation via secondary sources)
- **Numbers:** about 350 attested families (Corsetti 1996), with documentation back to 1919. About 1,000 Esperanto-speaking families with perhaps 2,000 children were estimated in 2004. — [ESF](https://www.esperantic.org/en/research/state-of-the-art/state-of-the-art-esperanto-linguistics-2/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_Esperanto_speakers)
- **Tok Pisin:** native speakers grammaticalised *baimbai* into *bai* (reduction, loss of stress, movement next to the verb; Sankoff & Laberge 1973) and systematised the transitive suffix *-im*. — [Tung](https://ejournals.bc.edu/index.php/lingua/article/download/5419/5049/0); [Wikipedia: Tok Pisin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tok_Pisin)
- **Modern Hebrew:** Zuckermann argues that Yiddish and other substrates of the revivalists shaped the result ("Israeli"), against the revivalists' intended Semitic grammar. — [Wikipedia: Revival of the Hebrew language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revival_of_the_Hebrew_language); [Revivalistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revivalistics)
- **Charters 2015**, reported by the ESF review, asks whether Esperanto actually achieves its ease goal. It notes Zamenhof lacked modern typological knowledge (e.g., WALS). — [ESF](https://www.esperantic.org/en/research/state-of-the-art/state-of-the-art-esperanto-linguistics-2/)

### Inferences
- Even a minimal, regular design isn't stable under native transmission. Unused or redundant categories (Esperanto's accusative, the participle-based tense/aspect system) erode, and fixed word order replaces case. This matches the Fedzechkina trade-off and the Hudson Kam & Newport regularisation results.
- Native-Esperanto evidence is weak as a test because every child is bilingual and input is sparse (often one parent), so "change" is confounded with incomplete acquisition. The ESF review itself says "relatively very little linguistic… work has been done".

### Gaps
- I found no large-sample or post-2010 corpus study of native Esperanto. A 2025 UD-treebank paper exists ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.udw-1.3.pdf)) but wasn't read.
- I didn't verify Lindstedt's paper directly (403).

## 5. Linguists' general critiques of the auxlang project, and rebuttals

### Takeaway
The main critiques are these: (1) neutrality is impossible, because any lexicon and typology favours some L1s, and Esperanto is lexically and typologically European; (2) "easy" depends on the learner's L1; (3) a natural lingua franca (English) already fills the role, so the problem is network effects, not language design; (4) a planned language has no culture or native community; (5) it threatens minority languages. The best quantitative rebuttal (Parkvall 2010) finds Esperanto "somewhat European, but considerably less so than the European languages themselves". Movement scholars counter the "no culture" point with evidence of a real speech community and literature.

### Cited Findings
- **Eurocentrism:** Esperanto, Ido and Interlingua "have been criticized for being too European and not global enough". "Euroclone" versus "worldlang" is now standard auxlang jargon. — [Wikipedia: International auxiliary language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_auxiliary_language)
- Esperanto's lexicon is said to be about 80% Romance, with Germanic, Greek and Slavic elements. — [EBSCO Research Starters](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/social-sciences-and-humanities/esperanto) [tertiary; the figure is a common estimate and I didn't verify it against a lexical count]
- **"Easy" depends on L1:** "foreign-language structures are more easily acquired when they resemble the learner's native tongue", so a lingua franca candidate "ought to be typologically neutral". — [Parkvall 2010 framing via ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/233566489_How_European_is_Esperanto_A_typological_study). Fettes concedes that every propaedeutic target language has been "a Western European language lexically related to Esperanto". — [Fettes](https://www.esperantic.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/Fettes_Esperanto-and-Language-Awareness.pdf)
- **Parkvall 2010**, "How European is Esperanto? A typological study", *LPLP* 34(1):63–79 [peer-reviewed]. Using WALS features, it found Esperanto "somewhat European in character, but considerably less so than the European languages themselves". One summary gives 44.3% "neutrality" over 76 features. That metric comes from a secondary summary and I couldn't verify it. — [Benjamins](https://benjamins.com/catalog/lplp.34.1.04par); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/How-European-is-Esperanto:-A-typological-study-Parkvall/aed3c9c844f50397984c2fad0eafddcb6fd21d0f)
- **"English already does the job":** critics argue a constructed auxlang "is unnecessary because natural languages such as English are already in wide use as auxlangs." — [Wikipedia: IAL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_auxiliary_language). The rebuttal is the economic-justice argument: under English hegemony, transfers to the UK and Ireland run to at least €10bn and likely €17–18bn a year (Grin 2005). — [fr.wikipedia: Rapport Grin](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapport_Grin)
- **"No culture":** critics cited include Mead & Modley 1967 and Steiner, *After Babel* (1974). Fettes rebuts that Esperanto has been "a cultural project as well as a linguistic one" with an "indigenous, non-national tradition". He also argues that "most worthwhile knowledge about Esperanto has been generated in the language itself, rendering it inaccessible to outside observers (Edwards 1993)", which lets outsiders make "pronouncements… which have little basis in reality". — [Fettes](https://www.esperantic.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/Fettes_Esperanto-and-Language-Awareness.pdf) [movement-sympathetic author]
- **Threat to minority languages:** this worry was "already prevalent in the late 19th century", and Zamenhof answered it in an essay in 1900. — [Wikipedia: IAL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_auxiliary_language). Fettes reports anecdotes of Esperanto learners becoming *more* interested in their heritage languages. — [Fettes](https://www.esperantic.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/Fettes_Esperanto-and-Language-Awareness.pdf) (anecdotal)
- **Lack of consensus among proponents:** the auxlang movement repeatedly failed to agree on "objective criteria by which to judge" competing projects. — [Wikipedia: IAL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_auxiliary_language)

### Inferences
- The neutrality critique is real but a matter of degree. Parkvall's numbers show neutrality can be measured, and a design can deliberately move toward the cross-linguistic median of WALS features. "Neutral" then means "typologically average", not "equally easy for everyone".
- Critiques (1) and (2) concern design, and the design response is to choose cross-linguistically common features. Critiques (3)–(5) are social, and no design choice answers them. This matches §1–2: adoption is decided socially.
- For a native-language thought experiment, critique (2) mostly disappears, since children acquire whatever they are exposed to. Critique (4) is turned around: a native language would *generate* culture. The neutrality question becomes a symbolic and political issue, not a learnability one.

### Gaps
- I found no named prominent linguist's systematic critique in a peer-reviewed venue (e.g., by Pinker or McWhorter) with a verifiable citation, so none is reported here.
- Gobbo's and Fiedler's peer-reviewed treatments of the neutrality question weren't accessible (403 or paywall).
- I found no study that measures how easy Esperanto is for speakers of non-European L1s compared with a national language.
