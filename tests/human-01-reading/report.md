# Human test 01: the author reads aloud (2026-10-09)

The first human data in the project. The author, a native English speaker with a little Spanish, read ten sentences aloud (in `docs/language.md` spelling, after the teaching capitals were dropped). Nobody else was available to listen, so this covers producing the sounds, not hearing them.

**Before the test** (questions while reading the first draft):
- "lon" looked like "Ion" in a sans-serif mobile font: l, I and 1 collide. This led to all-lowercase writing and dropping the teaching capitals.
- The reference called stress a "pitch rise", which sounded like a question. Reworded: the stressed syllable is higher, stepping down after it.
- Questions don't need a rising tone; added to the reference.

**Results:**
- **Nothing tripped the mouth**, including the fast-read storm sentence (line 6, read three times).
- **Nothing sounded English**; parts sounded a little Spanish (expected: five pure vowels, simple syllables).
- **"Remarkably easy to pronounce and keep from blending together"**, the author's words. This is the first human support for the sound system, which until now rested on the 1955 confusion model.
- **Rhythm:** with several two-syllable words in a row, the stressed-high pattern made the voice "bounce a lot". The author thinks practice would fix it, and suspects English bias.
- **Intonation habits:** the author used English energy and length on exclamations and a final rise on questions, and noted they only did so **because of the punctuation marks**.

**Not covered:** perception by another listener; speed; children.

**Follow-up, same day:** after declination was explained (stress peaks step down across a phrase, as English already does with "Mary wanted to buy a new car"), the author reread line 6. In their words, "the staircase was all that was needed… it now sounds not bouncy." So the bounce came from giving every word an equally high peak, and the specified declination fixes it.
