# Human test 01: the user reads aloud (2026-10-09)

The first human data in the project. The user, a native English speaker with a little Spanish, read ten sentences aloud (in `docs/language.md` spelling, after the teaching capitals were dropped). Nobody else was available to listen, so this covers producing the sounds, not hearing them.

**Before the test** (questions while reading the first draft):
- "lon" looked like "Ion" in a sans-serif mobile font: l, I and 1 collide. This led to all-lowercase writing and dropping the teaching capitals.
- The reference called stress a "pitch rise", which sounded like a question. Reworded: the stressed syllable is higher, stepping down after it.
- Questions don't need a rising tone; added to the reference.

**Results:**
- **Nothing tripped the mouth**, including the fast-read storm sentence (line 6, read three times).
- **Nothing sounded English**; parts sounded a little Spanish (expected: five pure vowels, simple syllables).
- **"Remarkably easy to pronounce and keep from blending together"**, the user's words. This is the first human support for the sound system, which until now rested on the 1955 confusion model.
- **Rhythm:** with several two-syllable words in a row, the stressed-high pattern made the voice "bounce a lot". The user thinks practice would fix it, and suspects English bias.
- **Intonation habits:** the user used English energy and length on exclamations and a final rise on questions, and noted they only did so **because of the punctuation marks**.

**Not covered:** perception by another listener; speed; children.
